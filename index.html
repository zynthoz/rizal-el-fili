Orientation · Before You Begin Prelude to 1891

A concise classroom introduction to the bridge between Rizal’s two novels and the historical spark behind the title.

Before the Story Begins: The Road to 1891

To understand El Filibusterismo, students must first understand what happened at the end of Noli Me Tángere thirteen years earlier, why José Rizal dedicated this sequel to three executed priests, and why the Spanish word filibustero carried such terror in nineteenth-century Manila.

01

Three Crucial Anchor Points for Students

Prelude · Bridge from Noli Me Tángere
1. The 13-Year Gap

Where Noli Me Tángere Left Off

In Noli Me Tángere (1887), the young reformist Crisóstomo Ibarra attempted to build a modern school in San Diego. Framed in a fabricated uprising by the friars, Ibarra was pursued across Laguna de Bay under gunfire while his friend Elías gave his life so he could escape.

Believing Ibarra dead, María Clara entered the Santa Clara convent. Meanwhile, the orphaned boy Basilio buried his mad mother Sisa in the forest. El Filibusterismo opens thirteen years later in an older, bitterer colony.

What to Watch: How thirteen years of unresolved trauma transformed both the boy Basilio and the man who survived that night.
2. The Gomburza Dedication

The Martyrs of Bagumbayan (1872)

Rizal dedicated this novel to three Filipino secular priests: Mariano Gómez (aged 72), José Burgos (aged 35), and Jacinto Zamora (aged 36), executed by garrote on February 17, 1872, following the Cavite Arsenal Mutiny.

Writing from memory in Europe, Rizal misremembered their execution date as "February 28" and estimated their ages. Yet the moral weight remained central: the Spanish government had executed innocent priests to terrify Filipinos, awakening national consciousness in Rizal’s generation.

What to Watch: The secular clergy’s dignity is embodied in the novel by Father Florentino.
3. The Accusation

What Filibustero Meant

Originally meaning a seventeenth-century Caribbean pirate, the word filibustero became a weaponized political label in the nineteenth-century Philippines. Friars and Spanish officials applied it to any Filipino who questioned taxes, friar estates, or colonial policy.

In an 1887 letter, Rizal explained that in Manila the word meant “a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged.” Charles Derbyshire’s 1912 English translation titled the book The Reign of Greed, capturing its theme of systemic colonial extraction.

What to Watch: How peaceful students and law-abiding farmers are branded "subversives" the moment they ask for justice.
Classroom Teaching Note · How to Follow the Story

As you present the novel, keep your eye on three interwoven storylines: (1) Simoun, the shadowy jeweler pulling strings from above; (2) Cabesang Tales and his family, crushed from below by the land system; and (3) Basilio and Isagani, university students caught between peaceful reform and systemic violence.

Unit 1 · Chapters I – III December · Pasig River

The novel opens on a December morning aboard the river steamer Tabo, traveling slowly upstream from Manila toward Laguna de Bay.

The Steamer Tabo: A Society Divided

Rizal introduces nineteenth-century colonial society aboard a single riverboat. The physical division between the upper and lower decks illustrates the colony’s racial and social hierarchy, while conversations onboard set the novel’s major conflicts into motion.

02

Aboard the Steamer Tabo: Hierarchy, Students, and River Legends

Chapters I – III · Sequential Storytelling
Who Is Here (Chapter I (1))

Under comfortable canvas awnings: the Spanish friars (Padre Salví, Padre Sibyla, Padre Camorra, Padre Irene), bureaucrat Don Custodio, journalist Ben-Zayb, pretentious socialite Doña Victorina, and the wealthy jeweler Simoun.

How Simoun First Appears

He is introduced as an influential foreign jeweler wearing a tall hat and large blue-tinted goggles that conceal his eyes. Known as the Governor-General’s “Brown Cardinal,” his true identity and secret agenda remain hidden from readers at this stage.

Chapter I (1): The Upper Deck and Forced Labor

As the steamer stalls repeatedly in the shallow, twisting river, the upper-deck passengers argue about how to improve navigation. Don Custodio suggests compelling riverside villagers to raise ducks so the birds will eat snails and deepen the riverbed.

The jeweler Simoun cuts through their discussion with a chilling counter-proposal: cut a brand-new canal straight through Manila, close the old Pasig River, and pay no wages at all. When Don Custodio asks where laborers will come from, Simoun replies: use convicts, and if they are not enough, draft all villagers, old men, and boys to work three to five months with their own tools and food.

“Don’t fool yourself, Don Custodio... only in this way are great enterprises carried out with small means. Thus were constructed the Pyramids, Lake Moeris, and the Colosseum in Rome... The dead remain dead, and might only is considered right by posterity.” Simoun · Chapter I, On the Upper Deck
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Simoun is arguing that great empires are built on the backs of unpaid, suffering people, and that history only honors the powerful rulers who used force, not the workers who died.
Who Is Here (Chapter II (2))

In the hot, cramped hold near the boilers: native Filipino travelers, Chinese merchants, and university students Basilio and Isagani, alongside the elderly Filipino secular priest Father Florentino.

The Students’ Goal

Basilio and Isagani are leading a student petition to open an Academy of Castilian, offering to fund teachers and classrooms with student contributions.

Chapter II (2): Water, Fire, and the Students

Below deck, the young poet Isagani and medical student Basilio explain their campaign for Spanish-language instruction. Capitan Basilio warns them that the friars oppose native education, but the students remain optimistic that their legal petition will succeed.

When Simoun comes below deck and taunts them by claiming that Filipinos lack energy because they drink only water instead of beer, Isagani delivers a sharp, poetic warning:

“Water is very sweet and can be drunk, but it can put out the fire of wine and beer... Water, when heated, becomes steam; when agitated, it becomes the ocean, which can rise and destroy.” Isagani to Simoun · Chapter II, On the Lower Deck
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Isagani is warning that although the Filipino people seem gentle and compliant like quiet water, if government oppression heats them up, they will turn into steam or a destructive tidal wave of rebellion.

The chapter also introduces Father Florentino, a dignified Filipino secular priest who retired to his coastal estate after the 1872 Gomburza executions to preserve his peace and conscience, and who raised his nephew Isagani.

The Three Legends (Chapter III (3))

1. Malapad-na-Bato: A sacred rock once revered by animist natives, later turned into a hideout for river bandits.
2. Doña Jerónima’s Cave: A woman walled inside a riverside cave by an Archbishop lover who broke his vow.
3. The Miracle of San Nicolás: A Chinese merchant spared when a crocodile turned to stone at his prayer.

The Hidden Plot Trigger

As the steamer enters Laguna de Bay, Ben-Zayb asks where a fugitive named “Ibarra” was shot thirteen years ago. Simoun goes deathly pale and stares into the distance without speaking.

Chapter III (3): Pasig Legends and the Silence on the Lake

Rejoining the upper deck, passengers tell historical legends of the river. When Father Florentino recounts the story of Doña Jerónima walled into a cave, Simoun pointedly asks Father Salví whether it would not have been more merciful to place her in a nunnery like Santa Clara—a sharp barb that causes Salví to shudder, remembering María Clara.

As the steamer enters the wide waters of Laguna de Bay, journalist Ben-Zayb asks the captain where a certain fugitive named “Guevara, Navarra, or Ibarra” was killed thirteen years ago. The captain points toward San Diego, explaining how the fugitive dove under rifle fire and water stained red with blood.

While the passengers gossip about the dead man, Simoun stands completely motionless, pale, and silent at the railing. When Ben-Zayb asks if he is unwell, Simoun claims only to be seasick—providing the reader’s first quiet clue that the jeweler has a direct, agonizing connection to that past.

Explicit Novel Fact

Rizal’s narrator explicitly writes in paragraph three: “...the Ship of State, so the joke runs, also has a slow and heavy gait.”

Literary Interpretation

The contrast between the upper and lower deck represents the racial, financial, and political divides of Spanish colonial rule.

Classroom Summary: What Chapters 1–3 Establish

1. The Ship as a Mirror of the State: The steamer’s round, clumsy form, its loud pretensions to modern progress, and its constant grounding on mudbars represent the colonial government—spending all its energy merely staying afloat without moving the country forward.

2. The Contrasting Responses to Injustice:

  • The Friars & Bureaucrats: Cling to power through censorship, intimidation, and racial condescension.
  • The Students (Basilio & Isagani): Trust in peaceful, legal reform through education and Spanish literacy.
  • Simoun: Cynically encourages administrative cruelty so the people will become desperate enough to explode in revolt.

Transition to Chapter IV (4): Leaving the steamer behind, the story moves to the nearby town of Tiani to show how colonial land laws destroy an innocent, hardworking peasant family.

Unit 2 · Chapters IV – X December · Tiani & San Diego

A peasant family’s struggle against agrarian dispossession, leading from peaceful legal appeals to armed resistance.

The Tragedy of Cabesang Tales

In Chapters 4 through 10, the novel leaves the Manila elite to show how colonial rule operates in rural villages. Through the systematic destruction of Cabesang Tales’ family, Rizal demonstrates how arbitrary taxes, corrupt courts, and clerical land monopolies push honest, hard-working Filipinos out of the law and into the mountains.

03

Cabesang Tales’ Land Dispute and Christmas Eve in San Diego

Chapters IV – V · Sequential Storytelling
Scene 1 · Clearing the Land

Labor and Grief in the Forest

Telesforo (Cabesang Tales), son of the broom-maker Tandang Selo, clears unclaimed woodland at the boundary of Tiani with his family. The grueling labor costs the lives of his wife and eldest daughter, Lucia, who die of malaria during the first harvest.

The Injustice: Just as the fields yield crops, a neighboring friar estate claims the land and levies an initial yearly rent (canon) of 20 to 30 pesos.
Scene 2 · Escalating Extortion

From 50 to 200 Pesos in Court

As the farm prospers, the estate doubles the rent to 50 pesos, then raises it to 200 pesos. Tales refuses to pay without seeing legal title deeds. He spends his entire life savings fighting in colonial courts, but fearful judges side with the religious corporation.

The Consequence: Tales loses his savings; his gentle son Tano is conscripted into military service when Tales can no longer buy a substitute.
Scene 3 · Kidnapping & Ransom

Juli Enters Servitude on Christmas Eve

When the government disarms farmers, Tales patrols his boundary with a small ax. Bandits kidnap him, demanding a 500-peso ransom.

His daughter Juli pawns her jewelry—keeping only a diamond-and-emerald locket given by her sweetheart Basilio—and indentures herself as a household maid to pious Hermana Penchang on Christmas Eve to raise the money.

The Sacrifice: A young woman surrenders her personal freedom to save her father from kidnappers.
Scene 4 · Christmas Eve

Basilio’s Return and Sinong’s Beating

That same Christmas Eve (Chapter V (5)), Basilio arrives in San Diego by carriage. His coachman Sinong is twice beaten by Civil Guards—first for forgetting his tax card, and second because his carriage lamp blew out.

Arriving at Capitan Tiago’s home on foot, Basilio learns that Tano has been conscripted and Cabesang Tales is captive in the mountains.

The Link: Both urban professionals and rural peasants are subject to arbitrary police brutality.
Transition to Chapter VI (6) · Why the Story Follows Basilio

Heartbroken by the news of Tales and Juli, Basilio cannot sleep. As midnight church bells ring across San Diego, he slips out into the dark woods to visit his mother’s grave—leading directly to the novel's most dramatic revelation.

04

Basilio’s Thirteen-Year Rise and the Secret in the Forest

Chapters VI – VII · The Woods of San Diego (Christmas Midnight)
Scene 1 · The Past

An Orphan Seeking Survival

Thirteen years earlier, the child Basilio buried his mother Sisa in this forest and fled starving to Manila. Desperate and ill, he followed Capitan Tiago and was taken in as an unpaid servant in exchange for enrollment at San Juan de Letrán.

The Ordeal: Ignored and insulted by professors because of his humble clothing, he passed by memorizing lessons without understanding them.
Scene 2 · His Education

From Neglect to Distinction

Transferring to the Jesuit-run Ateneo Municipal, Basilio flourished under encouraging instruction and science laboratories. Choosing medicine over law, he earned top clinical marks through hard work and discipline.

His Outlook: Basilio came to believe that knowledge, scientific advancement, and patient labor were the true paths to human dignity.
Scene 3 · Present Situation

A Future He Hopes to Protect

Now two months from graduating as a licensed physician, Basilio has been chosen to deliver the commencement address. He plans to marry Juli and build a quiet, peaceful life curing the sick.

His Priority: He avoids all political involvement to safeguard his hard-won career and protect his family.
Scene 4 · The Revelation

Simoun Unmasked as Crisóstomo Ibarra

While visiting Sisa's grave at midnight (Chapter VII (7)), Basilio watches a man remove blue goggles and dig with a spade. He recognizes the stranger who helped him thirteen years ago: Simoun is Crisóstomo Ibarra!

The Clash: Simoun reveals his revolutionary conspiracy and demands Basilio’s help. Basilio refuses, choosing medical science over vengeance.
Simoun’s Ideology · Acceleration and National Language

Why Simoun Opposes Spanish Instruction

Simoun explains his strategy to Basilio: he uses his wealth and friendship with corrupt officials to accelerate colonial abuses so that intolerable suffering will provoke a nationwide revolt. He fiercely attacks the students’ campaign for an Academy of Castilian:

“Spanish will never be the general language of the country, the people will never talk it, because the conceptions of their brains and the feelings of their hearts cannot be expressed in that language... While a people preserves its language, it preserves the marks of its liberty, as a man preserves his independence while he holds to his own way of thinking.” Simoun to Basilio · Chapter VII, Simoun
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Simoun argues that begging for the Spanish language will make Filipinos mere cultural copycats of Spain. He insists that preserving native languages is essential to maintaining true political and national independence.
Basilio’s Position · Science and Peaceful Labor

The Honest Citizen’s Dilemma

Basilio declines to join the conspiracy. He points out that he is not a politician; his duty is to cure physical diseases and alleviate pain. Vengeance cannot bring his murdered brother Crispin or his mother Sisa back to life.

Simoun warns him that passive submission only invites tyranny: “There are no despots where there are no slaves!” Yet because Basilio kept his secret thirteen years ago, Simoun lets him walk away safely into the dawn, telling him:

Simoun’s Parting Warning: “The day you change your mind, find me at my house on the Escolta.” Rizal establishes a crucial dynamic: how long can an honest Filipino maintain neutrality under a corrupt colonial regime?
05

A Mute Christmas, Village Pilates, and Tales Takes the Revolver

Chapters VIII – X · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter VIII (8) · Merry Christmas

Tandang Selo Struck Mute

On Christmas morning, Juli checks under the Virgin's icon hoping for a miraculous 250 pesos. Finding nothing, she bravely leaves to begin her servitude under Hermana Penchang.

Left alone in his empty house, Tandang Selo attempts to greet neighbors wishing him a Merry Christmas. Overcome with grief, no voice comes out of his throat: the old man has been struck completely mute.

Narrative Link: News of Selo’s muteness spreads through Tiani, but none of the authorities accept blame.
Chapter IX (9) · Pilates

Washing Hands of Injustice

Like Pontius Pilate, local officials disclaim all responsibility: the Civil Guard lieutenant claims he only obeyed arms decrees; the friar estate-administrator Fray Clemente calls it God’s punishment; and Hermana Penchang blames Juli for not knowing her Latin catechism!

Ransomed from bandits, Cabesang Tales returns home to find his fields given to a new tenant, his daughter enslaved, his father mute, and a court order giving him three days to vacate his home.

Narrative Link: In his darkest hour of ruin, Tales receives an unexpected overnight visitor: Simoun.
Chapter X (10) · Wealth and Want

The Revolver and the Outlaw

Simoun stays at Tales’ house, dazzling villagers with chests of diamonds. Noticing María Clara’s locket (kept by Juli), Simoun offers 500 pesos—enough to ransom Juli and save the farm.

Tales goes out to consult his family. Seeing Fray Clemente and the new tenant laughing on his fields, Tales snaps: he takes Simoun’s revolver, leaves the locket with a note of apology, and slays Fray Clemente, the new tenant, and the tenant's wife before fleeing to the hills.

Narrative Link: Tales becomes an outlaw; Civil Guards arrest mute Tandang Selo; the dispute reaches the Governor-General’s vacation desk at Los Baños in Chapter XI (11).
Historical Context vs. Literary Fiction · The Calamba Hacienda Dispute

What Happens in the Novel (Fiction): Cabesang Tales clears wild forest in Tiani, fights in court, takes Simoun’s revolver, kills the usurpers, and flees to the mountains as a bandit leader.

What Happened Historically in Calamba (Fact): In 1887–1891, Dominican friars claimed ownership of Calamba lands. Rizal and town leaders submitted a formal report to the government showing that tenants paid arbitrary, continuously increasing rents while the estate paid zero taxes. Under Governor-General Valeriano Weyler, the government sent artillery troops, burned tenant houses, and exiled twenty-five citizens, including Rizal’s brother Paciano and brothers-in-law.

Rizal’s Teaching Purpose: Tales' story is not a literal documentary of Calamba, but a creative embodiment of the agrarian injustices Rizal personally witnessed. At the end of Chapter X (10), Rizal explicitly addresses the “peaceful citizens of Calamba,” dedicating Tales' struggle to their courage.

Unit 3 · Chapters XI – XV Dec 31 – January · Los Baños & Manila

How colonial authorities treat peaceful petitions, and why higher education in Manila alienated an entire generation of youth.

The Students and the Campaign for Reform

While Cabesang Tales wages guerrilla war in the mountains, university students in Manila attempt to improve their country through legal education. Across five chapters, Rizal contrasts the frivolous governance of Spanish officials with the genuine intellectual hunger of Filipino youth—showing how peaceful reform is systematically obstructed.

06

Colonial Policy Over a Card Game at Los Baños

Chapter XI · Sequential Storytelling
1. Governing by Caprice

The Card Table at Los Baños

On New Year’s Eve, the Governor-General handles state business while playing cards (tresillo) with friars Sibyla, Irene, and Camorra.

Simoun sits in and bets his diamonds against shocking stakes: blank warrants for five-day imprisonment and summary deportation! When local officials complain about rural unrest, the General casually suspends the Tiani schoolmaster without a hearing simply for asking for a roof for his schoolhouse.

Teaching Point: Colonial decisions affecting millions are made as casual afterthoughts over a card game.
2. The Academy Debate

Split Over the Students' Petition

The secretary brings up the students' request to establish an Academy of Castilian:

The Spanish High Official and Dominican professor Padre Fernández defend the petition as fair and harmless. But Padre Sibyla and Padre Camorra vehemently object, arguing that natives who learn Spanish become insolent critics of the Church.

Crucial Seed: Padre Sibyla mentions student leader Basilio, and the Governor-General orders his secretary to write Basilio’s name down on the secret watchlist.
3. Camorra’s "Favor"

Tandang Selo Released for Juli

Before adjourning, Padre Camorra steps in to support a petition from Juli begging for the release of her mute grandfather, Tandang Selo.

The General grants the pardon to project an image of royal mercy. However, this creates a dangerous personal debt: Padre Camorra now expects Juli to be personally grateful to him.

Foreshadowing: This "mercy" sets a dangerous trap: Padre Camorra will later demand personal gratitude from Juli when crisis strikes her family.
Transition to Chapter XII (12) · Moving from the Palace to the Campus

With the students' petition delayed at Los Baños, classes resume in January at the University of Santo Tomás in Manila, where students face the harsh realities of colonial instruction.

07

Placido Penitente and the Classroom in Physics

Chapters XII – XIII · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XII (12) · Placido Penitente

Why Students Wanted Spanish Instruction

To understand why students fought for an Academy of Castilian, students must understand their reality: Spanish was the language of courts, government decrees, and medical science. Because friars refused to teach it, ordinary Filipinos could not read laws, defend their land titles, or participate in civil life.

Walking to the University of Santo Tomás, Placido Penitente ("peaceful sufferer"), an intelligent student from Batangas, is deeply discouraged. Four years of university study have brought him no real knowledge. On the street, he encounters:

  • Juanito Pelaez: A wealthy Spanish mestizo and friar favorite who boasts of spending his vacation flirting with girls in Tiani alongside Padre Camorra.
  • Paulita Gómez & Doña Victorina: Victorina openly smiles on wealthy Juanito while scorning Isagani, showing how elite families favored wealth over idealism.

Refusing to sign a petition he hasn't read, Placido enters his Physics class late.

Chapter XIII (13) · The Class in Physics

Locked Cabinets and Padre Millón’s Insult

Inside the bare tiered lecture hall of 250 fee-paying students, modern laboratory equipment—tubes, disks, bells, and prisms—remains locked inside glass cabinets, preserved only to show visiting foreigners that Filipinos have access to modern science.

The Dominican professor, Padre Millón, teaches physics not through experiments, but through confusing medieval logic. When Juanito Pelaez steps on Placido’s foot during a recitation, Padre Millón turns his sarcasm on Placido:

  • He marks Placido with fifteen absences (announcing that every time he catches someone, it counts as five).
  • When Placido objects that he cannot be marked absent and simultaneously fail a recitation, Millón mocks him as a “philosophaster” (a bogus philosopher).

Stung in his dignity, Placido throws down his textbook before 234 classmates: “You have no right to insult me. Keep your class—I can stand no more!” and walks out forever.

What Chapters 12–13 Reveal · Education as Radicalization

Rizal demonstrates that colonial education was designed to crush self-respect. Peaceful, moderate students like Placido Penitente are not born revolutionaries; they are driven toward radical resistance when lawful institutions humiliate them. Deeply alienated by this humiliation, Placido will soon seek out Simoun and enter the secret bomb workshop on Calle Iris.

08

The Student Assembly and Señor Pasta’s Selfish Counsel

Chapters XIV – XV · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XIV (14) · In the House of the Students

The Factions Inside the Student Movement

At the boarding house of wealthy student leader Makaraig, the students discuss their next step. Their debate reflects the different attitudes of the youth:

  • Sandoval: A generous peninsular Spaniard who passionately believes that Spain cannot refuse a peaceful petition to teach its own language.
  • Pecson: A cynical realist who warns that the friars will view their legal campaign as treason (filibusterismo).
  • Isagani: The moral backbone of the movement, committed to open, honorable civic action.

Makaraig reveals that the government has appointed bureaucrat Don Custodio as sole arbiter. Padre Irene suggested using the dancer Pepay to influence Custodio, but Isagani rejects underhanded favors, volunteering instead to lobby prominent Filipino lawyer Señor Pasta.

Chapter XV (15) · Señor Pasta

The Advice of Gray Hairs

Señor Pasta is the most prestigious Filipino lawyer in Manila, but he depends on friar contracts for his wealth. When Isagani pleads for his help, Pasta refuses.

Pasta advises Isagani to drop public causes: focus on your career, marry a wealthy girl, charge your clients high fees, and leave society to the government. He proudly strokes his white hair, saying Isagani will thank him when he has gray hairs of his own.

“When I have gray hairs like those, sir, and turn my gaze back over my past and see that I have worked only for myself, without having done what I plainly could and should have done for the country... then, sir, every gray hair will be a thorn, and instead of rejoicing, they will shame me!” Isagani to Señor Pasta · Chapter XV, Señor Pasta
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Isagani tells Pasta that living only for personal wealth and comfort is a shameful betrayal of one's people. An old age spent in selfish ease is not an honor, but a disgrace.
Transition to Chapter XVI (16) · The Failure of Legal Avenues

With Señor Pasta refusing to help and Don Custodio hesitating, the students' legal campaign begins to stall. Meanwhile, Simoun steps into the vacuum, accelerating his secret preparations for armed rebellion.

Unit 4 · Chapters XVI – XXIV January · Manila & The Operetta

Simoun orchestrates an armed uprising behind the scenes, timed around his desperate private desire to rescue María Clara.

Simoun’s Conspiracy and the Cancelled Signal

While the students await Don Custodio’s decision on the Academy, Simoun moves swiftly to assemble weapons, recruits, and psychological leverage. Across nine chapters, Rizal reveals how Simoun’s revolutionary machinery is woven together—and why his first planned uprising collapses before a single shot is fired.

09

Smuggled Rifles, the Quiapo Sphinx, and the Secret Bomb Shop

Chapters XVI – XIX · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XVI (16) · The Arsenal

Quiroga’s Smuggled Rifles

Wealthy Chinese merchant Quiroga hosts a banquet to secure political favor. Simoun corners Quiroga over an unpaid 9,000-peso debt for diamond bracelets.

Simoun agrees to reduce the debt to 7,000 pesos if Quiroga uses his import license to clear crates of smuggled rifles through customs and store them in his bazaar warehouses.

Why It Matters: Simoun secures a covert weapons depot in the heart of Manila.
Chapter XVII (17) · Quiapo Fair

Social Tensions and Carvings

At the festival, Padre Camorra ogles pretty women—including Paulita Gómez, provoking Isagani’s jealousy.

The crowd inspects native woodcarvings satirizing the colony: "The Philippine Press" depicted as a blind hag ironing cloth, and "The Country of Abaka" showing guards tying up a native.

Why It Matters: Displays the racial resentment simmering beneath Manila’s public festivities.
Chapter XVIII (18) · The Sphinx

Imuthis Terrifies Padre Salví

American showman Mr. Leeds presents a talking Egyptian head named Imuthis. Imuthis retells his ancient murder: framed by jealous priests who coveted his beloved and trapped her inside a sacred temple.

Recognizing the story of Ibarra and María Clara, Padre Salví faints in terror, groaning: “Mercy! She still lives!”

Why It Matters: Simoun psychologically tortures the friar responsible for María Clara’s confinement.
Chapter XIX (19) · The Bomb Maker

Placido at Calle Iris

Fleeing his mother’s pleas to apologize to the friars, angry Placido meets Simoun. Simoun takes him to a hidden workshop on Calle Iris where the suspended Tiani schoolmaster is manufacturing bombs and gunpowder.

Simoun orders his allies to be ready within a week: he has learned that María Clara is gravely ill and must be rescued before she dies.

Why It Matters: Simoun’s revolution is dangerously rushed by a private romantic urgency.
10

Don Custodio’s Sham Ruling and the Night of the Operetta

Chapters XX – XXII · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XX (20) · The Arbiter

Don Custodio’s "Eureka!"

Don Custodio struggles for fifteen days to formulate a decision that pleases the friars while pretending to be liberal.

Looking through his archived files, he pulls out the project for the School of Arts and Trades (run by Augustinian friars) and declares “Eureka!”: he will approve the Castilian Academy, but place it entirely under the religious orders, demoting the students to mere fee-collectors!

The Betrayal: The students' initiative is hijacked and placed back under friar control.
Chapter XXI (21) · Manila Types

“The Signal Will Be a Gunshot!”

Outside the sold-out Teatro de Variedades, crowds gather for the French operetta Les Cloches de Corneville.

The ragged vagrant Camaroncocido notices armed men lurking in the alleys. He overhears Simoun’s operatives whispering conflicting lies: telling native soldiers that the Governor-General ordered the revolt, while telling religious fanatics that the friars ordered it! He hears the password: “The signal will be a gunshot!”

The Strategy: Simoun relies on deception to manipulate both the military and the mob.
Chapter XXII (22) · The Performance

The Students Walk Out

Inside the theater, Isagani suffers seeing Paulita Gómez flirting in a box with Juanito Pelaez, while disguised Padre Irene peeks at French dancers from backstage.

Makaraig arrives with Don Custodio’s ruling. Realizing their academy has been turned into a clerical tax-gathering scheme, the students bitterly walk out of the theater, planning a satirical Chinese dinner to mock the decision.

Crucial Question: While the students walk out, why did Simoun's gunshot signal never ring out at 10:30 PM?
11

Ten-Thirty at Night: María Clara’s Death and the Aborted Uprising

Chapters XXIII – XXIV · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XXIII (23) · A Corpse

Why the First Conspiracy Collapsed

At 10:30 PM, while Manila attends the theater, Basilio sits alone studying medicine while nursing the opium-addicted Capitan Tiago (having previously used his savings to ransom Juli).

Simoun suddenly enters with a loaded revolver. He announces that within an hour, his revolution will break out across the city with Cabesang Tales below. He demands that Basilio lead a strike force to storm the Santa Clara convent and rescue María Clara:

“I have organized this revolution only to open the convent doors and rescue her!”

Stunned, Basilio delivers devastating news: María Clara died at six o’clock that very afternoon, showing Simoun a letter brought by Padre Irene.

Overcome with hysterical grief, Simoun cries out, drops his head, and flees into the night like a madman. He never gives the gunshot signal, leaving his waiting forces confused and causing the entire first uprising to dissolve.

Chapter XXIV (24) · Dreams

Isagani and Paulita by the Sea

The next afternoon, word spreads that Simoun is mysteriously bedridden with illness.

On the seafront Malecón promenade, Isagani meets Paulita Gómez. Hurt by her flirtation with Juanito Pelaez, Isagani nevertheless forgives her when she explains that Doña Victorina favors Juanito.

Isagani shares his passionate, idealistic vision of the future: modern railways linking the islands, flourishing commerce, and freedom won through brotherhood with Spain. Pampered and materialistic, Paulita smiles dubiously and dismisses his hopes:

“Dreams, dreams! I’ve heard it said that you have many enemies. Aunt says that this country must always be enslaved.” Paulita Gómez to Isagani · Chapter XXIV, Dreams
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Paulita dismisses national liberation as an impossible fantasy. She chooses personal comfort and social standing over dangerous patriotic dreams.
Teaching Analysis · The Flaw in Simoun's First Conspiracy

Rizal makes a critical thematic point: Simoun's revolution was built on personal obsession rather than genuine national interest. Because his uprising was designed around the rescue of María Clara, her death completely paralyzed his will to act. When the leader broke down over personal loss, the entire political conspiracy fell apart.

Unit 5 · Chapters XXV – XXXII Late Jan – April · Manila & Tiani

How state terror crushes peaceful reform: the arrest of the students, the death of Capitan Tiago, Juli’s tragedy in Tiani, and Basilio’s transformation.

The Pasquinades and the Wave of Terror

Following the collapse of the Castilian Academy, a wave of panic and political persecution sweeps through Manila. Across eight chapters, Rizal traces the human cost of state repression: innocent students are jailed, Capitan Tiago dies, Juli leaps to her death in Tiani, and an honest medical student is pushed into total despair.

The Student Banquet, the Pasquinades, and Padre Fernández

Chapters XXV – XXVII · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XXV (25) · The Dinner

Smiles and Tears at the Noodle Shop

Fourteen students dine at the Pansitería Macanista de Buen Gusto. Bitter over Don Custodio’s decision, they dedicate dishes satirically:

  • Pansit lang-lang: Dedicated to Don Custodio.
  • Lumpiang Intsik: Dedicated to Padre Irene.
  • Tortang alimango: Dedicated to the friars for moving backward like crabs.

Outside the window, a spy of Padre Sibyla watches them, then climbs into Simoun’s carriage.

What Follows: By dawn, seditious posters appear on university gates, blamed on the dinner.
Chapter XXVI (26) · The Posters

The Pasquinades and Basilio’s Arrest

The university is locked down by troops after seditious posters (pasquinades) are found on the doors. Students panic: Juanito Pelaez cowers and denies his involvement; Tadeo celebrates a holiday.

Basilio did not attend the dinner—he spent the night studying and tending hospital wards. Walking to Makaraig’s house merely to borrow graduation fees, Basilio is arrested on the spot because his name was placed on the Los Baños watchlist in Chapter XI (11).

The Injustice: The most politically neutral student is arrested simply for being educated.
Chapter XXVII (27) · The Debate

“That You Do Your Duty!”

Dominican professor Padre Fernández calls Isagani for an honest discussion. When Fernández asks what Filipino students want from the friars, Isagani answers: “That you do your duty!”

Isagani compares the friars to corrupt prison food-contractors who deliberately starve convicts' minds so they never leave jail. Moving as the conversation is, when Isagani surrenders to police, the Jesuits disown him to protect themselves.

The Reality: Even fair-minded priests like Padre Fernández cannot alter institutional oppression.
13

Citywide Panic, Capitan Tiago’s Death, and Juli’s Suicide in Tiani

Chapters XXVIII – XXX · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XXVIII (28) · Tatakut (Terror)

Panic and Capitan Tiago’s Death

Hysterical rumors grip Manila: people whisper of student-bandit alliances and night massacres. Frightened sentries shoot innocent civilians on the streets.

Padre Irene visits the bedridden Capitan Tiago and terrifies him with exaggerated tales of executions and Basilio’s arrest. Trembling with horror, Tiago clutches Irene's arm in a convulsion and dies in agony on the floor.

The Result: Basilio loses his only guardian and protector in Manila.
Chapter XXIX (29) · The Funeral

Pomp and Disinheritance

Padre Irene arranges a lavish funeral with three priests and expensive incense, but dresses Tiago's corpse in a cheap Franciscan habit to save money.

Tiago’s manipulated will leaves his estate to the Church and religious orders. Basilio’s 25-peso legacy is revoked for alleged "ingratitude," though Irene hypocritically boasts that he will pay it out of his own pocket.

The Result: Basilio is left penniless and friendless in Bilibid Prison.
Chapter XXX (30) · Juli’s Sacrifice

The Leap from the Convent Window

In Tiani, news arrives that while wealthy students were bailed out, Basilio remains jailed alone facing deportation to the Carolines. Townspeople urge Juli to plead with Padre Camorra.

Terrified of Camorra's past advances, Juli resists until Sister Bali pushes her inside the convent doors. That evening, Juli leaps from the convent window to her death on the stones below rather than surrender her honor. At twilight, mute Tandang Selo beats on the locked door, then vanishes into the mountains carrying his hunting spear.

The Climax of Grief: Tales' family is completely shattered; Juli chooses death over dishonor.
14

The Spanish High Official’s Resignation and Paulita’s Betrayal

Chapters XXXI – XXXII · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XXXI (31) · The High Official

A Spaniard Speaks for Justice

In Manila, newspapers hush up Juli's death, reporting only that Padre Camorra was transferred. In the mountains, Cabesang Tales leads guerrilla raids under the name Matanglawin ("Hawk-Eye").

At Malacañang Palace, an honorable Spanish High Official pleads with the Governor-General to release Basilio, pointing out that Basilio is completely innocent and only needs to finish his medical degree. The General arrogantly refuses, claiming that punishing an innocent youth inspires greater terror.

Outraged, the High Official defends the moral dignity of Spain and affirms the Filipinos' right to rebel against tyranny. He resigns his post and tells his Filipino coachman as he departs:

“Some day when you declare yourselves independent, remember that there were not lacking in Spain hearts that beat for you and struggled for your rights!” The High Official · Chapter XXXI, The High Official
Chapter XXXII (32) · Effect of the Pasquinades

The Total Ruin of the Students

By April, the academic year ends in disaster: exams are failed or suspended, Tadeo burns his books, and Makaraig sails for Europe.

Basilio spends over three months in Bilibid Prison. There, the coachman Sinong visits him and breaks the tragic news: Juli is dead and Tandang Selo has vanished into the mountains with his spear.

Meanwhile, Simoun recovers and forms a business partnership with Don Timoteo Pelaez. Heartbroken Isagani learns the final bitter truth: Paulita Gómez has dumped him to marry wealthy Juanito Pelaez. Their high-society wedding is scheduled for late April before the Governor-General returns to Spain.

The Stage Is Set: Basilio has lost his mother, brother, career, and sweetheart. He has nothing left to live for except revenge.
Unit 6 · Chapters XXXIII – XXXV Late April · Calle Anloague

The climax of Simoun’s conspiracy: the pomegranate lamp, the mined wedding banquet, and Isagani’s fateful intervention.

The Wedding Feast and the Pomegranate Lamp

Three months after the student arrests, Simoun launches his second conspiracy. Unlike his first attempt, which sought to rescue María Clara, this second plot is driven by cold, total destruction—using a society wedding as the trap to annihilate the entire leadership of the Spanish colonial regime.

15

La Última Razón, the Wedding Feast, and the Pomegranate Lamp

Chapters XXXIII – XXXV · Sequential Storytelling
Lámpara de Simoun · Oxidized-gold pomegranate holding over 1 liter of nitroglycerin

Chapter XXXIII (33): A Transformed Basilio Seeks Revenge

Freed from Bilibid Prison in late April through Simoun’s influence, a hollow-eyed, transformed Basilio enters Simoun’s laboratory. Having lost his mother, brother, career, and sweetheart Juli, Basilio admits his past peaceful ideals were an illusion:

“I have been a bad son and a bad brother... Now give me arms and let the revolution break out!”

Simoun reveals his weapon: an oxidized-gold lamp shaped like a split pomegranate set with carnelian seeds. Inside, an inner tank of steel two centimeters thick holds over a liter of nitroglycerin. Simoun calls it:

“Concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, injustice, outrage—the last resort of the weak, force against force, violence against violence.”

The Plan: The lamp will illuminate the wedding feast of Juanito Pelaez and Paulita Gómez. After twenty minutes, the flame will flicker dim. When someone raises the wick, a fulminate of mercury cap will detonate the nitroglycerin, triggering sacks of gunpowder hidden in the roof and floor to wipe out all colonial leaders at once.

Chapter XXXIV (34): The Mined Wedding on Calle Anloague

Why the wedding? Because it gathers the entire colonial hierarchy—the Governor-General, the Archbishop, the provincial governors, and the wealthy merchants—into one confined location.

The feast takes place at Capitan Tiago’s former mansion on Calle Anloague, bought and gaudily remodeled by groom’s father Don Timoteo Pelaez. On the open-air azotea, an exclusive octagonal dining kiosk is set for the seven supreme rulers of church and state—secretly wired to gunpowder sacks below.

Outside in the dark street, Basilio watches guests arrive. He spots heartbroken Isagani wandering nearby, staring up at the brightly lit windows hoping for one last glimpse of Paulita. Simoun arrives in a carriage, delivers the pomegranate lamp upstairs, and quickly departs, pale and silent.

Chapter XXXV (35) (Part 1): The Handwriting on the Wall

Upstairs, the Governor-General admires the dazzling pomegranate lamp glowing at the center of the banquet table.

During the meal, a square slip of paper written in red ink is passed around:

Mene, Tekel, Phares — Juan Crisostomo Ibarra

The words quote the biblical Book of Daniel ("numbered, weighed, and divided"), signifying impending destruction. When the paper reaches Padre Salví, the friar recognizes Ibarra's authentic handwriting and turns ashen with horror.

At that moment, the pomegranate lamp begins to dim. The Governor-General says: “The lamp is going out. Will you turn up the wick, Padre Irene?”

Chapter XXXV (35) (Part 2): Why Isagani Threw the Lamp

Outside on the street, Basilio meets Isagani. Desperate to save his friend’s life before the house explodes, Basilio blurts out the terrifying secret:

“Isagani, listen to me! That house is mined, it’s going to blow up at any moment... Simoun’s lamp—all will perish in its ruins!”

Basilio forgets one decisive human factor: Isagani still loves Paulita Gómez!

Realizing that Paulita will be blown to pieces in seconds, Isagani wrenches free, races up the grand staircase, and bursts into the dining kiosk just as Padre Irene reaches for the lamp wick. Isagani snatches the burning lamp, hurls it over the railing into the Pasig River, and dives into the water himself as servants shout “Thief!”. The explosion is defused in the dark river depths.

Teaching Analysis · The Irony of the Climax

Rizal stages a masterpiece of moral and psychological irony: the corrupt colonial regime is saved not by its own army or police, but by the love of the very student who opposed it most fiercely. Isagani’s romantic devotion to Paulita saves the lives of the friars and officials who oppressed his countrymen.

Unit 7 · Chapters XXXVI – XXXVIII May · Manila & The Mountain Pass

Press censorship, the hunt for the fugitive Simoun, Isagani’s secret regret, and the tragic mountain ambush.

Silenced Presses and Fratricide in the Hills

The novel does not end with the thrown lamp. In Chapters 36 through 38, Rizal traces the immediate consequences of the failed conspiracy: colonial censors gag the press, Simoun is hunted by the very outlaws he deceived, Isagani confronts what his impulse prevented, and Cabesang Tales’ family meets a devastating, fratricidal end.

Government Censorship, Betrayed Outlaws, and Isagani’s Regret

Chapters XXXVI – XXXVII · Sequential Storytelling
Chapter XXXVI (36) · Ben-Zayb’s Afflictions

Gag Orders and Outlaws Swearing Vengeance

Following the wedding disturbance, journalist Ben-Zayb writes a sensational article glorifying the Governor-General’s calm bravery. But the Governor-General strictly censors the press, forbidding any mention that Manila’s rulers were panicked by a slip of paper.

Investigating a robbery at a riverside villa that left Padre Camorra bruised, police capture a bandit. Under interrogation, the outlaw confesses that a white-haired Spaniard had promised them money and rifles to storm the city twice, and stood them up twice!

The furious outlaws vow to hunt down and kill the white-haired Spaniard who betrayed them. Police search Simoun’s Escolta house and discover he has vanished, leaving behind hundreds of gunpowder cartridges.

Chapter XXXVII (37) · The Mystery

Isagani’s Secret Sorrow in Santa Cruz

At the home of the silversmith Orenda family in Santa Cruz, relatives gossip about the wedding. A worker named Chichoy reveals terrifying news from Calle Anloague: sacks of gunpowder were found concealed under the dining kiosk floor and inside the chairs!

Guests shudder, wondering who the "wicked thief" was who stole the lamp. Listening in the corner, Isagani realizes for the first time the full magnitude of what he stopped: by saving Paulita, he saved the very tyrants who enslaved his country.

“If that thief had known what it was all about and had been able to reflect, surely he wouldn’t have done as he did... For nothing in the world would I want to be in his place!” Isagani · Chapter XXXVII, The Mystery
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Isagani is speaking about himself. He bitterly regrets his impulsive act, realizing he preserved the colonial system out of love for a woman who married someone else. He leaves Manila forever to return to Father Florentino’s coast.
17

Fatality: Carolino and the Mute Grandfather on the Cliff

Chapter XXXVIII · Mountain Pass in Luzon

Across southern Luzon, Matanglawin’s guerrillas raid estates while Civil Guards retaliate against helpless villagers. In a scorched mountain pass, a squad of native soldiers escorts seven bound, half-naked peasant prisoners.

Among the native soldiers is Carolino (who recently returned from service in the Caroline Islands). Disgusted by the brutality of soldier Mautang, Carolino pleads for the prisoners, saying: “They are men like ourselves.”

Suddenly, guerrillas open fire from the high crags above! Mautang is shot dead and the Spanish corporal is wounded. In blind rage, the corporal orders all bound kneeling prisoners executed on the spot.

Spotting a guerrilla signaling on a high ledge, the corporal forces Carolino to shoot him. Carolino fires, and the man tumbles behind the rocks. An instant later, an elderly figure waving a hunting spear appears on the rock. The soldiers fire a volley, felling the old man.

Carolino is Tano, the conscripted son of Cabesang Tales—and the dying old man felled on the rock is his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo, who stares into Tano’s eyes with dying agony and points a rigid finger behind the crags before breathing his last.

Remembering how Tandang Selo left Tiani carrying his hunting spear after Juli’s suicide (Chapter XXX (30)), the full tragedy of Tales' family is revealed: colonial oppression forced Tano into a Spanish uniform to unknowingly shoot down his own flesh and blood.

Teaching Analysis · The Climax of Colonial Fratricide

Chapter XXXVIII (38) is one of the most heartbreaking chapters in Philippine literature. Rizal demonstrates that colonial tyranny's greatest crime is fratricide: turning brother against brother and son against grandfather, forcing native conscripts to defend the very regime that destroyed their homes.

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX May · Solitary House on the Pacific

Simoun’s final deathbed confession to Father Florentino, the moral evaluation of the revolution, and the burial of the treasure in the sea.

Conclusion: Moral Reckoning by the Sea

In the novel’s final chapter, the narrative moves away from Manila to Father Florentino’s solitary stone house overlooking the Pacific Ocean. Here, the dying conspirator makes his final confession, and Rizal delivers the novel’s central ethical thesis on liberty, sacrifice, and true national redemption.

18

Simoun’s Confession and the Pacific Abyss

Chapter XXXIX · The Final Reckoning

At Father Florentino’s coastal home, timid Don Tiburcio de Espadaña flees into hiding, misreading a military telegram (“Spaniard hidden house Padre Florentino cojera [cogerá, 'will capture'] tonight eight alive or dead”) as his wife Doña Victorina hunting down his lameness (cojera)!

The telegram actually refers to Simoun, who arrived two days earlier severely wounded, carrying his heavy steel chest of diamonds. Entering his room to warn him that Civil Guards arrive at 8:00 PM, Father Florentino discovers that Simoun has already swallowed slow poison to avoid being taken alive.

Simoun’s Dying Confession

In the fading twilight, Simoun reveals his full identity: how thirteen years earlier he returned to the country as Crisóstomo Ibarra full of hope and love for María Clara, was framed and hunted, escaped death through Elías, amassed a fortune in Cuba, and used his gold to buy influence and deliberately provoke colonial cruelty.

Bitter in his death agony, Simoun asks why God denied him victory while allowing corrupt friars and rulers to triumph. Father Florentino replies with quiet, sorrowful honesty: Simoun’s wounds came “from the very ones you have instigated to crime” (the outlaws he deceived), because a holy cause cannot be won by evil means:

“Because you chose means that He could not sanction... Hate never produces anything but monsters and crime criminals! Love alone realizes wonderful works, virtue alone can save! No, if our country has ever to be free, it will not be through vice and crime... Redemption presupposes virtue, virtue sacrifice, and sacrifice love!” Father Florentino to Simoun · Chapter XXXIX, Conclusion
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Freedom cannot be achieved by copying the vices, bribery, and cruelty of the oppressor. True national liberation requires moral integrity, civic virtue, and loving sacrifice for the people.

Why Independence Is Not Enough

When Simoun asks what Filipinos must do if peaceful reform fails, Father Florentino delivers the novel's most famous warning against exchanging one form of tyranny for another:

“While the Filipino people has not sufficient energy to proclaim, with head erect and bosom bared, its rights... why grant them liberty? With Spain or without Spain they would always be the same, and perhaps worse! Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?” Father Florentino · Chapter XXXIX, Conclusion
Modern Plain-English Meaning: Simply changing rulers from Spanish to Filipino will not bring real freedom if citizens remain cowardly, passive, or greedy. A society of compliant slaves will only produce new dictators once power changes hands.

The Call to the Youth and the Ocean Abyss

Simoun presses Florentino’s hand in silent acknowledgment, sighs, and dies peacefully in the dark. Kneeling beside the corpse, Father Florentino utters his moving invocation to the future generation:

“Where are the youth who will consecrate their golden hours, their illusions, and their enthusiasm to the welfare of their native land? Pure and spotless must the victim be that the sacrifice may be acceptable! Where are you, youth, who will embody in yourselves the vigor of life that has left our veins? We await you, O youth! Come, for we await you!” Father Florentino’s Invocation · Chapter XXXIX

Taking the heavy steel chest containing Simoun’s fabulous wealth, Father Florentino walks down to the windswept cliff and hurls the treasure into the churning Pacific abyss:

“May Nature guard you in her deep abysses among the pearls and corals of her eternal seas... When for some holy and sublime purpose man may need you, God will in His wisdom draw you from the bosom of the waves. Meanwhile, there you will not work woe, you will not distort justice, you will not foment avarice!”

Literary Comparison & Scholarly Documentation
Dimension Noli Me Tángere (Berlin, 1887) El Filibusterismo (Ghent, 1891)
Title & Dedication Latin phrase (“Touch Me Not”), diagnosing the social cancer; dedicated To My Fatherland. Colonial political accusation (The Subversive / The Reign of Greed); dedicated to the martyred secular priests Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora.
Protagonist’s Role Crisóstomo Ibarra: An idealistic, European-educated reformer attempting to build a modern school within Spanish law. Simoun: A bitter, disillusioned conspirator using wealth and official corruption to hasten colonial collapse and ignite revolution.
Underlying Message Exposes friar abuses and appeals to Spain for assimilation, civil rights, and enlightened governance. Demonstrates that peaceful assimilation has been blocked, while warning that a revolution built on vengeance and hatred will produce new tyrants.
Tone & Structure Romantic, satirical, and expansive across 64 chapters; broad canvas of provincial life. Dark, dramatic, and tightly wound across 39 chapters; focused on political debates, student struggles, and state repression.
1. Exile & Censorship

Dapitan Exile (July 1892)

Rizal shipped crates of the Ghent edition to José María Basa in Hong Kong. Smuggled copies reached Manila, where Spanish customs confiscated them. Colonial authorities cited Rizal's subversive writings when Governor-General Eulogio Despujol deported him to Dapitan in July 1892.

2. Revolutionary Readership

The Katipunan Readers

Although Father Florentino warned against corrupt conspiracy, revolutionary leaders such as Andrés Bonifacio (who kept copies of both novels) interpreted Tales’ dispossession and the crushed student academy as evidence that peaceful reform under Spain had reached a dead end.

3. Educational Mandate

Republic Act No. 1425 (1956)

Sponsored by Senators Jose P. Laurel and Claro M. Recto, Republic Act No. 1425 mandates courses on Rizal’s life, works, and writings across all educational institutions, requiring unexpurgated editions as basic texts in collegiate courses and adequate library copies in schools.

Concluding Lesson: The Twofold Failure in El Filibusterismo

In teaching El Filibusterismo, the instructor should highlight the novel’s rigorous double critique:

1. The Failure of the Colonial System: Across thirty-eight chapters, Rizal proves that every peaceful path to dignity is systematically closed off. Cabesang Tales is robbed of his land by corrupt courts (Chapter IV (4)–10); Placido Penitente is insulted for seeking knowledge (Chapter XII (12)–13); the students’ Castilian Academy is turned into friar fee-collecting (Chapter XIV (14)–22); Basilio’s devotion to medicine cannot save Juli or keep him out of prison (Chapter XXVI (26)–32); and an honorable Spanish official can only resign in futility (Chapter XXXI (31)). Colonial rule makes peaceful reform impossible.

2. The Failure of Vengeance: Yet Rizal simultaneously ensures that Simoun’s conspiracy collapses twice—first when María Clara dies (Chapter XXIII (23)), and second when Isagani throws the lamp into the river to save Paulita (Chapter XXXV (35)). Simoun is mortally wounded not by Spanish soldiers, but by the very outlaws he deceived. Through Father Florentino, Rizal warns that a revolution built on corruption, bribery, and private hatred will fail to redeem a people.

Freedom cannot be bought with gold or achieved through crime. True liberation requires moral dignity, civic virtue, and self-sacrifice—entrusted to an uncorrupted generation of youth.
