Part I · Historical Context 1872 – 1891

Written between October 1887 and March 1891; published in Ghent, Belgium, on September 18, 1891.

From Reformist Diagnosis to Political Crisis

Thirteen years after his disappearance at the end of Noli Me Tángere, Crisóstomo Ibarra returns to the Philippines under a new identity. Now known as Simoun, he has become a wealthy jeweler with close connections to the colonial government. Unlike the idealistic student he once was, he no longer believes that peaceful reform can change the country.

01

Meaning of the Title: El Filibusterismo

Outline I.A · Etymology and Colonial Usage

In the seventeenth century, the Spanish word filibustero referred to pirates operating in the Caribbean Sea. By the late nineteenth century in the Philippines, colonial officials and friars used the term to label any native Filipino or educated ilustrado suspected of undermining Spanish rule or advocating separation from Spain.

In an 1887 letter to his Austrian friend Ferdinand Blumentritt, José Rizal explained how much terror the label carried in Manila: “It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged, or a presumptuous man.” Charles Derbyshire’s 1912 English translation popularized the title The Reign of Greed, emphasizing the institutional corruption depicted in the story, whereas the original Spanish title more directly means The Subversion.

Historical Shift of the Term
17th Century · Caribbean Piracy

Derived from the Dutch vrijbuiter (“freebooter”), describing raiders who attacked Spanish shipping.

Late 19th Century · Philippine Colony

Applied by Spanish authorities and censors to anyone questioning friar estates or colonial policy.

1891 & 1912 · Rizal and Derbyshire

Rizal adopts the colonial accusation for his title; Derbyshire renders its theme in English as The Reign of Greed.

02

Historical Background and Writing Timeline (1872–1891)

Outline I.A–C · Gomburza, Calamba, and Ghent
17 Feb 1872 Bagumbayan, Manila

Execution of the Gomburza Priests

Following the Cavite Mutiny, colonial authorities executed three Filipino secular priests by garrote: Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora. Rizal was eleven years old in 1872, and his brother Paciano had lived and studied with Father Burgos. Nineteen years later, Rizal dedicated El Filibusterismo to their memory.

“The Church, by refusing to degrade you, has placed in doubt the crime that has been imputed to you; the Government, by surrounding your trials with mystery and shadows, causes the belief that there was some error, committed in fatal moments; and all the Philippines, by worshiping your memory and calling you martyrs, in no sense recognizes your culpability.”

José Rizal, Dedication to Gomburza (1891)

Because Rizal wrote the dedication from memory in Europe without access to Manila parish or court records, he misstated the date and ages. Historical records from the National Historical Commission of the Philippines (NHCP) and historian Ambeth Ocampo establish the exact figures:

Execution Date

Fr. José Burgos

Fr. Jacinto Zamora

1887 – 1891 Calamba, Laguna

First Drafts and the Calamba Hacienda Evictions

Rizal began writing the sequel in Calamba in October 1887 while tenant farmers, including his own family, contested rising rents and expanding boundaries on the Dominican-owned hacienda. Between 1890 and 1891, Governor-General Valeriano Weyler sent troops to enforce evictions, tear down houses, and exile Rizal’s brother Paciano and brothers-in-law. This land conflict directly informed the chapters on Cabesang Tales.

1890 – 1891 Madrid & Dagupan

Divisions in Madrid and the Marriage of Leonor Rivera

While revising the novel in Europe, Rizal broke with Marcelo H. del Pilar over the leadership of the Filipino colony in Madrid and the editorial policy of La Solidaridad, concluding that lobbying Spanish politicians abroad could no longer secure meaningful reform. Late in 1890, he also received word from Leonor Rivera that she was engaged to British railway engineer Henry Kipping (whom she married in Dagupan on June 17, 1891), an experience reflected in Paulita Gómez breaking off her engagement with Isagani.

Aug – Sept 1891 Biarritz & Ghent

Printing Halted at Page 112 and Rescued by Valentin Ventura

Rizal finished the manuscript in Biarritz on March 29, 1891, and moved to Ghent, Belgium, where the F. Meyer-Van Loo Press offered lower printing rates than Brussels. Sharing a small room with José Alejandrino, Rizal pawned his jewelry to pay the press in installments.

“I have already pawned all my jewels, I live in a tiny room, I eat in the cheapest restaurant in order to economize and be able to publish my book; soon I will have to stop its publication if no money comes… Sometimes I feel like burning my manuscript.”

José Rizal to José María Basa, Ghent, August 6, 1891 (stopped at page 112)

Learning that printing had stopped, Valentin Ventura sent funds from Paris to pay the remaining balance. The book came off the press on September 18, 1891, and Rizal sent Ventura the original 279-page handwritten manuscript in gratitude.

Part II.A · Chapters 1–10 Act I

The Pasig River steamer, the land dispute in Tiani, and the meeting in the San Diego forest.

Colonial Hierarchy and Agrarian Dispossession

The opening ten chapters introduce late nineteenth-century colonial society aboard a slow Pasig River steamship, trace how a law-abiding farming family in Tiani is driven into armed rebellion, and stage the central debate between reform and revolution.

03

Chapters 1–2: Social Hierarchy Aboard the Bapor Tabo

Outline II.A.1–2 · Visual Centerpiece
Who Occupies This Space

Spanish friars (Fathers Salví, Camorra, Sibyla, and Irene), colonial official Don Custodio, journalist Ben Zayb, the Europeanized Doña Victorina, and the jeweler Simoun.

Physical Conditions

Shaded beneath canvas awnings, seated in comfortable armchairs with cigars and cold drinks, far removed from the heat of the engine.

What It Reveals About Colonial Society

Policy is debated by officials and clerics insulated from the people who perform the colony’s labor. Doña Victorina complains about indios on board while ignoring the Filipino captain and crew steering the vessel.

Simoun’s Forced-Labor Proposal

When the officials argue over how to clear the shallow Pasig River, Simoun proposes digging a straight canal from Manila to Laguna by compelling townspeople, men, women, and children, to work for months without pay. When Don Custodio objects that unpaid labor has triggered uprisings in the past, Simoun deliberately provokes the ruling class by praising authoritarian force:

“Wait! Was that how the pyramids of Egypt were built, or the Colosseum in Rome? Were there revolts? No—and the dead remain dead, while posterity bows before the work!” Simoun on the Upper Deck, Chapter 1
Simoun · View Character Profile

Crisóstomo Ibarra disguised as an affluent jeweler who encourages worse colonial abuses in order to hasten a general uprising.

Who Occupies This Space

Native Filipino passengers (indios), Chinese peddlers and merchants, provincial women, and university students including Basilio and Isagani.

Physical Conditions

Crowded onto wooden benches among trunks, baskets, and cargo crates directly beside the noise and heat of the steam boiler.

What It Reveals About Colonial Society

The colonized majority bears the physical weight and heat of the state without representation, yet it is below deck where the younger generation organizes for educational reform and national dignity.

Isagani’s Reply to Simoun

Coming down to the lower deck, Simoun offers beer to Basilio and Isagani. When they decline and Simoun remarks that Father Camorra blames Filipino passivity on drinking too much water, Isagani answers with a warning that compares calm citizens to water heated by fire:

“Tell Father Camorra that if he drank water instead of wine or beer, perhaps we might all be better off and fewer rumors would circulate… Water is sweet and can be drunk, though it drowns out wine and beer and puts out fire; and heated, it becomes steam; and ruffled, it is the ocean that once destroyed humanity and made the earth tremble to its foundations!” Isagani to Simoun, Chapter 2 (Debajo de cubierta)
Circular Hull

Almost round like a native dipper (tabo), making it hard to distinguish bow from stern: an administration uncertain whether it is moving forward or backward.

Coat of White Paint

Painted white to project European modernity while remaining grimy and rusted underneath.

Whistle and Bamboo Poles

Blows its steam whistle constantly like a ruler issuing decrees, while Filipino crewmen must push it off sandbars with bamboo poles.

A Microcosm of Nineteenth-Century Rule

By opening the novel on the Bapor Tabo, Rizal establishes the social structure of the colony before introducing any subplot. Racial and clerical privilege sits above deck; native labor and student idealism sit below deck; and the vessel itself labors against the current of history while claiming to lead the country toward progress.

Chapters 4 & 10: Cabesang Tales and the Hacienda Dispute

Outline II.A.3 & IV.C · Agrarian Dispossession
Progression of Rent and Loss in Tiani
1. Clearing the Forest 2 Family Deaths

Tales clears unclaimed forest with his family; his wife and eldest daughter Lucia die of malaria.

2. First Friar Claim 20 – 30 Pesos

A religious order claims the fields. Tandang Selo advises Tales to pretend a crocodile swallowed the money.

3. Second Levy & Tax Duty 50 Pesos

Named village tax-collector (Cabesa), Tales is forced to cover unpaid taxes of dead neighbors.

4. Final Rent Hike & Lawsuit 200 Pesos

Tales asks the friars for a title deed. None exists, yet provincial judges rule against him.

5. Kidnapping & Eviction 500-Peso Ransom

Tano is conscripted; Juli becomes a servant on Christmas Day to pay bandits; Tales takes Simoun’s revolver.

Cabesang Tales, son of the woodcarver Tandang Selo, clears unused forest land in Tiani and invests his savings and labor into the soil. Once the fields become productive, a neighboring friar corporation claims ownership and raises the annual rent from twenty or thirty pesos to fifty, and then to two hundred pesos. Tales exhausts every legal channel first, spending his savings on lawyers and court appeals, only to learn that colonial judges will not rule against the religious order even when it produces no title deed.

After colonial authorities confiscate his shotgun and bolo, bandits capture Tales and demand a 500-peso ransom. Because his son Tano has already been conscripted into the colonial army, his daughter Juli sells her jewelry, keeping only a locket that once belonged to María Clara, and enters domestic servitude under Hermana Penchang on Christmas morning to borrow the remaining 250 pesos. That night, Tandang Selo loses his ability to speak.

In Chapter 10, Simoun stays at Tales’ house to sell jewelry to wealthy townspeople while Tales faces eviction within three days. The next morning, Tales leaves María Clara’s locket in Simoun’s empty holster, takes Simoun’s revolver, kills the friar administrator and the new tenant who took his fields, and joins the mountain outlaws under the name Matanglawin.

Cabesang Tales

Tenant farmer of Tiani who turns to armed resistance after losing his land and legal appeal.

Juli

Daughter of Cabesang Tales and fiancée of Basilio; enters domestic servitude to ransom her father.

05

Chapters 6–7: Basilio and Simoun in the San Diego Forest

Outline II.A.4 & IV.A.1 · Reform vs. Revolution
1. Simoun’s Argument Against Assimilation

Rejecting Hispanization

Visiting his mother Sisa’s grave on Christmas Eve, medical student Basilio recognizes Simoun without his tinted glasses as Crisóstomo Ibarra. Rather than silence him, Simoun tries to win him over. When Basilio explains that university students want a Spanish-language academy to gain equal rights with Spain, Simoun argues that adopting the colonizer’s language without political freedom will erode national identity:

“Spanish will never be the general language of the country, the people will never talk it, because the conceptions of their brains and the beatings of their hearts do not have phrases in that idioma; each people has its own tongue, as it has its own way of feeling! What are you going to do with Castilian, the few of you who will speak it? Kill off your own originality, subordinate your thoughts to other brains, and instead of making yourselves free, make yourselves truly slaves!” Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 7
2. Basilio’s Position

Faith in Science and Peaceful Work

Taken in as an unpaid servant by Captain Tiago thirteen years earlier, Basilio endured classroom neglect and poverty to reach his final year of medical school. Two months away from graduation, he hopes to marry Juli and work quietly as a provincial doctor.

Basilio refuses Simoun’s conspiracy. He replies that political vengeance cannot bring back his mother Sisa or his brother Crispin, and that his duty is to treat physical illness rather than join an armed revolt. Simoun allows him to leave, predicting that experience under colonial rule will eventually force Basilio to change his mind.

Basilio · View Character Profile

Graduating medical student who initially chooses personal perseverance and clinical science over political confrontation.

Part II.B · Chapters 11–24 Act II

University classrooms, legal petitions for the Castilian Academy, and Simoun’s aborted first uprising.

Colonial Education and the Student Campaign

In Manila, the middle chapters examine why peaceful institutional reform fails. Rizal depicts a university system that discourages scientific inquiry, a bureaucracy that neutralizes student petitions, and a revolutionary plot that collapses because of Simoun’s private attachment to María Clara.

06

Chapter 13: The Physics Class at Santo Tomas

Outline II.B.1–2 & IV.B · Education as Control

After showing the Captain-General deciding state matters over card games with friars at Los Baños (Chapter 11), Rizal turns to higher education in Chapter 13. Placido Penitente, a respected student from Batangas, sits among two hundred fifty classmates in the physics hall of the University of Santo Tomas. Brass pulleys, batteries, and Leyden jars sit locked behind glass cabinets so visiting foreigners will admire the university’s equipment, yet students are never permitted to use them.

Instead of teaching experimental physics, Father Millon forces students to recite memorized definitions and ridicules their pronunciation. Pushed past his limit by public insults over the attendance roll, Placido throws down his textbook, walks out of class, and later meets Simoun, who shows him a workshop on the edge of Manila where a former schoolteacher is loading gunpowder into shells.

Chapter 13 · Father Millon’s Roll Book
15 Absences
Inflated Attendance Count

When Placido points out that he missed class only three times, Father Millon claims he multiplies each absence by five to cover the days he did not call the roll.

“Absent in Spirit”

Asked how Placido can be marked absent on the same day he recites, Millon answers that he is “absent in spirit.”

Placido’s Response

“Enough, Father, enough! Your Reverence can put down all the absences you wish, but you have no right to insult me!”

07

Chapters 14–20: Campaign for the Castilian Academy

Outline II.B.3 · Señor Pasta and Don Custodio
1. Señor Pasta (Chapter 15)

Self-Preservation Among Educated Elites

Seeking lawful approval for their Spanish-language academy, student leaders Isagani, Macaraig, and Sandoval appeal to influential figures in Manila. Isagani visits Señor Pasta, a prominent Filipino lawyer who advises the friars. Pasta refuses to get involved, telling Isagani to focus on earning a living, marry a wealthy wife, and avoid civic causes, pointing to his own gray hair as proof of wisdom. Isagani answers:

“When I have gray hairs like those, sir, and turn my gaze back over my past and see that I have worked only for myself, without having done what I plainly could and should have done for the country that has given me everything… every gray hair will be a thorn, and instead of rejoicing in them, I shall be ashamed of them!” Isagani to Señor Pasta, Chapter 15
2. Don Custodio (Chapter 20)

Bureaucratic Compromise

The petition is referred to Spanish official Don Custodio de Salazar y Sánchez de Monteredondo, whose office shelves are lined with hundreds of unread folders labeled Projects in Study and Projects in Preparation.

Influenced by Father Sibyla and the dancer Pepay, Don Custodio announces a compromise that strips the academy of independence: the school may open only if a religious order directs it, leaving the students responsible merely for collecting tuition fees.

Isagani · View Character Profile

Student poet and nephew of Father Florentino; leading advocate for the student academy.

08

Chapters 16–23: Smuggled Weapons, the Quiapo Fair, and María Clara’s Death

Outline II.B.4–5 · The First Uprising Aborted
1. Chapter 16 · Quiroga’s Warehouse

Rifles Hidden in Crates

Chinese merchant Quiroga owes Simoun 9,000 pesos for three diamond bracelets. Simoun reduces the debt to 7,000 pesos in exchange for storing smuggled crates of rifles in Quiroga’s warehouse, planning to plant weapons in homes across Manila to provoke mass arrests and panic.

2. Chapters 17–18 · The Quiapo Fair

Mr. Leeds and Imuthis

At a fairground booth, American illusionist Mr. Leeds presents a talking Egyptian head named Imuthis. Speaking before Father Salví, the head recounts how a priest forged letters to frame him and pursue a young woman into a temple, a parallel to Ibarra and María Clara that causes Salví to faint in fear.

3. Chapters 22–23 · Santa Clara

The Aborted 8:00 PM Signal

At 7:00 PM, with armed men waiting outside Manila for his 8:00 PM cannon signal, Simoun visits Basilio and assigns him to rescue María Clara from the Santa Clara convent. When Basilio tells him she died at 6:00 PM that afternoon, Simoun collapses in grief and abandons the attack, showing how his revolt depended on personal motive.

Part II.C · Chapters 25–34 Act III

Subversive posters at the university, Juli’s death in Tiani, and Simoun’s nitroglycerin lamp.

Student Arrests, Juli’s Death, and Simoun’s Plot

After the failure of the student petition, colonial authorities use anonymous posters to arrest university leaders. The consequences fall hardest on Basilio and Juli, turning Basilio from a reluctant medical student into Simoun’s accomplice.

09

Chapters 25–29: The Subversive Posters and Captain Tiago’s Will

Outline II.C.1–3 · Institutional Repression
1. Chapters 25–26 · Noodle-Shop Banquet and Arrests

The Pasquinades Pretext

Disappointed by Don Custodio’s ruling, fourteen students hold a satirical dinner at a Chinese noodle shop, the Pansiteria Macanista de Buen Gusto, dedicating dishes to the friars and Custodio. The next morning, seditious posters (pasquinades) are discovered on the university doors.

Authorities treat the posters as evidence of a conspiracy and arrest the student association members. Although Basilio did not attend the dinner and spent the morning treating patients at San Juan de Dios Hospital, he is arrested when he visits Macaraig’s house to borrow money for his graduation fees.

2. Chapters 27–29 · Unequal Justice

Wealthy Students Freed, Basilio Abandoned

Following a frank debate between Isagani and the Jesuit Father Fernández over the failings of colonial education (Chapter 27), family connections and money quickly secure freedom for the affluent students: Macaraig leaves for Europe and Isagani is released through his uncle’s help.

Only Basilio remains in jail. Terrified by Father Irene’s stories of executions, Captain Tiago dies, and Irene announces as executor that Tiago’s fortune has been left to religious institutions, revoking even Basilio’s twenty-five-peso student bequest.

10

Chapter 30: Juli and Father Camorra in Tiani

Outline II.C.3 · Human Cost of Clerical Abuse

In Tiani, news spreads that Basilio is the sole student remaining in prison and faces deportation to the Carolines. Without money for a lawyer, townspeople and the local judge tell Juli that only the parish priest, Father Camorra, has enough influence in Manila to secure Basilio’s release. Juli resists going to the convent for days because she knows Camorra’s reputation for abusing village women and remembers his advances when she previously sought help for her grandfather.

When rumors arrive that Basilio’s deportation order is imminent, Juli reluctantly walks to the parish convent at dusk accompanied by Sister Bali. Overcome with dread on the staircase, she has to be urged inside as the doors close.

That night, screams are heard from the parish house, followed by a fall onto the stone courtyard below: rather than submit to Father Camorra’s assault, Juli leaps from the convent window to her death.

The next morning, finding the doors of the judge and town officials closed to his grief, her mute grandfather Tandang Selo takes his hunting spear and leaves town to join the outlaws in the mountains.

Chapters 33–35: Simoun’s Conspiracy and the Pomegranate Lamp

Outline II.C.4 & IV.D.3 · Visual Centerpiece
Exhibit Plate II · La última razón
Simoun’s pomegranate lamp containing two liters of nitroglycerin

1. A Wedding Gift for Calle Anloague

Released from prison through Simoun’s intervention after two months, Basilio learns of Juli’s death and goes directly to Simoun’s house on the Escolta asking for a weapon. Simoun takes him into his private laboratory and opens a velvet-lined leather case holding an ornate vessel shaped like a ripe pomegranate the size of a man’s head, crafted in gold and oxidized steel with carnelian seeds.

Presented as Simoun’s wedding gift to newlyweds Juanito Pelaez and Paulita Gómez, the lamp is designed to sit unsuspected at the center of the main dining table.

2. Nitroglycerin and Mercury Fulminate

Inside the heavy casing is an iron chamber holding two liters of liquid nitroglycerin. Once lit, the burner produces a bright white glare calculated to dim after twenty minutes. When a guest or servant turns the wheel to raise the wick, an internal pin strikes a cap of mercury fulminate, detonating the flask.

“Yes, nitroglycerin! And it is also something more than nitroglycerin: it is concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, and injustices! It is the supreme argument of the weak—force against force, violence against violence!” Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 33

3. Decapitating the Colonial Government

The wedding reception takes place at Captain Tiago’s former mansion on Calle Anloague, which Juanito’s father Don Timoteo Pelaez has purchased and redecorated. Simoun’s associates have already lined the ceilings and floors of the house with sacks of gunpowder so no guest inside can survive the blast.

Because the Captain-General, senior friars, judges, and prominent officials are all attending the banquet, the explosion is meant to wipe out Manila’s ruling authorities in one stroke and serve as the signal for Cabesang Tales’ fighters and Basilio’s armed crowd to seize the city.

4. Basilio’s Warning and Isagani’s Intervention

Standing across the street waiting for the blast, Basilio sees his friend Isagani lingering outside the mansion to catch one last glimpse of his former fiancée, Paulita Gómez. Unwilling to let Isagani die, Basilio warns him that the glowing lamp in the dining kiosk is nitroglycerin.

Thinking only of Paulita’s life, Isagani rushes upstairs at the exact moment the flame dims and Father Irene reaches for the wick, grabs the lamp from the table, and throws it into the Pasig River.

Part II.D · Chapters 35–39 Act IV

The banquet at Calle Anloague, the countryside ambush, and Simoun’s final hours by the Pacific Ocean.

The Wedding Banquet and the Final Confession

When the bomb at Calle Anloague fails to detonate, Simoun’s conspiracy unravels overnight. The closing chapters trace the human aftermath in the provinces and bring the hunted fugitive to the coastal home of Father Florentino.

Chapter 35: Mane, Thecel, Phares and Isagani’s Choice

Outline II.D.1–2 · The Foiled Conspiracy

Mane · Thecel · Phares

Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra

1. Inside the Dining Hall

The Warning from the Book of Daniel

After placing the lamp on the centerpiece table and leaving the mansion, Simoun leaves behind a slip of parchment written in red ink bearing the Aramaic words from Belshazzar’s feast: Mane, Thecel, Phares (“Measured, Weighed, Found Wanting”), signed with the name Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra.

Passed among the guests, the note reaches Father Salví, who recognizes Ibarra’s handwriting from thirteen years earlier and turns pale with terror just as the lamp’s flame begins to sink.

2. Moral Irony of the Climax

Saving the System He Opposed

Warned on the street below by Basilio, Isagani bursts into the dining kiosk before Father Irene can turn the wick, seizes the lamp, and leaps from the balcony into the Pasig River, where the guests mistake him for a thief.

The scene underscores a central contradiction in the novel: the student who argued most forcefully against colonial injustice before Señor Pasta and Father Fernández ends up saving the Captain-General and the friars because he cannot bear to let Paulita die.

13

Chapters 36–38: Censorship and the Death of Tandang Selo

Outline II.D.3 · Colonial Violence in the Provinces

In Manila, the Captain-General forbids Ben Zayb and the press from publishing any account of the wedding incident in order to conceal the threat to the government (Chapter 36). Meanwhile, in the provinces, Filipino soldiers of the Guardia Civil march bound village suspects along a mountain road (Chapter 38). When a guard named Mautang beats the prisoners, a young soldier nicknamed Carolino, recently returned from service in the Caroline Islands, objects to the cruelty. Carolino is actually Tano, the conscripted son of Cabesang Tales, unaware of what has befallen his sister Juli and his family.

When Matanglawin’s guerrillas ambush the patrol from the cliffs above, the corporal threatens to shoot Carolino unless he fires at an elderly rebel standing on a boulder with a bamboo lance.

Carolino obeys and fires. Reaching the top of the rocks, he sees the dying old man look straight into his eyes and point a finger silently at him: colonial conscription has made Tano kill his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo.
14

Chapter 39: Simoun’s Confession and Father Florentino’s Response

Outline II.D.4 & IV.A.2 · Final Chapter
1. Simoun’s Confession

Why Did the Uprising Fail?

Shot while fleeing Manila, Simoun seeks shelter with Isagani’s uncle, the retired Filipino secular priest Father Florentino. Informed by telegram that the Guardia Civil will come to arrest the fugitive at eight that evening, Simoun drinks slow-acting poison so he will not be captured alive and confesses his true identity as Crisóstomo Ibarra.

Recounting thirteen years of betrayal and failed plans, Simoun asks why God allowed colonial injustice to endure while frustrating his attempt to end it:

“Is it God’s will that these islands continue in the condition in which they suffer? Must the weak die so that the wicked may live? Why did that God deny me His aid?” Simoun to Father Florentino, Chapter 39
2. Father Florentino’s Response

The Moral Conditions of Freedom

Father Florentino replies that Simoun’s movement failed because he tried to win liberty by spreading bribery, crime, and hatred rather than building civic virtue among the people:

“Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow? And no doubt they will be, because whoever submits to tyranny loves it! Señor Simoun, so long as our people are not prepared, so long as they enter the struggle deceived or driven, without a clear consciousness of what they are to do, the wisest attempts will fail, and better that they do fail!” Father Florentino, Chapter 39

Simoun presses the priest’s hand and dies quietly without speaking again. After nightfall, Father Florentino carries Simoun’s iron chest of jewels to the cliff and throws it into the Pacific Ocean, praying that Nature keep the treasure hidden until it is needed for a just and honorable purpose.

Father Florentino · View Character Profile

Dignified native Filipino secular priest who voices the novel’s closing argument on moral readiness.

Parts V & VI · Conclusion 1887 – 1956

Comparison with Noli Me Tángere, historical reception, and concluding literary interpretation.

Legacy and Concluding Interpretation

Published five years before the 1896 Philippine Revolution, El Filibusterismo exposed the dead end of colonial assimilation while raising difficult ethical questions about armed revolt that shaped both revolutionary leaders and modern Philippine education.

Comparing Noli Me Tángere (1887) and El Filibusterismo (1891)

Outline V · Structural Comparison
Analytical Dimension Noli Me Tángere (Berlin, 1887) El Filibusterismo (Ghent, 1891)
Dedication & Tone Dedicated “To My Fatherland,” diagnosing colonial abuses as a “social cancer”; romantic, satirical, and descriptive. Dedicated to the executed priests Gomburza (1872); political, analytical, and focused on ideological debate.
Protagonist’s Trajectory Crisóstomo Ibarra: Returns from Europe trusting that education and cooperation with Spanish authorities will uplift San Diego. Simoun: Returns from Cuba using wealth and bribery to worsen abuses and destroy the colonial elite by force.
Scope of Critique Focuses primarily on the power of the Spanish friar orders (frailocracia) while appealing to Madrid for reform. Critiques the entire colonial system, including Madrid-appointed governors, courts, friars, and passive educated Filipinos.
Structure & Length 64 Chapters (plus the omitted chapter “Elias and Salome”); broad portrait of provincial society. 39 Chapters; tighter structure centered on Manila, Tiani, and debates over reform and revolution.

Historical Reception and the Unresolved Question of 1891

Outline VI · Reception & Concluding Synthesis
1. Censorship and Exile (1891–1892)

Hong Kong to Dapitan

Rizal shipped most of the Ghent copies in crates to José María Basa in Hong Kong before sailing from Marseilles on October 18, 1891. Spanish customs confiscated smuggled copies in Manila, and colonial censors cited subversive writings when Governor-General Despujol exiled Rizal to Dapitan in July 1892.

2. Revolutionary Readers (1892–1896)

Influence on the Katipunan

Although Father Florentino cautioned against premature revolt, Andrés Bonifacio—who kept copies of both novels—and members of the Katipunan saw in the defeated student academy and Cabesang Tales’ dispossession clear evidence that peaceful reform under Spain was no longer possible.

3. National Preservation (1925–1956)

The Manuscript and the Rizal Law

Bought from Valentin Ventura in 1925 for ₱10,000, the 279-page manuscript survived the 1945 Battle of Manila and a brief 1961 theft at the National Library. On June 12, 1956, Republic Act No. 1425 (authored by Sen. Claro M. Recto and sponsored by Sen. Jose P. Laurel) required unexpurgated editions to be taught across Philippine schools and universities.

Why Rizal Leaves the Conflict Unresolved

El Filibusterismo refuses to offer a simple political formula. Across its first thirty-eight chapters, Rizal shows every lawful path to dignity closed off: Cabesang Tales loses his land in colonial courts, the students’ Castilian Academy is hollowed out by Don Custodio, and Basilio’s quiet compliance cannot save Juli or keep him out of prison. By showing why ordinary citizens are pushed toward rebellion, the novel explains why colonial rule in the Philippines was approaching collapse.

At the same time, Rizal allows Simoun’s conspiracy to fail twice—first when María Clara dies, and second when Isagani throws the lamp into the Pasig—because Simoun’s revolution is built on private vengeance, manipulation, and the deliberate worsening of public suffering. Through Father Florentino, Rizal argues that political separation alone does not guarantee freedom if a society has not developed civic responsibility and respect for justice.

Rather than choosing between passive submission and vengeance-driven terror, Rizal places the burden on the next generation: to build a nation morally prepared not merely to overthrow tyranny, but to avoid becoming tyrants themselves.
