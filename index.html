01

The Meaning of Filibustero and the Gomburza Dedication

Orientation · Historical Context · 1872 & 1891 · Manila & Europe

Originally referring to seventeenth-century Caribbean buccaneers, the Spanish word filibustero was weaponized by colonial authorities in the nineteenth-century Philippines to condemn any Filipino suspected of subverting colonial rule.

Writing to Ferdinand Blumentritt in 1887, Rizal explained: “The word filibustero is very little known in the Philippines... The educated people fear the reach of the word... It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be on the gallows.” Charles Derbyshire’s 1912 English translation titled it The Reign of Greed, whereas the original Spanish literally means The Subversion.

Dedication Page · February 17, 1872

To the Memory of the Three Priests

Rizal dedicated the novel to secular priests Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora, executed by garrote at Bagumbayan following the 1872 Cavite Mutiny.

Historical note: Writing from memory in Europe, Rizal misdated their execution as “28 de Febrero” and estimated their ages (actual NHCP records: Gómez 72–73, Burgos 35, Zamora 36–37).

Causal Bridge: The 1872 Bagumbayan martyrdom shattered the illusion of colonial reform and set Rizal on the path to writing the sequel.
02

Four Milestones that Shaped El Filibusterismo

Orientation · Historical Chronology · 1872 – 1891 · Calamba to Ghent
Milestone 1 · Feb 1872

Gomburza Martyrdom

Execution of secular priests Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora at Bagumbayan. A ten-year-old Rizal vows to avenge their memory through letters and national awakening.

Milestone 2 · 1887–1891

Calamba Land Evictions

Dominican rent increases and military evictions of Rizal’s family and tenants under Gov.-Gen. Weyler. Directly inspires the dispossession of Cabesang Tales.

Milestone 3 · 1890–1891

Madrid Schism & Heartbreak

Disillusionment with Del Pilar’s lobbying in Spain; Leonor Rivera marries Henry Kipping, inspiring Paulita Gómez’s abandonment of Isagani for Juanito Pelaez.

Milestone 4 · Sept 1891

Ghent Printing Crisis

Printing halts at page 112 in Ghent due to extreme poverty. Valentin Ventura loans funds from Paris, allowing publication on September 18, 1891.

Historical Focus: Rizal’s own family struggles and disillusionment in Europe directly transformed into the darker, urgent tone of the sequel.
03

The Bridge from Noli Me Tángere: The Return

Orientation · Literary Continuity · Thirteen Years After Noli Me Tángere
Noli Me Tángere · Berlin, 1887

Crisóstomo Ibarra: The Peaceful Reformer

A wealthy, European-educated young Filipino returns to San Diego trusting in Spanish justice, modern education, and legal progress. He seeks to build a public schoolhouse and marry his childhood love, María Clara.

Result: Framed for an uprising by Padre Salví, hunted by civil guards, and reported killed on Laguna de Bay.

El Filibusterismo · Ghent, 1891

Simoun: The Cynical Subversive

Thirteen years later, Ibarra returns unrecognizable behind large blue-tinted goggles and a foreign accent. Wealthy from Cuban mines, he acts as the Governor-General’s “Brown Cardinal,” using bribery and administrative tyranny to drive the people to violent revolution.

Core Question: Can violent retaliation redeem a nation, or does corrupt revolution destroy itself?

Classroom Orientation: The story begins on a morning in late December aboard a river steamer traveling upstream from Manila.
04

The Steamer Tabo: A Society Divided

Unit 1 · Chapters I – III · Late December · Pasig River
The Bapor Tabo navigating upstream toward Laguna de Bay

Rizal opens the novel aboard the river steamer Tabo, explicitly comparing it to the colonial “Ship of State”:

  • Clumsy and Round: Shaped like a native coconut-shell dipper (tabo), moving with a heavy, awkward gait.
  • Whitewashed Rust: Freshly painted white on the outside to hide dirt, rust, and mechanical decay.
  • Stalled on Sandbars: Continually running aground, pushed forward only by sailors with bamboo poles.
  • Racial & Class Divide: Two distinct decks physically segregating the colonial rulers from the ruled.
Novel Fact: Rizal’s narrator states in paragraph 3: “...the Ship of State, so the joke runs, also has a slow and heavy gait.”
05

Chapter I: On the Upper Deck and Forced Labor

Unit 1 · Chapter I (1) · Pasig River · Upper Deck

Beneath comfortable canvas awnings sit the Spanish friars (Padre Salví, Padre Sibyla, Padre Camorra, Padre Irene), bureaucrat Don Custodio, journalist Ben-Zayb, pretentious socialite Doña Victorina, and the jeweler Simoun.

As the steamer stalls in the shallow, winding river, Don Custodio proposes forcing riverside villagers to breed ducks so the birds will eat snails and deepen the mud.

Simoun’s Chilling Counter-Proposal: Dig a brand-new straight canal through Manila, close the Pasig River, and pay no wages at all. Draft all convicts, villagers, old men, and boys to work with their own tools and food.

“The dead remain dead, and might only is considered right by posterity... Thus were constructed the Pyramids, Lake Moeris, and the Colosseum in Rome.” Simoun · Chapter I, On the Upper Deck
Plain-English Meaning: Simoun argues that powerful empires are built on the suffering of unpaid forced laborers, and history honors the brutal conquerors, not the victims who died.
What happens next: Rejected by the scandalized aristocrats, Simoun descends below deck to inspect the passengers in the lower hold.
06

Chapter II: On the Lower Deck — The Steam Warning

Unit 1 · Chapter II (2) · Pasig River · Lower Deck

Crowded in the stifling hold beside the boilers and cargo sit native Filipino travelers, Chinese merchants, and university students Basilio and Isagani, accompanied by secular priest Father Florentino.

Basilio and Isagani are leading a student movement to establish an Academy of Castilian, offering to fund teachers and classrooms through student contributions.

Simoun approaches below deck and taunts them, claiming Filipinos lack energy and spirit because they drink only water instead of beer. Isagani delivers a famous counter-warning:

“Water is very sweet and can be drunk, but it can put out the fire of wine and beer... Water, when heated, becomes steam; when agitated, it becomes the ocean, which can rise and destroy.” Isagani to Simoun · Chapter II, On the Lower Deck
Plain-English Meaning: Isagani warns that while the Filipino people seem gentle and submissive like quiet water, if colonial oppression heats them up, they will turn into destructive steam and a tidal wave of revolution.
What happens next: Father Florentino is called to the upper deck, where the passengers begin sharing river legends.
07

Chapter III: Legends and Simoun's Haunting Silence

Unit 1 · Chapter III (3) · Entering Laguna de Bay
Three River Legends

Folklore of the Pasig

1. Malapad-na-Bato: A sacred rock once worshiped by spirits, later turned into a bandits' hideout.

2. Doña Jerónima’s Cave: A woman walled inside a river cave by her lover, an Archbishop who broke his marriage vow.

3. Miracle of San Nicolás: A Chinese merchant spared when a crocodile turned to stone at his prayer.

The Hidden Plot Trigger

The Waters of San Diego

As the steamer enters Laguna de Bay, Ben-Zayb asks where a fugitive named “Guevara, Navarra, or Ibarra” was killed thirteen years ago. The captain points to the water, describing how the fugitive dove under gunfire and the lake turned red with blood.

Simoun stands completely motionless, deathly pale, staring silently into the distance without uttering a word.

Transition to Chapter IV: Leaving the steamer behind, the narrative moves to the nearby town of Tiani to show how colonial land laws destroy an innocent family.
08

Chapter IV: Cabesang Tales & Agrarian Dispossession

Unit 2 · Chapter IV (4) · Tiani, Laguna

Telesforo (Cabesang Tales), son of old broom-maker Tandang Selo, clears dense forest land at the cost of his wife and eldest daughter Lucia’s lives to malaria.

The Escalation of Extortion

Friar estate claims ownership → Rents raised from 20–30 to 50, then 200 pesos → Tales files lawsuit → Colonial judges acknowledge his justice but refuse to rule against the religious order without deeds → Tales spends his life savings → Son Tano is conscripted → Tales patrols fields with a shotgun, then bolo, then axe.

Kidnapping & Debt Bondage

Juli’s Sacrifice

Disarmed of all weapons by the government, Tales is kidnapped by mountain bandits for a 500-peso ransom.

His daughter Juli (Juliana) sells all her jewelry (saving only María Clara’s locket given by Basilio) and borrows the remaining 250 pesos by entering domestic servitude under Hermana Penchang on Christmas Day.

Historical Context: Directly reflects the 1887–1891 Calamba Hacienda dispute where the Dominican estate raised rents and evicted Rizal’s family.
09

Christmas Eve: Beaten Coachmen and Mute Sorrow

Unit 2 · Chapters V & VIII (5 & 8) · Christmas Eve & Morning · San Diego & Tiani
Chapter V (5) · A Coachman’s Christmas Eve

Sinong’s Beating

Basilio arrives in San Diego by carriage to spend Christmas. His coachman Sinong is twice beaten by the Civil Guard—first for forgetting his tax card (*cédula*), and second because his carriage lamp blew out.

Arriving at Capitan Tiago’s home, Basilio learns that Tano has been conscripted into the military and Cabesang Tales is held captive in the mountains.

Chapter VIII (8) · Merry Christmas

Tandang Selo Struck Mute

Christmas morning in Tiani: Juli bids a tearful farewell to her home and grandfather to report to Hermana Penchang as an indentured servant.

Finding himself completely alone, his son captive and both grandchildren taken, seventy-year-old Tandang Selo suffers a stroke and becomes completely mute (pipi).

What happens next: At midnight on Christmas Eve, Basilio slips out to visit his mother Sisa's grave in the forest.
10

Chapters VI & VII: Basilio Discovers Simoun's Secret

Unit 2 · Chapters VI & VII (6 & 7) · Christmas Eve Midnight · San Diego Forest

Basilio's Background (Ch. 6): Orphaned son of Sisa who rose from Capitan Tiago's unpaid houseboy and neglected Letrán pupil to top medical clinician at the Ateneo.

The Discovery (Ch. 7): Visiting his mother's grave at midnight, Basilio hears digging and watches a man remove his blue-tinted goggles. Basilio recognizes him: Simoun is Crisóstomo Ibarra!

Simoun draws his revolver, admitting he could kill Basilio, but instead offers him an alliance: “I have returned to destroy that system... by precipitating its corruption.”

“You hold a secret that can ruin me... but I won’t kill you. You and I are both dry branches that thirst for light.” Simoun to Basilio · Chapter VII
Basilio's Rejection: Basilio refuses to join Simoun's violent conspiracy: “I only want to practice medicine, cure the sick, ransom Juli, and live in peace.” Simoun warns him that neutrality under tyranny is impossible.
Teaching Key: Rizal preserves chronological discovery: the reader and Basilio learn Simoun's identity together in Chapter 7.
11

Chapters IX & X: The Revolver for the Locket

Unit 2 · Chapters IX & X (9 & 10) · Tiani · Tales' Usurped House

Ransomed from bandits with Juli's indenture money, Tales returns to find his house occupied, his daughter enslaved, and a 3-day eviction notice served by the friars.

Simoun stays at Tales' house to sell diamonds to wealthy townsfolk. When Tales shows him María Clara's gold-and-emerald locket (given to Basilio and then to Juli), Simoun urgently offers 500 pesos or any diamond.

Looking out the window, Tales sees the friar administrator and the new tenant laughing at his usurped land.

The Murders of Tiani

The Fatal Exchange

Tales slips away into the night, leaving the locket inside Simoun's empty revolver holster.

Next morning: The friar administrator and the new tenant are found murdered on the land with mouths stuffed with soil. A note in blood reads: “Cabesang Tales”.

Tales joins the mountain guerrillas as Matanglawin. The Civil Guard arrests mute Tandang Selo.

Causal Link: Tales’ murders and the outlaw uprising dominate the Governor-General’s agenda in Chapter XI.
12

Chapter XI: Los Baños — Arbitrary Governance

Unit 3 · Chapter XI (11) · Los Baños, Laguna

At the hot springs of Los Baños, the Captain-General, friars (Sibyla, Irene, Camorra), and bureaucrat Don Custodio play cards (tresillo) while casually deciding administrative decrees:

  • Firearms Ban: Simoun wins blank deportation warrants and supports banning hunting rifles to disarm natives.
  • Silenced Teacher: A village schoolmaster asking for a roof for his classroom is suspended for complaining.
  • The Students' Petition: The Academy of Castilian splits the table. Sibyla fears rebellion; Irene defends it for bribes. The General puts Basilio on the police watchlist.
Padre Camorra's Condition

Juli's Imprisoned Grandfather

When petitions for the release of mute Tandang Selo are raised, Padre Camorra agrees to advocate for the old man—on one condition: that Selo's granddaughter, Juli, come to his convent in Tiani to beg him in person.

Causal Link: The Captain-General refers the student petition to Don Custodio, moving the action to the universities of Manila.
13

Chapters XII & XIII: Placido and the Physics Class

Unit 3 · Chapters XII & XIII (12 & 13) · University of Santo Tomas, Manila

Placido Penitente (Ch. 12): A bright, reluctant student from Batangas, tempted to quit university because of the constant disrespect shown to native youths by colonial professors.

The Class in Physics (Ch. 13): Dominican professor Padre Millón teaches physics in a spacious lecture hall where expensive European instruments are kept permanently locked behind glass cabinets—never touched by students.

Millón subjects students to humiliating scholastic word-games, marks Placido absent for 15 days, and calls him a “philosophaster.”

Placido’s Breaking Point:
“Enough, Padre, enough! Your Reverence may mark me with all the faults you please, but you have no right to insult me!”

Placido drops his books and storms out of the university forever, pushing him straight into Simoun’s revolutionary network.
Pedagogical Significance: Rizal illustrates how colonial education was weaponized to humiliate Filipino minds rather than cultivate scientific literacy.
14

Chapters XIV & XV: Student Idealism vs. Selfish Careerism

Unit 3 · Chapters XIV & XV (14 & 15) · Manila · Makaraig's House & Pasta's Office

In the House of the Students (Ch. 14): Student leaders gather at wealthy Makaraig's house. Spanish student Sandoval champions liberal equality; Pecson cynically predicts friar imprisonment; Isagani resolves to lobby lawyer Señor Pasta.

Señor Pasta's Counsel (Ch. 15): Pasta, Manila’s foremost native lawyer, advises Isagani to submit to the friars, study medicine, marry, and leave politics to the government.

“When I have gray hairs like yours, sir, and look back upon my past and see that I worked only for myself... every hair will be a thorn, and instead of glorying in them, I shall blush for them.” Isagani to Señor Pasta · Chapter XV
Plain-English Meaning: Isagani tells the veteran lawyer that achieving comfortable professional success while abandoning one’s country is shameful, not admirable.
What happens next: While students lobby peacefully, Simoun prepares the military weapons for his violent uprising.
15

Chapters XVI – XVIII: Smuggled Rifles and the Talking Head

Unit 4 · Chapters XVI – XVIII (16–18) · Binondo & Quiapo Fair
Chapter XVI · Quiroga's Dinner

Smuggled Weapons

At merchant Quiroga's lavish banquet, Simoun reduces Quiroga’s 9,000-peso debt to 7,000 pesos on one condition: Quiroga must store smuggled crates of rifles in his warehouse under the cover of commercial merchandise.

Simoun explains the guns will be used for an impending national uprising.

Chapters XVII & XVIII · The Sphinx

Padre Salví Collapses

At the Quiapo Fair, American illusionist Mr. Leeds presents the sphinx head Imuthis.

The head speaks of being framed and murdered by a lustful priest who desired his beloved—mirroring Padre Salví's framing of Ibarra over María Clara. Recognizing the accusation, Salví collapses in hysterics.

What happens next: Simoun recruits the furious Placido Penitente to his underground network.
16

Chapters XIX & XXI: The Clandestine Workshop and the Operetta

Unit 4 · Chapters XIX & XXI (19 & 21) · Calle Iris & Teatro de Variedades

Placido Recruited (Ch. 19): Embittered by UST, Placido meets Simoun, who takes him to a secret pyrotechnic workshop on Calle Iris where bombs and ammunition are manufactured alongside pyrotechnist Castila.

The Theater Spectacle (Ch. 21): Manila society flocks to the Teatro de Variedades for the French operetta Les Cloches de Corneville.

While the audience is captivated by the singing, Simoun positions his armed units across the city to strike at a coordinated signal.

The Plan for Tonight

The Dual Objective

1. Detonate bombs at government gates and coordinate with Cabesang Tales’ mountain fighters entering Manila.

2. Personally breach the Santa Clara convent to rescue María Clara from Padre Salví.

Crucial Timing: Simoun plans the revolt for that very night—until a single piece of news stops him cold.
17

Chapters XX – XXIV: Custodio's Decree and the Aborted Revolt

Unit 4 · Chapters XX – XXIV (20–24) · 10:30 PM · Capitan Tiago's House

The Compromised Academy (Ch. XX & XXII): Don Custodio copies an Augustinian file to approve the Academy, but places it under religious supervision, reducing students to mere fee collectors without academic freedom. At the theater (Ch. XXII), students mock their defeat.

The Aborted Revolt (Ch. XXIII): At 10:30 PM, Simoun visits Basilio, demanding he lead a strike team to free María Clara from the nunnery: “Tonight the revolution breaks out!” Next day on the Malecón (Ch. XXIV), Isagani shares his dreams with Paulita.

Basilio's Fatal Revelation (Ch. XXIII):
“María Clara is dead. She died at six o’clock this evening.”

Simoun collapses in wild, grief-stricken agony. Rushing out into the night without giving the attack signal, the first revolution is completely aborted.
Teaching Key: Simoun's conspiracy was driven by personal passion; when María Clara died, his initial plot collapsed from within.
18

Chapters XXV & XXVI: The Banquet and the Pasquinade Panic

Unit 5 · Chapters XXV & XXVI (25 & 26) · Pansitería & University of Santo Tomas

The Satirical Dinner (Ch. 25): Mocking their defeat, fourteen students hold a dinner at the Pansiteria Macanista de Buen Gusto, dedicating ironic dishes to friars and Don Custodio. Padre Sibyla's servant spies on them.

The Pasquinades (Ch. 26): Next morning, treasonous handwritten posters (pasquinades) appear on university doors.

The regime uses the posters as a pretext for mass arrests. Innocent Basilio, visiting Makaraig to borrow licensing fees, is arrested due to the Chapter 11 watchlist.

Reactions to the Arrests

Juanito Pelaez: Panics, denies knowing any student leaders, and begs to be forgotten.

Isagani: Speaks fearlessly in defense of student dignity, surrenders voluntarily, and faces his interrogators.

Basilio: Having avoided politics to study medicine, is trapped as the sole student without wealthy patrons.

What happens next: Mass panic sweeps Manila while Isagani engages in a fearless debate with his Dominican professor.
19

Chapters XXVII & XXVIII: Intellectual Defiance and Citywide Panic

Unit 5 · Chapters XXVII & XXVIII (27 & 28) · Dominican Cell & Streets of Manila

The Friar and the Filipino (Ch. 27): Summoned by Dominican professor Padre Fernández, Isagani charges that friar education deliberately starves the Filipino mind like corrupt prison contractors who pocket inmates' food rations.

Tatakut (Ch. 28): Wild rumors of impending insurrection paralyze Manila. Panic-stricken soldiers fire at shadows, killing innocent villagers.

Padre Irene's terrifying tales of executions induce a fatal heart attack in Capitan Tiago.

“You say we are ungrateful... Can you expect gratitude from a man whose eyes you have put out, so that he may not see the light?” Isagani to Padre Fernández · Chapter XXVII
Plain-English Meaning: Isagani tells his teacher that Spain cannot demand loyalty and gratitude from Filipinos after deliberately denying them education and human dignity.
What happens next: Wealthy students buy their release, but patronless Basilio remains trapped in Bilibid Prison.
20

Chapters XXIX – XXXI: Juli's Martyrdom and the Resigned Official

Unit 5 · Chapters XXIX – XXXI (29–31) · Tiani & Governor's Palace

Capitan Tiago's Will (Ch. 29): Padre Irene alters Tiago's will, leaving his fortune to the Church and revoking Basilio's 25-peso legacy. Basilio remains the sole student in prison.

Juli's Suicide (Ch. 30): In Tiani, Sister Bali pressures Juli into the convent to plead with Padre Camorra for Basilio. To escape Camorra's sexual assault, Juli leaps from the convent window to her death. Mute Tandang Selo flees with his spear.

Chapter XXXI · The Spanish Conscience

The High Official Resigns

A noble Spanish High Official defends innocent Basilio against the Governor-General’s arbitrary “reason of state,” proclaiming: “Spain must be great not by oppressing, but by honoring justice!”

Rebuffed by the General, the High Official resigns his office and departs for Spain.

Causal Bridge: With peaceful avenues destroyed, Basilio emerges from Bilibid Prison a transformed, vengeful man.
21

Chapter XXXII: Broken Students and Paulita's Choice

Unit 5 · Chapter XXXII (32) · April · Manila
The Crushed Generation

The Death of Reform

Exam season arrives in April: Makaraig flees to Europe; Pecson becomes a court clerk; Tadeo burns his textbooks.

Coachman Sinong visits jailed Basilio and delivers the crushing news that Juli is dead and Tandang Selo has vanished into the hills.

Paulita Gómez & Juanito Pelaez

The High-Society Match

Unwilling to marry an imprisoned reformer who shares his uncle's lonely coastal retreat, Paulita Gómez abandons Isagani.

She agrees to marry wealthy, sycophantic Juanito Pelaez. Simoun partners with groom’s father Don Timoteo Pelaez to finance a lavish wedding reception.

Transition to Climax: The wedding banquet provides Simoun with the ultimate gathering of colonial rulers for his second conspiracy.
22

Chapter XXXIII: A Transformed Basilio Demands Arms

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXIII (33) · Late April · Simoun's Laboratory

Released from Bilibid Prison in late April through Simoun’s influence, a hollow-eyed, emaciated Basilio enters Simoun’s laboratory.

Having lost his mother Sisa, brother Crispin, career, and sweetheart Juli, Basilio renounces his peaceful ideals:

“I have been a bad son and a bad brother... Now give me arms and let the revolution break out! I am ready to serve you!”

Simoun welcomes Basilio as his trusted lieutenant, declaring that tonight the conspiracy will finally succeed.

Simoun leads Basilio to the workbench and reveals his weapon: an oxidized-gold lamp shaped like a split pomegranate.

“Concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, injustice, outrage—the last resort of the weak, force against force!” Simoun · Chapter XXXIII
What happens next: Simoun explains the lethal mechanical structure and chemical timing of the weapon.
23

The Centerpiece: The Nitroglycerin Pomegranate Lamp

Unit 6 · Chapters XXXIII & XXXIV (33 & 34) · Calle Anloague · Technical Anatomy
Lámpara de Simoun · Explosive vessel loaded with nitroglycerin

Simoun's weapon combines fine craftsmanship with modern high-explosive chemistry:

  • The Vessel: Handcrafted crystal and oxidized gold shaped like a split pomegranate set with carnelian seeds.
  • The Core: An inner container of steel 2 centimeters thick holding over one liter of nitroglycerin.
  • The Detonator: A concealed capsule of fulminate of mercury wired to the lamp’s wick.
  • The Timed Trap: The lamp will burn for 20 minutes before dimming; raising the wick detonates the lamp, igniting gunpowder bags beneath the floor.
Target: The Governor-General, the Archbishop, provincial governors, and friars assembled in one room.
24

Chapter XXXIV: The Mined Wedding on Calle Anloague

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXIV (34) · 7:00 – 8:00 PM · Calle Anloague

The Venue: The wedding banquet takes place at Capitan Tiago’s former mansion, bought and ostentatiously remodeled by Don Timoteo Pelaez with gaudy chrome lithographs and mirrors.

On the open-air azotea, an exclusive octagonal kiosk is set for the seven supreme rulers of church and state—secretly wired to sacks of gunpowder beneath the floorboards.

Simoun arrives in a carriage, delivers the pomegranate lamp to the banquet table, and quickly departs pale and hurried.

Outside in the Dark

Isagani Lingers in the Street

Armed with a revolver, Basilio watches guests arrive. He spots heartbroken Isagani wandering nearby, staring up at the brightly lit windows hoping for one last glimpse of Paulita.

Basilio remembers the countdown: in minutes, the entire mansion will be blown into the sky.

What happens next: Upstairs, the wedding feast begins; the clock ticks toward the fatal detonation.
25

Chapter XXXV: Mene, Tekel, Phares & The Thrown Lamp

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXV (35) · 9:00 PM · The Climax

During the meal, a square slip of paper written in red ink is passed among the dignitaries:

Mene, Tekel, Phares — Juan Crisostomo Ibarra

Padre Salví recognizes Ibarra's authentic handwriting and turns ashen with horror. The lamp begins to dim. The Governor-General says: “Padre Irene, will you turn up the wick?”

Isagani’s Intervention:
Outside, Basilio blurts out that the house is mined. Remembering Paulita is inside, Isagani races up the grand staircase, bursts into the kiosk just as Irene touches the wick, snatches the burning lamp, and hurls it over the railing into the Pasig River before diving in himself!

The explosion is defused in the dark depths of the river.
The Twist: Simoun’s conspiracy fails not by military counter-attack, but because Isagani’s love for Paulita overpowers revolutionary violence.

Teaching Analysis: The Supreme Irony of the Climax

Unit 6 · Climax Analysis · Literary & Moral Interpretation
Irony 1 · The Identity of the Savior

Saved by Love, Not by Force

The corrupt colonial regime—its Governor-General, corrupt friars, and extortionist judges—was saved not by its army or secret police, but by the love of the very student who opposed it most fiercely.

Isagani's chivalric devotion to Paulita Gómez saved the lives of the men who oppressed his countrymen.

Irony 2 · The Defect of Vengeance

The Collapse of Corrupt Revolution

Simoun’s revolution collapsed twice: first when María Clara died (Ch. 23), and second when Isagani saved Paulita (Ch. 35).

Rizal demonstrates that a revolution built on deception, personal hatred, and indiscriminate slaughter contains the seeds of its own destruction.

What happens next: Chapters 36 through 38 trace the immediate, devastating consequences of the failed conspiracy.
27

Chapters XXXVI & XXXVII: Silenced Presses and Santa Cruz Secrets

Unit 7 · Chapters XXXVI & XXXVII (36 & 37) · Manila & Santa Cruz

The Gagged Press (Ch. 36): The Governor-General strictly censors Ben-Zayb’s sensational article describing the wedding plot to prevent panic.

Meanwhile, betrayed bandits raid a Pasig country house, wounding Padre Camorra. A captured bandit confesses that a white-haired Spaniard promised them weapons and money, and vows vengeance against Simoun.

Simoun’s hidden store of gunpowder is discovered in his house; Simoun flees the city.

Chapter XXXVII · The Silversmith's House

Isagani Confronts His Act

At the Orenda family home in Santa Cruz, silversmith Chichoy reveals that sacks of gunpowder were found under the dining kiosk floor.

Listening in the corner, Isagani realizes the full magnitude of what he stopped. He remarks cryptically that the thief acted without reflecting, and departs forever for Father Florentino’s coast.

What happens next: In the Luzon mountains, the tragedy of Cabesang Tales’ family reaches a horrific culmination.
28

Chapter XXXVIII: Fatality — Fratricide in the Hills

Unit 7 · Chapter XXXVIII (38) · Luzon Mountains · The Ambush

Conscripted Carolino (Tano): Cabesang Tales’ son Tano, serving as conscript soldier Carolino, marches through the rocky mountains with colonial troops hunting *Matanglawin*’s guerrillas. Carolino protests the brutal torture of captured prisoners.

During a cliffside ambush, troops are ordered to fire on a guerrilla standing on the rocky heights, waving a spear.

The Tragic Extinction of Tales’ Family:
Carolino fires his rifle. Climbing the crags, he discovers he has shot his own grandfather, mute Tandang Selo, who points dying fingers toward the mountain cave where Tales has fled.

The Family Destroyed: Mother & sister Lucia dead of malaria; Tales an outlaw; Juli dead of suicide; Selo shot dead by his own grandson.
Causal Link: Hunted and mortally wounded by outlaws and authorities, Simoun reaches Father Florentino’s house on the Pacific coast.
29

Chapter XXXIX: The Fugitive's Poison and Confession

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX (39) · Pacific Coast · Father Florentino's House

Wounded and hunted, Simoun takes refuge in Father Florentino’s solitary stone house on the Pacific coast.

When a telegram arrives announcing that the Civil Guard will arrest Simoun at eight o’clock, Simoun drinks poison to avoid capture and public execution.

He calls Father Florentino to his bedside and confesses his entire identity as Crisóstomo Ibarra, recounting his thirteen years of exile and vengeance.

Simoun’s Agonized Question

Why Has God Abandoned Me?

Simoun asks why God struck down his revolution while allowing the corrupt Spanish rulers and friars to flourish:

“Does God condone the crimes of the oppressors? Why should He refuse liberty to an oppressed people?”

What happens next: Father Florentino delivers Rizal’s supreme theological and moral response.
30

Father Florentino's Dialogue: Liberty, Virtue, and the Youth

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX (39) · Theological Resolution

Father Florentino gently explains that God will not bless freedom achieved through corruption, bribery, and terror:

“Pure and spotless must the victim be that the sacrifice may be acceptable! What is the use of independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?” Father Florentino · Chapter XXXIX
Plain-English Meaning: A nation that achieves freedom through murder and deceit will only create a new generation of home-grown tyrants. True independence requires moral virtue, education, and labor.

Comparative Analysis: Noli Me Tángere vs. El Filibusterismo

Unit 9 · Literary Analysis · Berlin 1887 vs. Ghent 1891
Dimension Noli Me Tángere (1887) El Filibusterismo (1891)
Core Mood Romantic, idealistic, satirical, warm sunlight. Dark, cynical, tragic, nocturnal, urgent.
Protagonist Crisóstomo Ibarra: hopeful educator and builder. Simoun: disguised jeweler and revolutionary provocateur.
View on Spain Believes lawful assimilation and reform are possible. Concludes Spanish colonial rule is irredeemably corrupt.
Primary Conflict Enlightenment education vs. religious obscurantism. Radical subversion vs. moral and spiritual redemption.
The Climax Persecution and escape across Laguna de Bay. The defused wedding lamp and suicide confession.
Takeaway: Noli diagnoses the disease; Fili exposes the failure of violent retaliation without moral foundation.

The 1896 Revolution and Republic Act No. 1425

Unit 9 · Historical Legacy · 1896 & 1956 · Philippine History
1896 Philippine Revolution

The Spark of Katipunan

Andrés Bonifacio and the Katipunan adopted “Rizal” as a password, citing *El Filibusterismo* as proof that Spanish rule could never be reformed peacefully.

Simoun’s failure also provided a crucial warning: a revolution built on private vengeance will collapse unless guided by civic virtue.

Republic Act No. 1425 (1956)

The Rizal Law

Sponsored by Senator Claro M. Recto to mandate the study of Rizal's life, works, and writings across Philippine educational institutions.

Legal Nuance: The law mandates unexpurgated collegiate reading of both novels, while secondary schools keep copies in school libraries.

Civic Impact: The novel remains a living document challenging each new generation to examine its civic responsibilities.
