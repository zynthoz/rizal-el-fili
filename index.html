PROLOGUE

Begun in Calamba in October 1887; completed in Biarritz on March 29, 1891; printed in Ghent on September 18, 1891.

Thirteen years after his disappearance, Crisóstomo Ibarra returns.

A wealthy jeweler. A trusted adviser to the Captain-General. A man who moves freely between the powerful and the oppressed.

But Ibarra is no longer seeking justice through education and reform. He has returned as Simoun, carrying a plan that could bring an empire to its knees—or bury an entire country in blood.

I · The Title

Rizal to Ferdinand Blumentritt, 29 March 1891.

The Word Filibustero

In the seventeenth century, a filibustero was a Caribbean pirate. By the late nineteenth century in the Philippines, Spanish colonial authorities and friars used the word for a different kind of threat: any native or ilustrado suspected of subverting Spanish rule or imagining separation from Madrid.

Writing to his Austrian friend Ferdinand Blumentritt in 1887, Rizal explained that the educated class feared the word like a death sentence: “It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged, or a presumptuous man.” While Charles Derbyshire’s classic 1912 English edition popularized the interpretive title The Reign of Greed—capturing the systemic avarice depicted across the novel—the Spanish title El Filibusterismo more literally signifies The Subversion or The Practice of the Subversive.

17 February 1872

Three secular priests. One garrote at Bagumbayan. A memory that outlived the empire.

“The Church, by refusing to degrade you, has placed in doubt the crime that has been imputed to you; the Government, by surrounding your trials with mystery and shadows, causes the belief that there was some error, committed in fatal moments; and all the Philippines, by worshiping your memory and calling you martyrs, in no sense recognizes your culpability.” — José Rizal, Dedication to Gomburza (1891)

Following the 1872 Cavite Mutiny, colonial authorities executed the Filipino secular priests Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora. Working from memory in Europe without archival records, Rizal misremembered both the date of their execution and their ages—a telling detail showing that what endured for him was not a colonial dossier, but the boyhood trauma of 1872:

1887 – 1891

Calamba, Madrid, Biarritz, and the F. Meyer-Van Loo Press in Ghent.

The Years Between Noli and Fili

Why did the tone of Rizal’s writing darken so sharply between 1887 and 1891? The years of composition coincided with upheaval both in Laguna and among the Filipino colony in Spain:

01

The Calamba Agrarian Dispute (1887–1891)

While Rizal was writing the novel, tenant farmers in Calamba—including Rizal’s own family—contested the escalating land rentals and expanding estate boundaries of the Dominican hacienda. In 1890–1891, Governor-General Valeriano Weyler sent troops to enforce evictions, demolish houses, and exile Rizal’s brother Paciano and brothers-in-law—events strongly echoed in the novel’s account of Cabesang Tales.

02

Fractures in Madrid (1890–1891)

In Madrid, bitter disagreements between Rizal and Marcelo H. del Pilar over the leadership of the Filipino expatriate community and the direction of La Solidaridad left Rizal disillusioned with peaceful lobbying before Spanish politicians. He concluded that the real struggle lay back inside the Philippines, not in Madrid salons.

03

Loss of Leonor Rivera (1890–1891)

In late 1890 in Madrid, Rizal received Leonor Rivera’s letter announcing that she was set to marry British railway engineer Henry Kipping (their wedding took place in Dagupan on June 17, 1891, after the manuscript was finished in Biarritz). Readers have long noted the biographical parallel between Rizal’s loss and Isagani losing Paulita Gómez to Juanito Pelaez.

Halted on the Press in Ghent

Rizal completed the manuscript in Biarritz on March 29, 1891, and moved to Ghent, Belgium, where printing costs at the F. Meyer-Van Loo Press were lower than in Brussels. Living in a cramped room with José Alejandrino, Rizal pawned his jewels and rationed his meals. By August 6, 1891, he wrote to Basa that printing had stopped at page 112 because his funds were exhausted: “Sometimes I feel like burning my manuscript.” Learning of his predicament, Valentin Ventura sent money from Paris to pay the printer. The book came off the press on September 18, 1891.

ACT I Chapters 1 – 10

Por encima y por debajo de cubierta · Up and down the decks on the Pasig River.

Aboard the Bapor Tabo

One morning in December, the steamer Tabo labors up the winding Pasig River toward Laguna—carrying friars, bureaucrats, and Doña Victorina under the awnings above, and students, merchants, and peasants beside the furnace below.

Chapters 1 – 2

Satire and conversation on the river.

Ducks, Canals, and Cold Beer

Before the novel turns dark, it is bitingly funny. On the shaded upper deck, Doña Victorina—dyed curls, European gowns, and all—complains that the river would move faster if there were no indios on board, while ignoring the Filipino helmsman steering the boat. Beside her, the journalist Ben Zayb (whose name is an anagram of Ybañez), Don Custodio, and the friars argue over how to straighten the shallow Pasig River.

Simoun, the tall jeweler in blue-tinted spectacles who speaks with a strange mix of accents, cuts into their polite chatter with a calm proposal: dig a straight canal from Manila to Laguna by forcing every town’s inhabitants—men, women, and children—to work for months without pay, bringing their own food and tools. When Don Custodio protests that forced labor has caused revolts in the past, Simoun replies without blinking:

“Wait! Was that how the pyramids of Egypt were built, or the Colosseum in Rome? Were there revolts? No—and the dead remain dead, while posterity bows before the work!” — Simoun on the Upper Deck, Chapter 1

Minutes later, descending to the stifling lower deck where Basilio and Isagani sit among cargo crates, Simoun offers them beer. When Basilio apologizes that they do not drink alcohol and Simoun jokes that Father Camorra claims Filipinos lack energy because they drink too much water, Isagani answers quietly:

“Tell Father Camorra that if he drank water instead of wine or beer, perhaps we might all be better off and fewer rumors would circulate… Water is sweet and can be drunk, though it drowns out wine and beer and puts out fire; and heated, it becomes steam; and ruffled, it is the ocean that once destroyed humanity and made the earth tremble to its foundations!” — Isagani to Simoun, Chapter 2 (Debajo de cubierta)

Simoun

Crisóstomo Ibarra thirteen years after his disappearance on the lake. A jeweler who sells diamonds to the ruling class while financing their destruction.

Chapters 4, 8 – 10

Cabesang Tales · Riqueza y miseria (Wealth and Want).

The Clearing at Tiani

Tales, son of the old woodcarver Tandang Selo, had worked as a sharecropper until he saved enough for two carabaos and a few hundred pesos. With his wife, his son Tano, and his daughters Lucia and Juli, he cleared a stretch of thick forest on the edge of town that he believed belonged to no one. While breaking the soil, fever struck the camp; Tales buried his wife and his eldest daughter Lucia in the very fields they had cleared.

When the first harvest ripened, a neighboring religious corporation claimed the land as part of its boundaries, demanding twenty or thirty pesos a year. Old Tandang Selo urged patience: “Pretend that the thirty pesos were swallowed by a crocodile.” Tales paid. The next year the friars raised the rent to fifty pesos. Tales paid again, became village tax-collector (Cabesang Tales), and covered out of his own pocket the taxes of neighbors who had died or moved away.

When the rent was raised to two hundred pesos and the friar administrator told him that if he could not pay, another man would be given his land, Tales rebelled. He demanded to see a single title deed; the friars had none, yet the judges in the provincial capital ruled against him because no judge dared risk his post by ruling against the corporation.

Tales patrolled his boundaries first with a shotgun, then—when the government banned firearms—with a long bolo, and finally with an axe. Captured by bandits who knew he had money saved for his appeal, he was held for a 500-peso ransom. His son Tano had already been conscripted into the colonial army because Tales refused to buy his exemption. To raise the ransom, Juli sold her jewelry—all except a diamond-and-emerald locket that had once belonged to María Clara—and borrowed 250 pesos from the pious Hermana Penchang on condition that she work in Penchang’s house as a servant starting Christmas morning. That night, old Tandang Selo lost his voice forever.

In Chapter 10, Simoun lodges at Tales’ house to display trays of diamonds, rubies, and sapphires to the wealthy families of Tiani. Tales watches from the corner—evicted from his land, his house ordered vacated within three days, his daughter a servant, his father mute. The next morning, Simoun’s leather holster is empty. In its place lies María Clara’s locket and a note from Tales asking forgiveness for taking the revolver to join the bandits. Outside town, three bodies are found in Tales’ stolen fields: the friar administrator and the new tenant, their mouths stuffed with earth, and the tenant’s wife with her throat cut and a scrap of paper beside her written in blood: Tales.

Cabesang Tales

Son of Tandang Selo; father of Tano and Juli. A village tax-collector who loses his wife, his land, and his court case before taking to the hills as Matanglawin.

Juli

Daughter of Cabesang Tales. Basilio’s beloved. Forced into domestic servitude on Christmas Day to ransom her father, then driven to her death while trying to free Basilio.

Chapters 6 – 7

Christmas Eve beneath the balete tree in San Diego.

Two Men by a Grave

On Christmas Eve, Basilio slips into the old forest of the Ibarras to visit the unmarked grave of his mother, Sisa. Thirteen years earlier he had been an illiterate fugitive boy; since then, taken in by Captain Tiago, he had swept classrooms, endured the indifference of professors who never called on him, and risen to the top of his medical class—two months away from becoming a physician, planning to marry Juli and practice quietly in the provinces.

Hearing the crunch of gravel and seeing the beam of a lantern, Basilio peers through the roots of the balete tree and sees the jeweler Simoun—without his blue spectacles—digging with a spade. Basilio steps out and offers to help the man who had dug his mother’s grave thirteen years before.

Simoun draws a revolver and levels it at him, then lowers the barrel. Instead of killing the only witness to his survival, he tries to win him over. When Basilio explains that he and his fellow students are petitioning for an academy to teach Castilian Spanish so that the Philippines and Spain may become a single nation with equal rights, Simoun answers with the longest political speech in the novel:

“Spanish will never be the general language of the country, the people will never talk it, because the conceptions of their brains and the beatings of their hearts do not have phrases in that idioma; each people has its own tongue, as it has its own way of feeling! What are you going to do with Castilian, the few of you who will speak it? Kill off your own originality, subordinate your thoughts to other brains, and instead of making yourselves free, make yourselves truly slaves!” — Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 7

Basilio answers plainly: he is not a politician. He studied medicine because physical sickness is the only thing he knows how to cure, and no amount of vengeance will bring back his mother’s hair or his brother’s smile. Simoun lets him walk back into the trees, telling him that when he changes his mind, Simoun’s house on the Escolta will be open.

Basilio

Orphaned son of Sisa and medical student under Captain Tiago’s patronage. Two months from graduation, he asks only to heal the sick and marry Juli.

ACT II Chapters 11 – 24

Los Baños, the University of Santo Tomas, the Quiapo Fair, and the Santa Clara cloister.

Cabinets of Glass, Petitions on Paper

Back in Manila, the novel follows the ordinary routines of colonial life—a physics lecture, a lawyer’s office, a merchant’s dinner party, a fairground sideshow, and an evening at the French operetta—until two pieces of news arrive on the same night.

Chapters 11 – 13

La clase de física · Father Millon and Placido Penitente.

The Instruments Behind the Glass

In Chapter 13, Placido Penitente—once considered the best Latinist in his hometown in Batangas—sits on a wooden bench among two hundred fifty students in the physics amphitheater of the University of Santo Tomas. Along the walls stand mahogany cabinets with glass doors housing brass pulleys, batteries, and Leyden jars. Once a year they are dusted so foreign visitors may admire the university’s resources; no student has ever laid a finger on them. Above the blackboard, a charcoal inscription left by a forgotten rebel still reads: ¡Viva!

The professor, Father Millon, does not lecture on physics; he quizzes students on whether a mirror of polished wood is technically a mirror of surface or substance, mimicking their pronunciation to make the class laugh. When he calls on Placido and marks him with fifteen absences for three days missed—calculating that each time he called the roll and Placido was not there counted as five—Placido asks how he can be marked absent and recite the lesson on the same day. Father Millon sneers: “¡Aba! Because you can be absent in spirit!”

After twenty minutes of public ridicule, Placido stands up, tosses his textbook onto the platform, and says in a voice trembling with rage:

“Enough, Father, enough! Your Reverence can put down all the absences you wish, but you have no right to insult me! Let Your Reverence keep your class—I cannot stand any more!” — Placido Penitente, Chapter 13

Walking out without a plan, Placido wanders the docks of Manila until he crosses paths with Simoun—who takes him that night to a hidden house on the outskirts of the city where a former schoolteacher is packing gunpowder into iron shells.

Chapters 14–15, 20

Un abogado · El ponente (The Arbitrator).

Señor Pasta’s Gray Hairs and Don Custodio’s Shelves

While Placido walks out, Isagani, Macaraig, and Sandoval try to win approval for their Academy of Castilian through legal channels. Isagani visits Señor Pasta, the wealthiest Filipino lawyer in Manila, who began life doing odd jobs for the friars as a poor student and now serves as their legal counsel.

Pasta listens with half-closed eyes as he writes, then advises Isagani like a father: put the academy out of his head, study how to draft lawsuits, marry a rich and devout girl, and leave the government alone. Touching his own thinning white hair, Pasta asks why any young man should sacrifice his future for thankless strangers. Isagani answers before leaving:

“When I have gray hairs like those, sir, and turn my gaze back over my past and see that I have worked only for myself, without having done what I plainly could and should have done for the country that has given me everything… every gray hair will be a thorn, and instead of rejoicing in them, I shall be ashamed of them!” — Isagani to Señor Pasta, Chapter 15

The students’ petition lands on the desk of Don Custodio de Salazar y Sánchez de Monteredondo (Chapter 20), whom Manila newspapers call “indefatigable” and “active.” Rizal devotes an entire satirical chapter to Custodio’s career: a Spaniard of modest birth who married a wealthy mestiza in Manila, traveled to Madrid where no one noticed him, and returned to the Philippines determined to reform everything from the ground up—proposing that Manila streets be paved with wooden planks held together by nails, and that convict laborers work only at night while chained by the ankles. Behind his desk stand hundreds of folders labeled Projects in Study, Projects in Preparation, and Projects Presented. When he cannot solve the student academy dilemma, he consults the high-kicking bailarina Pepay and Father Sibyla—producing a ruling that hands the school to the religious orders and turns the students into unpaid fee-collectors.

Isagani

A student poet raised by his uncle, Father Florentino, on the Pacific coast. Engaged to Paulita Gómez and chief spokesman for the student academy.

Chapters 16–18, 22–23

Las tribulaciones de un chino · La feria de Quiapo · Un cadáver.

The Sphinx at Quiapo and the Bell of Santa Clara

That same week, Simoun dines at the bazaar of the Chinese merchant Quiroga (Chapter 16), who owes him 9,000 pesos for three diamond bracelets Quiroga gave to a theater lady who was not his wife. Simoun offers to forgive two thousand pesos of the debt if Quiroga will store several crates of “fireworks and glassware” arriving on the next steamer—crates actually packed with rifles that Simoun plans to slip into the homes of wealthy citizens to trigger mass arrests.

After dinner, Father Salví, Father Camorra, and Ben Zayb stroll through the lantern-lit Quiapo Fair (Chapters 17–18) and enter the booth of the American showman Mr. Leeds. From a black wooden box containing a handful of dust from the Pyramid of Khufu, rises a cadaverous head with thick black hair named Imuthis. Fixing its sunken eyes directly on Father Salví, the head tells how, in the days of Pharaoh Amasis, he returned home from Greece and Babylon, loved the daughter of a priest, and was betrayed by a young priest of Abydos who forged his letters, had him shot down while fleeing across Lake Moeris, and pursued the girl into the island sanctuary of Isis. Hearing his own secret history with Ibarra and María Clara spoken aloud in the dark, Salví gasps: “¡Misericordia! ¡Vive aún!” (“Mercy! She still lives!”) and faints dead away.

Five nights later (Chapters 22–23), while Manila crowds the Teatro de Variedades for the French operetta and the students learn that Don Custodio has gutted their academy, Simoun comes to Basilio’s room at seven in the evening. Outside the city, Cabesang Tales’ men and disaffected regiments are waiting in the dark for Simoun’s first cannon shot at eight o’clock. Simoun gives Basilio one hour to choose sides—and reveals that Basilio’s specific task in the uprising is to lead a detachment to break down the doors of the Santa Clara convent and bring out a single person: María Clara.

Basilio looks at him in shock and says: “¡Ay! ¡Señor Simoun, llega usted demasiado tarde! ¡María Clara ha muerto!” (“You have come too late! María Clara is dead!”) She had died at six o’clock that afternoon; Basilio had just come from the convent where the bells were tolling the funeral knell, and hands Simoun a letter from Father Salví that Captain Tiago had wept over an hour before.

Simoun does not fire the cannon. He walks out of the house grasping his chest like a man struck by lightning, leaving his regiments waiting in the fields until dawn.

María Clara

Daughter of Pia Alba and Father Dámaso; cloistered inside the Convent of Santa Clara for thirteen years until her death on the night of Simoun’s first planned uprising.

ACT III Chapters 25 – 34

Risas y llantos · Pasquines · Juli · La última razón.

Posters on the Doors, Steps to the Convent

While Simoun lies sick in bed refusing to see anyone, a student dinner party turns into a conspiracy trial—and the two people who tried hardest to stay out of politics pay the highest price.

Chapters 25 – 30

The university gates in Manila and the parish convent in Tiani.

The Pasquinades and the Convent Window

On the night fourteen students mock Don Custodio’s ruling over bowls of noodles at the Pansiteria Macanista de Buen Gusto (Chapter 25), someone pastes seditious placards (pasquinades) on the doors of the university threatening bloodshed and rebellion (Chapter 26). Before noon the next day, the student leaders are rounded up.

Basilio was not at the noodle shop; he spent that morning at the San Juan de Dios Hospital tending his patients. When he walks to Macaraig’s boarding house merely to borrow money for his medical degree fees, a lieutenant of the Guardia Civil takes down his name and marches him to prison in chains alongside Macaraig, who laughs at the absurdity of it.

Within days, the wealthy students are out. Macaraig obtains a passport and sails for Europe; Isagani—after a candid philosophical conversation with the Jesuit Father Fernández on why students distrust their teachers (Chapter 27)—is freed through his uncle Father Florentino’s intercession. Only Basilio stays behind bars. Worse, Captain Tiago dies in terror after Father Irene fills his head with tales of firing squads; as executor, Irene announces that Tiago’s estate has gone to the convent of Santa Clara, the Pope, the Archbishop, and the religious corporations, with even Basilio’s modest twenty-five-peso student bequest revoked—leaving him in prison without a centavo or a lawyer.

In Tiani (Chapter 30), word arrives that Basilio is to be deported to the Carolines—and rumor adds that prisoners rarely survive the voyage. For days Juli refuses to go near the parish convent; she knows what Father Camorra—“the Horse,” as the townsfolk call him—had demanded when she thanked him for helping her grandfather Tandang Selo. Night after night she wakes screaming from dreams of her father wandering the hills and Basilio drowning in the dark.

When the town judge and Sister Bali tell her that Father Camorra is the only man who can get Basilio out, Juli walks to the convent at dusk with Sister Bali, stops on the stairs weeping, and has to be pushed through the doorway as the doors close behind her. Later that night, the town hears a woman’s screams and a dull thud on the stone courtyard: Juli has thrown herself from the convent window. At dawn, mute old Tandang Selo knocks at the doors of the judge, the gobernadorcillo, and the convent; finding them all bolted, he takes his bamboo hunting spear and walks into the forest.

Chapters 33 – 34

Simoun’s house on the Escolta, two months later.

The Flask of Tears

Two months later, on the afternoon before the Captain-General sails back to Spain, Simoun buys Basilio’s release. When the servant shows Basilio into Simoun’s study, even the jeweler is startled: the quiet medical student’s eyes are sunken and bloodshot, his hair unkempt, his voice flat.

“Señor Simoun,” Basilio says, “four months ago you talked to me in the woods of your plans. I refused then. I was wrong; you were right. Three months and a half ago the revolution was about to break out, and I did not want to take part either; the movement failed, and the payment for my conduct has been imprisonment, the death of my fiancée! Now I have come to tell you: give me a weapon and let the revolution come!”

Simoun locks the door, leads Basilio into his inner laboratory, and lifts from a velvet-lined Russian leather case a heavy lamp shaped like a pomegranate. Unscrewing the burner, he exposes an iron chamber containing two liters of a clear yellowish liquid. Basilio steps back and whispers: “Nitroglycerin!”

“Yes, nitroglycerin! And it is also something more than nitroglycerin: it is concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, and injustices! It is the supreme argument of the weak—force against force, violence against violence!” — Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 33

That evening at nine o’clock, Juanito Pelaez and Paulita Gómez—who broke off with Isagani after his arrest, deciding that a girl of sense could not marry a provincial poet who courted danger—are holding their wedding banquet at Captain Tiago’s former mansion on Calle Anloague. Simoun’s agents have already hidden sacks of gunpowder in the ceilings and beneath the floorboards. Simoun will bring the pomegranate lamp as his wedding gift and set it in the middle of the dining kiosk where the Captain-General and the highest officials sit. Twenty minutes later the light will begin to fade; when someone raises the wick, a cap of mercury fulminate will detonate the house—and not a single guest inside will leave alive.

ACT IV Chapters 35 – 39

La fiesta · Fatalidad · Conclusión.

The Contradiction on the Staircase

At the height of the conspiracy, Simoun’s revolution is undone not by Spanish soldiers or friar spies, but by the most articulate patriot among the students—who saves the very regime that has ruined his friends.

Mane · Thecel · Phares

Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra

Chapter 35

Outside and inside the mansion on Calle Anloague.

Isagani at the Banquet

Standing on the sidewalk across from Captain Tiago’s old house, Basilio watches the carriages arrive and tries to keep his hatred sharp by thinking of Juli’s broken body in Tiani. Yet when he sees the newlyweds Paulita and Juanito step down in white silk, he feels a sudden pang for his friend Isagani.

Minutes later, Simoun comes down the stairs pale as wax, climbs into his carriage, and orders the driver to race for the Escolta. Knowing the twenty-minute countdown has begun, Basilio turns to hurry away—and collides with Isagani, who is standing in the street staring up at the illuminated windows where Paulita sits.

Basilio grabs his arm and begs him to come away. Isagani shakes his head without taking his eyes off Paulita’s silhouette: “Why should I go away? Tomorrow she will be another’s!” Desperate to save his friend’s life, Basilio forgets Simoun’s orders and blurts out the secret: “Do you want to die? That house is mined! That white light in the kiosk is nitroglycerin! In a few minutes the whole house will fly into the air!”

Upstairs in the dining kiosk, a strip of parchment written in red ink—Mane, Thecel, Phares · Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra—is being passed from hand to hand. Father Salví recognizes Ibarra’s handwriting and slumps back in his chair speechless with terror. At that exact moment, the lamp’s flame begins to sink. The Captain-General tells Father Irene to turn up the wick.

Before Irene’s fingers can touch the wheel, a tall young man bursts up the stairs like a shadow, knocks aside a servant, seizes the pomegranate lamp from the middle of the table, and leaps from the azotea rail—hurling the bomb into the Pasig River before diving into the current himself.

Here lies one of the sharpest ironies in the novel: Isagani—the student who defied Señor Pasta and indicted three centuries of colonial rule before Father Fernández—acts in the decisive instant not as a citizen, nor as a revolutionary, but as a lover who cannot bear to see Paulita die, even if saving her means saving the Captain-General, Father Salví, and Don Custodio along with her.

Chapter 38

Fatalidad · A mountain pass in the provinces.

Carolino’s Rifle

While Manila newspapers are forbidden by the Captain-General to print a single word about the wedding disturbance (Chapter 36), native Filipino Guardia Civil soldiers march a column of roped villagers along a scorching mountain road (Chapter 38). When a soldier named Mautang whips the bound prisoners with a rattan cane to make them walk faster, only a newly arrived private—nicknamed Carolino because he has just returned from military service in the Carolines—protests: “Leave them alone! It is enough that they are prisoners; they are men like ourselves!”

Carolino is Tano, the conscripted son of Cabesang Tales and brother of Juli, who still knows nothing of what has happened to his family during his years overseas.

Suddenly, rifle shots ring out from the high limestone rocks above the pass: Matanglawin’s outlaws are ambushing the patrol. Mautang falls dead with a bullet through his chest. The corporal points to an old man with white hair waving a bamboo lance on top of a boulder and shouts at Carolino: “Aim at that one, Carolino! Show what you can do, or I’ll put a bullet through you!”

Carolino raises his rifle and fires. When the soldiers scramble up the rocks to bayonet the survivors, Carolino walks toward the dying old man propped against the stone. The old man fixes his wide, dimming eyes on Carolino’s face, opens his mouth as if trying to speak a name that will not come out, and slowly raises a bony finger to point at the boy behind the rifle. Standing frozen over the body, Tano recognizes his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo.

Chapter 39

Dusk by the Pacific Ocean.

The Unresolved Question at the Edge of the Sea

Wounded by gunfire during his escape from Manila, Simoun reaches the seaside house of Isagani’s uncle, the native Filipino secular priest Father Florentino. When a telegram arrives from the local Guardia Civil lieutenant ordering the fugitive surrendered at eight that evening, Simoun refuses to flee and tells the priest he has already taken slow-acting poison: “Have no fear; I shall not fall alive into anyone’s hands. My secret will die with me!”

Wait—he adds—there is one man who deserves to know the truth. As the room darkens, Simoun confesses that thirteen years ago he was Crisóstomo Ibarra, and recounts how he returned from Cuba determined to fan colonial greed and injustice until the country exploded. Gripping the priest’s hand as the poison burns through him, Simoun asks the question that haunts the final pages of the book:

“Is it God’s will that these islands continue in the condition in which they suffer? Must the weak die so that the wicked may live? Why did that God deny me His aid?” — Simoun to Father Florentino, Chapter 39

Father Florentino answers that Simoun’s plot failed because he tried to cure a corrupted society by corrupting it further—fomenting crime, hatred, and avarice in the name of liberty:

“Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow? And no doubt they will be, because whoever submits to tyranny loves it! Señor Simoun, so long as our people are not prepared, so long as they enter the struggle deceived or driven, without a clear consciousness of what they are to do, the wisest attempts will fail, and better that they do fail!” — Father Florentino, Chapter 39

Simoun says nothing more; he presses the priest’s hand, sighs once, and dies in silence. Yet notice what Rizal does in this final scene: he does not give Simoun a speech of repentance, nor does Father Florentino’s sermon undo what happened to Cabesang Tales, to Juli, to Basilio, or to Tandang Selo. Florentino calls for patience, civic virtue, and willingness to suffer—yet the preceding thirty-eight chapters have just shown how every peaceful effort by Tales, Basilio, and the students was crushed by the very institutions they trusted.

When night falls, Father Florentino carries Simoun’s iron chest of diamonds and rubies out to the cliff above the Pacific Ocean and casts it into the waves, asking Nature to hide it in the abyss until men need it for a pure and holy purpose.

Father Florentino

A Filipino secular priest forced by his wealthy mother to take holy orders against his will, who retired to his coastal estate after the execution of Gomburza in 1872.

EPILOGUE 1891 – 1956

From Ghent and Hong Kong to the 1896 Revolution and Republic Act No. 1425.

If peaceful reform is denied, what remains?

Simoun’s revolution fails. His treasure disappears beneath the sea. The people he hoped to liberate remain under colonial rule. Yet the questions he leaves behind do not disappear.

Dimension Noli Me Tángere (Berlin, 1887) El Filibusterismo (Ghent, 1891)
Dedication & Tone Dedicated “To My Fatherland” (diagnosing a social cancer on the steps of the temple); satirical, romantic, and diagnostic. Dedicated to the martyred priests Gomburza (1872); dark, dialectical, and haunted by the limits of reform.
Protagonist’s Arc Crisóstomo Ibarra: Returns from Europe hoping to build a secular school in San Diego in partnership with Spanish authorities. Simoun: Returns from Cuba using wealth and bribery to worsen colonial abuses and detonate the ruling elite at a wedding feast.
Scope of Critique Focuses primarily on the abuses of the local friar orders (frailocracia) while appealing to Madrid’s conscience. Examines the entire colonial structure—Madrid-appointed governors, judges, friars, the press, and the complicity of educated Filipinos.
Structure 64 Chapters (plus the deleted chapter “Elias and Salome”); expansive portrait of town life in San Diego. 39 Chapters; concentrated around Manila, Tiani, and the ideological arguments between Simoun, Basilio, Isagani, and Florentino.
Afterlife of the Text

Documented circulation, revolutionary readers, and preservation.

I

Ghent to Hong Kong and Manila (1891–1892)

After sending complimentary copies to Blumentritt, Graciano López Jaena, and Valentin Ventura in September 1891, Rizal shipped the bulk of the Ghent edition in crates to Hong Kong addressed to José María Basa before sailing from Marseilles himself on October 18, 1891. While many copies smuggled into Manila were confiscated by Spanish customs and the Comisión Permanente de Censura, surviving copies circulated hand to hand—and possession of subversive leaflets and writings was cited when Governor-General Despujol deported Rizal to Dapitan in July 1892.

II

Readers in the Katipunan (1892–1896)

Historians have long noted the tension between Father Florentino’s warning against premature revolt and the way revolutionary readers received the novel. For Andrés Bonifacio—who kept copies of both Noli and Fili in his personal collection—and for members of the Katipunan founded the night Rizal’s deportation was announced in July 1892, the collapse of the students’ academy and the dispossession of Cabesang Tales dramatized a colony that could no longer be reformed from within.

III

The 1925 Manuscript & The 1956 Rizal Law

In 1925, the Philippine government purchased the original 279-page handwritten manuscript from Valentin Ventura for ₱10,000; preserved at the National Library, it survived the 1945 destruction of Manila and was recovered intact after a brief theft in December 1961. In 1956, following heated national debates between Senator Claro M. Recto, Senator Jose P. Laurel, and the Catholic hierarchy, Republic Act No. 1425 mandated the reading of unexpurgated editions of both novels across Philippine schools and universities.

Was Simoun wrong to seek revolution—or was the society that created him already beyond reform?

Rizal leaves the reader at the edge of the Pacific with no easy answer.
