PART I · PROLOGUE

Begun in Calamba in October 1887; completed in Biarritz on March 29, 1891; printed in Ghent on September 18, 1891.

Thirteen years after his disappearance, Crisóstomo Ibarra returns.

A wealthy jeweler. A trusted adviser to the Captain-General. A man who moves freely between the powerful and the oppressed.

But Ibarra is no longer seeking justice through education and reform. He has returned as Simoun, carrying a plan that could bring an empire to its knees—or bury an entire country in blood.

01

The Word Filibustero

OUTLINE I.A.1 · MEANING OF THE TITLE

In the seventeenth century, a filibustero was a Caribbean pirate. By the late nineteenth century in the Philippines, Spanish colonial authorities and friars used the word for a different kind of threat: any native or ilustrado suspected of subverting Spanish rule or imagining separation from Madrid.

Writing to his Austrian friend Ferdinand Blumentritt in 1887, Rizal explained that the educated class feared the word like a death sentence: “It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged, or a presumptuous man.” While Charles Derbyshire’s classic 1912 English edition popularized the interpretive title The Reign of Greed—capturing the systemic avarice depicted across the novel—the Spanish title El Filibusterismo more literally signifies The Subversion or The Practice of the Subversive.

ETYMOLOGICAL EVOLUTION
1. 17th Century · Antilles

From Dutch vrijbuiter (“freebooter”): a pirate raiding Spanish shipping in the Caribbean.

2. 1887 · Colonial Manila

“A dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged.” — José Rizal to Ferdinand Blumentritt.

3. 1912 · Derbyshire Edition

Translated into English as The Reign of Greed, turning the accusation onto the colonial state.

02

17 February 1872 · The Gomburza Dedication

OUTLINE I.A.2–3 · DEDICATION & ARCHIVAL CORRECTIONS
17 February 1872

Three secular priests. One garrote at Bagumbayan. A memory that outlived the empire.

“The Church, by refusing to degrade you, has placed in doubt the crime that has been imputed to you; the Government, by surrounding your trials with mystery and shadows, causes the belief that there was some error, committed in fatal moments; and all the Philippines, by worshiping your memory and calling you martyrs, in no sense recognizes your culpability.” — José Rizal, Dedication to Gomburza (1891)

Following the 1872 Cavite Mutiny, colonial authorities executed the Filipino secular priests Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora. Working from memory in Europe without archival records, Rizal misremembered both the date of their execution and their ages—a telling detail showing that what endured for him was not a colonial dossier, but the boyhood trauma of 1872:

03

Three Crises That Darkened the Sequel (1887–1891)

OUTLINE I.B.1–3 · AUTHORIAL MOTIVATION
I · Calamba (1887–1891)

The Calamba Agrarian Dispute

While Rizal was writing the novel, tenant farmers in Calamba—including Rizal’s own family—contested the escalating land rentals and expanding estate boundaries of the Dominican hacienda. In 1890–1891, Governor-General Valeriano Weyler sent troops to enforce evictions, demolish houses, and exile Rizal’s brother Paciano and brothers-in-law—events strongly echoed in Cabesang Tales.

II · Madrid (1890–1891)

Disillusionment with the Propaganda Movement

In Madrid, bitter disagreements between Rizal and Marcelo H. del Pilar over the leadership of the Filipino expatriate community and the direction of La Solidaridad left Rizal disillusioned with peaceful lobbying before Spanish politicians. He concluded that the real struggle lay back inside the Philippines, not in Madrid salons.

III · Dagupan (1890–1891)

The Loss of Leonor Rivera

In late 1890 in Madrid, Rizal received Leonor Rivera’s letter announcing that she was set to marry British railway engineer Henry Kipping (their wedding took place in Dagupan on June 17, 1891, after the manuscript was finished in Biarritz). Readers have long noted the biographical parallel with Isagani losing Paulita Gómez to Juanito Pelaez.

Step 1 · Oct 1887 – 1890

Calamba, London & Madrid

Begins drafting the sequel in Calamba in October 1887; revises and expands chapters across London (1888), Paris, Madrid, and Brussels.

Step 2 · 29 March 1891

Completed in Biarritz

Finishes the 39-chapter manuscript in Biarritz, France, and moves to Ghent, Belgium, for lower printing rates at the F. Meyer-Van Loo Press.

Step 3 · 6 August 1891

Stopped at Page 112

Living in poverty with José Alejandrino and pawning his jewels, Rizal runs out of funds. He writes to Basa: “Sometimes I feel like burning my manuscript.”

Step 4 · 18 Sept 1891

Valentin Ventura’s Rescue

Valentin Ventura sends funds from Paris to finish the printing on September 18, 1891. In gratitude, Rizal gifts Ventura the original handwritten manuscript.

05

Aboard the Bapor Tabo & The Return of Simoun

OUTLINE II.A.1–2 · CHAPTERS 1–2
1. UPPER DECK · POR ENCIMA DE CUBIERTA (CHAPTER 1)

Awnings, Canals, and Forced Labor

On the shaded upper deck, Doña Victorina complains that the river would move faster if there were no indios on board, while ignoring the Filipino helmsman steering the boat. Beside her, the journalist Ben Zayb (an anagram of Ybañez), Don Custodio, and the friars argue over how to straighten the shallow Pasig River.

Simoun, the tall jeweler in blue-tinted spectacles who holds the ear of the Captain-General, cuts in with a chilling proposal: dig a straight canal from Manila to Laguna by forcing every town’s inhabitants to work for months without pay. When Don Custodio protests that forced labor causes revolts, Simoun replies:

“Wait! Was that how the pyramids of Egypt were built, or the Colosseum in Rome? Were there revolts? No—and the dead remain dead, while posterity bows before the work!” — Simoun on the Upper Deck, Chapter 1
2. LOWER DECK · DEBAJO DE CUBIERTA (CHAPTER 2)

Cold Beer, Water, and Rising Steam

Descending to the stifling lower deck where Basilio and Isagani sit among cargo crates beside the furnace, Simoun offers them beer. When Basilio says they do not drink alcohol and Simoun jokes that Father Camorra claims Filipinos lack energy because they drink too much water, Isagani answers quietly:

“Tell Father Camorra that if he drank water instead of wine or beer, perhaps we might all be better off and fewer rumors would circulate… Water is sweet and can be drunk, though it drowns out wine and beer and puts out fire; and heated, it becomes steam; and ruffled, it is the ocean that once destroyed humanity and made the earth tremble to its foundations!” — Isagani to Simoun, Chapter 2 (Debajo de cubierta)

Simoun (Click for Profile)

Crisóstomo Ibarra thirteen years after his disappearance on the lake. A jeweler who sells diamonds to the ruling class while financing their destruction.

The Clearing at Tiani: Cabesang Tales & Juli

OUTLINE II.A.3 · CHAPTERS 4, 8–10
STEP-BY-STEP ESCALATION OF DISPOSSESSION
1. Clearing the Virgin Forest 2 Graves

Tales clears thick forest on the edge of town; his wife and eldest daughter Lucia die of fever before the first harvest.

2. First Harvest Claim 20 – 30 Pesos

Friar corporation claims the land. Tandang Selo urges patience: “Pretend that the thirty pesos were swallowed by a crocodile.”

3. Village Tax-Collector 50 Pesos

Rent is raised; made Cabesa, Tales covers from his own pocket the unpaid taxes of dead or absent neighbors.

4. The Court Appeal 200 Pesos

Tales demands to see a title deed; the friars have none, yet provincial judges rule against him.

5. Bandit Kidnapping 500 Pesos

Tano is conscripted; Juli sells her jewelry and borrows 250 pesos from Hermana Penchang to become her servant on Christmas Day.

6. Chapter 10 · Wealth and Want 1 Revolver

Leaving María Clara’s locket in Simoun’s holster, Tales takes the revolver and joins the outlaws as Matanglawin.

Tales, son of the old woodcarver Tandang Selo, cleared a stretch of forest with his family, burying his wife and eldest daughter Lucia in the very soil they broke. When the neighboring religious corporation raised the annual rent from thirty pesos to fifty, and finally to two hundred pesos, Tales demanded to see a single title deed. None existed, yet the courts ruled against him, and colonial authorities disarmed him of his shotgun and bolo.

When bandits kidnapped Tales for a 500-peso ransom after his son Tano was conscripted into the colonial army, his daughter Juli sold all her jewelry except a locket that had once belonged to María Clara, and bound herself as a domestic servant to the pious Hermana Penchang on Christmas Day. That night, old Tandang Selo lost his voice forever.

In Chapter 10, Simoun lodges at Tales’ house to display trays of diamonds and rubies to the wealthy families of Tiani. Evicted from his fields and ordered out of his house within three days, Tales swaps María Clara’s locket for Simoun’s revolver. Outside town, three bodies are found: the friar administrator and the new tenant with their mouths stuffed with earth, beside a scrap of paper written in blood: Tales.

Cabesang Tales

Son of Tandang Selo; father of Tano and Juli. Dispossessed farmer who becomes the guerrilla chief Matanglawin.

Juli

Daughter of Cabesang Tales and Basilio’s beloved. Enters servitude on Christmas Day to ransom her father.

07

Two Men by a Grave: Assimilation vs. Revolution

OUTLINE II.A.4 & IV.A.1 · CHAPTERS 6–7
1. SIMOUN’S CRITIQUE OF HISPANIZATION

The Trap of Castilian Spanish

On Christmas Eve, Basilio visits his mother Sisa’s grave beneath the balete tree in the Ibarra forest and discovers Simoun digging without his blue spectacles. Instead of shooting the only witness to his survival, Simoun tries to recruit him. When Basilio explains that the students are petitioning for an academy to teach Castilian Spanish so the Philippines and Spain may be united, Simoun answers:

“Spanish will never be the general language of the country, the people will never talk it, because the conceptions of their brains and the beatings of their hearts do not have phrases in that idioma; each people has its own tongue, as it has its own way of feeling! What are you going to do with Castilian, the few of you who will speak it? Kill off your own originality, subordinate your thoughts to other brains, and instead of making yourselves free, make yourselves truly slaves!” — Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 7
2. BASILIO’S CHOICE OF CLINICAL SCIENCE

Survival and Peaceful Reform

Taken in thirteen years earlier as an unpaid servant by Captain Tiago, Basilio had swept classrooms, endured professors who ignored him, and risen to his final year of medical school—two months away from becoming a physician and marrying Juli.

Standing over his mother’s grave, Basilio rejects Simoun’s conspiracy: he chose medicine because physical illness is the only thing he knows how to cure, and no amount of political vengeance will bring back his mother Sisa or his brother Crispin. Simoun lets him go, warning that when colonial justice teaches him otherwise, Simoun’s house on the Escolta will be open.

Basilio

Orphaned son of Sisa and graduating medical student under Captain Tiago’s patronage.

Los Baños, the University of Santo Tomas, the Castilian Academy, and the Santa Clara Cloister.

08

The Class in Physics: Instruments Behind Glass

OUTLINE II.B.1–2 & IV.B · CHAPTERS 11–13

While the Captain-General decides state petitions over card games at Los Baños (Chapter 11), Placido Penitente—once the best Latinist in Batangas—sits among two hundred fifty students in the physics amphitheater of the University of Santo Tomas (Chapter 13). Along the walls stand mahogany cabinets with glass doors housing brass pulleys, batteries, and Leyden jars. Once a year they are dusted so foreign visitors may admire the university’s resources; no student is ever allowed to touch them. Above the blackboard, a charcoal inscription left by a forgotten student still reads: ¡Viva!

The professor, Father Millon, quizzes students on medieval scholastic riddles rather than experimental science, mimicking their pronunciation to make the class laugh. Publicly humiliated over the attendance roll, Placido tosses his textbook onto the platform, walks out of the university, and that night accompanies Simoun to a hidden house where a former schoolteacher is packing gunpowder into iron shells.

FATHER MILLON’S ROLL BOOK (CH. 13)
15 Absences
1. Colonial Arithmetic

When Placido protests that he missed class only three times, Millon replies that since he checks the roll five times a lesson, three days missed equals fifteen absences.

2. “Absent in Spirit”

Asked how a student can be marked absent and recite the lesson on the same day, Millon sneers: “¡Aba! Because you can be absent in spirit!”

3. Placido’s Walkout

“Enough, Father, enough! Your Reverence can put down all the absences you wish, but you have no right to insult me!”

The Student Petition: Señor Pasta & Don Custodio

OUTLINE II.B.3 · CHAPTERS 14–15, 20–21
1. SEÑOR PASTA · THE COMPLICIT TECHNOCRAT (CH. 15)

Every Gray Hair a Thorn

Led by Isagani, Macaraig, and Sandoval, the students petition to open an Academy of Castilian. Isagani visits Señor Pasta, the wealthiest Filipino lawyer in Manila, who serves as legal counsel to the friars. Pasta advises him to abandon the academy, focus on making money, marry a rich girl, and leave the government alone—pointing to his own gray hair as proof of prudence. Isagani replies:

“When I have gray hairs like those, sir, and turn my gaze back over my past and see that I have worked only for myself, without having done what I plainly could and should have done for the country that has given me everything… every gray hair will be a thorn, and instead of rejoicing in them, I shall be ashamed of them!” — Isagani to Señor Pasta, Chapter 15
2. DON CUSTODIO · THE ARBITRATOR (CH. 20)

Folders of Imaginary Reform

The petition lands on the desk of Don Custodio de Salazar y Sánchez de Monteredondo (“Buena Tinta”), who once proposed paving Manila streets with nailed wooden planks and working convict laborers at night chained by the ankles.

Behind his desk stand hundreds of folders labeled Projects in Study, Projects in Preparation, and Projects Presented. After consulting the bailarina Pepay and Father Sibyla, Custodio approves the academy only under the strict supervision of the religious orders—turning the students into unpaid fee-collectors.

Isagani

Idealistic student poet raised by his uncle Father Florentino; chief spokesman for the academy.

Gun-Running, The Sphinx Imuthis & María Clara’s Death

OUTLINE II.B.4–5 · CHAPTERS 16–18, 22–23
I · Chapter 16

Quiroga’s 9,000-Peso Debt

Simoun dines at the bazaar of Chinese merchant Quiroga, who owes him 9,000 pesos for three diamond bracelets given to a theater lady. Simoun reduces the debt to 7,000 pesos on condition that Quiroga store smuggled crates of rifles disguised as fireworks and glassware.

II · Chapters 17–18

The Talking Head Imuthis

At the Quiapo Fair, American showman Mr. Leeds presents a sphinx head named Imuthis from the Pyramid of Khufu. Speaking directly in front of Father Salví, Imuthis tells how a priest of Abydos forged letters to frame him and pursue a maiden into the island temple of Philae—causing a terrified Salví to faint (“¡Misericordia! ¡Vive aún!”).

III · Chapters 22–23

6:00 PM at Santa Clara

On the night of the French operetta at the Teatro de Variedades, Simoun’s regiments wait outside Manila for his 8:00 PM cannon signal. At 7:00 PM, Simoun tells Basilio his task is to break into the Santa Clara convent and rescue María Clara—only for Basilio to reveal she died at 6:00 PM.

6:00 PM · María Clara Dies in the Cloister 7:00 PM · Simoun Arrives at Basilio’s Room 8:00 PM · The Cannon Signal Never Fires

“¡Ay! ¡Señor Simoun, llega usted demasiado tarde! ¡María Clara ha muerto!”

Learning of her death and reading Father Salví’s funeral notice, Simoun rushes out grasping his chest and abandons his waiting regiments—exposing how deeply his first revolution was driven by private obsession rather than national liberation.

While Simoun lies sick in bed, a satirical noodle-shop banquet becomes the pretext for mass arrests—and the two characters who tried hardest to stay out of politics pay the highest price.

11

The Pasquinades & Exit Captain Tiago

OUTLINE II.C.1–3 · CHAPTERS 25–29
1. THE BANQUET & SUBVERSIVE POSTERS (CH. 25–26)

Arrested Without a Warrant

On the night fourteen students mock Don Custodio’s ruling over bowls of noodles at the Pansiteria Macanista de Buen Gusto (Chapter 25), seditious placards (pasquinades) are found pasted on the doors of the university threatening rebellion (Chapter 26).

Basilio was not at the banquet; he spent that morning tending patients at the San Juan de Dios Hospital. When he walks to Macaraig’s boarding house merely to borrow money for his graduation fees, the Guardia Civil arrests him in chains alongside Macaraig.

2. FATHER FERNÁNDEZ & CAPTAIN TIAGO’S WILL (CH. 27–29)

Only the Poor Remain in Prison

After debating the Jesuit Father Fernández on how colonial schooling deliberately stunts Filipino minds (Chapter 27), Isagani is freed through his uncle’s intercession, and wealthy Macaraig sails for Europe. Only poor Basilio stays behind bars.

Worse, Captain Tiago dies in terror after Father Irene fills his head with tales of firing squads; as executor, Irene announces that Tiago’s estate has gone to Santa Clara and the religious orders, with even Basilio’s modest twenty-five-peso student bequest revoked.

12

Chapter 30 · Juli at the Tiani Convent

OUTLINE II.C.3 · CHAPTER 30
THE PARISH CONVENT IN TIANI

The Window at Dusk

In Tiani, word arrives that Basilio is the last student in prison and is to be deported to the Carolines—and rumor adds that prisoners rarely survive the voyage. For days Juli refuses to go near the parish convent; she knows what Father Camorra—“the Horse,” as the townsfolk call him—had demanded when she thanked him for helping her grandfather Tandang Selo.

When the town judge and Sister Bali tell her that Father Camorra is the only man who can save Basilio, Juli walks to the convent at dusk with Sister Bali, stops on the stairs weeping, and has to be pushed through the doorway as the doors close behind her.

Later that night, the town hears a woman’s screams and a dull thud on the stone courtyard: Juli has thrown herself from the convent window.

At dawn, mute old Tandang Selo knocks at the doors of the judge, the gobernadorcillo, and the convent; finding them all bolted, he takes his bamboo hunting spear and walks into the forest.

SIMOUN’S LABORATORY ON THE ESCOLTA

The Flask of Tears

Two months later, Simoun buys Basilio’s release from prison. Learning of Juli’s death and Tandang Selo’s disappearance, a hollowed-out Basilio walks into Simoun’s study: “Four months ago you talked to me in the woods of your plans. I refused then. I was wrong; you were right… The payment for my conduct has been imprisonment, the death of my fiancée! Now I have come to tell you: give me a weapon and let the revolution come!”

Simoun leads him into his laboratory and opens a Russian leather case holding a lamp shaped like a pomegranate. Unscrewing the burner, he exposes an iron chamber holding two liters of yellowish liquid. Basilio whispers: “Nitroglycerin!”

“Yes, nitroglycerin! And it is also something more than nitroglycerin: it is concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, and injustices! It is the supreme argument of the weak—force against force, violence against violence!” — Simoun to Basilio, Chapter 33

That night at nine o’clock, Paulita Gómez—who abandoned Isagani after his arrest—and Juanito Pelaez hold their wedding banquet at Captain Tiago’s former mansion on Calle Anloague, with sacks of gunpowder hidden under the floors. Twenty minutes after Simoun sets the lamp on the dining table, the flame will sink; when someone raises the wick, the house and the entire colonial elite will be blown into the air.

At the height of the conspiracy, Simoun’s revolution is undone not by Spanish soldiers or friar spies, but by the most articulate patriot among the students—who saves the very regime that has ruined his friends.

Mane, Thecel, Phares & Isagani’s Leap

OUTLINE II.D.1–2 · CHAPTER 35

Mane · Thecel · Phares

Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra

1. OUTSIDE ON CALLE ANLOAGUE

Basilio’s Warning on the Sidewalk

Standing across the street from Captain Tiago’s old mansion, Basilio watches Simoun place the lamp, descend the stairs pale as wax, and order his carriage to race for the Escolta. Knowing the twenty-minute countdown has begun, Basilio turns to flee—and collides with Isagani, who has come for one last look at Paulita’s silhouette in the window.

When Isagani refuses to leave (“Why should I go away? Tomorrow she will be another’s!”), Basilio forgets Simoun’s orders out of friendship and blurts out the secret: “Do you want to die? That house is mined! That white light in the kiosk is nitroglycerin!”

2. UPSTAIRS IN THE DINING KIOSK

The Lamp Hurl into the Pasig

Upstairs, a red-ink parchment bearing the biblical warning Mane, Thecel, Phares · Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra (“Measured, Weighed, Found Wanting”) leaves Father Salví speechless with terror. As the lamp’s flame sinks, the Captain-General tells Father Irene to turn up the wick.

Before Irene can touch the wheel, Isagani bursts up the stairs, seizes the pomegranate lamp, and leaps from the azotea—hurling the bomb into the Pasig River. In the decisive instant, the novel’s most eloquent student patriot acts not as a revolutionary, but as a lover who cannot bear to let Paulita die, saving the colonial elite along with her.

Chapters 36–38 · Press Censorship & Carolino’s Rifle

OUTLINE II.D.3 · CHAPTERS 36–38
A MOUNTAIN PASS IN THE COUNTRYSIDE (CHAPTER 38)

Tano and Tandang Selo

While the Captain-General censors Ben Zayb’s grandiose newspaper article to conceal the wedding attack (Chapter 36), native Filipino Guardia Civil soldiers march a column of roped suspects along a scorching mountain road (Chapter 38). When a soldier named Mautang whips the bound prisoners, only a young private—nicknamed Carolino after returning from military service in the Carolines—protests: “Leave them alone! It is enough that they are prisoners; they are men like ourselves!” Carolino is Tano, the conscripted son of Cabesang Tales and brother of Juli.

Ambushed from the high limestone cliffs by Matanglawin’s outlaws, the corporal points to a white-haired old man waving a bamboo lance on a boulder and orders Carolino at gunpoint: “Aim at that one, Carolino! Show what you can do, or I’ll put a bullet through you!”

Carolino fires. Climbing the rocks, he finds the dying old man fixing wide, dimming eyes on his face and slowly raising a bony finger to point at the boy behind the rifle: Tano has shot his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo.

16

Chapter 39 · Simoun’s Confession & Father Florentino’s Verdict

OUTLINE II.D.4 & IV.A.2 · CHAPTER 39
1. SIMOUN’S CONFESSION BEFORE EIGHT O’CLOCK

The Fugitive’s Question

Wounded by gunfire during his escape from Manila, Simoun takes refuge at the Pacific coastal home of Isagani’s uncle, the native Filipino secular priest Father Florentino, and swallows slow-acting poison so he will not be taken alive at eight o’clock.

Revealing that thirteen years ago he was Crisóstomo Ibarra, Simoun asks why God thwarted his plans to liberate the country:

“Is it God’s will that these islands continue in the condition in which they suffer? Must the weak die so that the wicked may live? Why did that God deny me His aid?” — Simoun to Father Florentino, Chapter 39
2. FATHER FLORENTINO’S ETHICAL THESIS

Slaves of Today, Tyrants of Tomorrow

Florentino answers that Simoun’s movement failed because he tried to redeem a corrupted society through bribery, hatred, and crime rather than civic virtue:

“Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow? And no doubt they will be, because whoever submits to tyranny loves it! Señor Simoun, so long as our people are not prepared, so long as they enter the struggle deceived or driven, without a clear consciousness of what they are to do, the wisest attempts will fail, and better that they do fail!” — Father Florentino, Chapter 39

Simoun presses the priest’s hand and dies in silence without a speech of repentance. At nightfall, Florentino carries Simoun’s iron chest of jewels to the cliff and casts it into the Pacific Ocean, praying that Nature guard it in the abyss until humanity needs it for a pure and holy purpose.

Father Florentino

Dignified native Filipino secular priest who retired to his Pacific estate after the 1872 execution of Gomburza.

PARTS V & VI · EPILOGUE 1887 – 1956

Structural Comparison (Noli vs. Fili) and Historical Legacy.

If peaceful reform is denied, what remains?

Simoun’s revolution fails and his treasure disappears beneath the sea, yet the novel’s indictment of colonial rule helped ignite the 1896 Philippine Revolution within five years of its printing.

Noli Me Tángere (1887) vs. El Filibusterismo (1891)

OUTLINE V · STRUCTURAL COMPARISON
Dimension Noli Me Tángere (Berlin, 1887) El Filibusterismo (Ghent, 1891)
Primary Tone & Genre Dedicated “To My Fatherland” (“A Social Cancer” / Work of the Heart); romantic, satirical, emotional, and diagnostic. Dedicated to the martyred priests Gomburza (1872) (“The Reign of Greed” / Work of the Head); political, dark, and dialectical.
Protagonist’s Evolution Crisóstomo Ibarra: Trusting, idealistic reformist who returns from Europe hoping to build a secular school in cooperation with Spain. Simoun: Disillusioned conspirator who returns from Cuba using bribery and accelerationism to detonate the ruling elite.
Target of Critique Primarily exposes the abuses of the local friar orders (frailocracia) while holding out hope that Madrid will intervene. Condemns the entire colonial apparatus—Madrid-appointed governors, judges, friars, the press, and the complicity of educated Filipinos.
Length & Structure 64 Chapters (plus the deleted chapter “Elias and Salome”); expansive portrait of town life in San Diego. 39 Chapters; concentrated around Manila, Tiani, and the ideological arguments between Simoun, Basilio, Isagani, and Florentino.

Historical Impact, Reception & Legacy (1891–1956)

OUTLINE VI.A–C · RECEPTION, KATIPUNAN & RIZAL LAW
I · Smuggling & Exile (1891–1892)

Ghent to Hong Kong and Dapitan

Rizal shipped most of the Ghent edition in crates to Hong Kong addressed to José María Basa before sailing from Marseilles himself on October 18, 1891. Spanish customs and the Comisión Permanente de Censura seized smuggled copies in Manila, and possession of subversive writings was cited when Governor-General Despujol deported Rizal to Dapitan in July 1892.

II · The 1896 Revolution

Andrés Bonifacio & The Katipunan

While Father Florentino warned against premature revolt, revolutionary leaders read the novel as proof that peaceful reform was dead. For Andrés Bonifacio—who kept copies of both Noli and Fili—and the Katipunan founded the night Rizal’s deportation was announced in July 1892, the failure of the student academy and the resistance of Cabesang Tales pointed toward separation from Spain.

III · Preservation & RA 1425

The 1925 Manuscript & 1956 Rizal Law

In 1925, the Philippine government purchased the 279-page manuscript from Valentin Ventura for ₱10,000; preserved at the National Library, it survived World War II and was recovered after a brief theft in December 1961. On June 12, 1956, Republic Act No. 1425 (authored by Sen. Claro M. Recto and sponsored by Sen. Jose P. Laurel) mandated the reading of unexpurgated editions across Philippine schools and universities.

Was Simoun wrong to seek revolution—or was the society that created him already beyond reform?
