Thirteen Years After Noli Me Tángere

José Rizal’s first novel, Noli Me Tángere (1887), closed in catastrophe: the idealistic young reformist Crisóstomo Ibarra saw his school project destroyed, was framed in a fabricated uprising, and vanished under gunfire on Laguna de Bay after the boatman Elías drew away his pursuers. Believing Ibarra dead, María Clara entered the cloister of Santa Clara, while the orphaned boy Basilio buried his mother Sisa and burned Elías’s body in the San Diego woods. El Filibusterismo opens thirteen years later in a colony where those unresolved wounds have deepened into a nationwide crisis.

1. Established in the Novel

Chronological plot events, character actions, motivations, and verbatim quotations from Chapters I–XXXIX are presented strictly in the order Rizal reveals them—without premature spoilers.

2. Literary Interpretation

Analytical commentary on Rizal’s symbolism, irony, and structural parallels is placed inside separate What This Reveals callouts rather than mixed into plot summaries.

3. Historical Context

Documented nineteenth-century Philippine history (Gomburza, the Calamba Hacienda evictions, Madrid reform politics, and the 1891 Ghent printing) is explicitly distinguished from fictional events.

01

Meaning of El Filibusterismo and Historical Timeline (1872–1891)

Historical Context · Etymology, Gomburza, Calamba & Ghent

In the seventeenth century, the Spanish word filibustero (from the Dutch vrijbuiter, “freebooter”) referred to pirates raiding shipping in the Caribbean Sea. By the late nineteenth century in the Philippines, Spanish officials and friars used the word to brand any native Filipino, secular priest, or educated ilustrado suspected of questioning clerical authority or seeking separation from Spain.

Writing to his Austrian friend Ferdinand Blumentritt on March 29, 1887, Rizal explained the fear the accusation provoked in Manila: “The word filibustero is still very little known in the Philippines; the common people do not know it yet. I heard it for the first time in 1872 when the tragic executions took place... It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged, or a presumptuous man.” While the Spanish title translates literally as The Subversion, Charles Derbyshire’s 1912 English edition popularized the thematic title The Reign of Greed.

Execution of the Gomburza Secular Priests

After the Cavite Arsenal Mutiny, colonial authorities executed three Filipino secular priests by garrote: Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora. Rizal was eleven years old, and his elder brother Paciano had lived in Father Burgos’s household. Nineteen years later, Rizal dedicated El Filibusterismo to their memory.

“The Church, by refusing to degrade you, has placed in doubt the crime that has been imputed to you; the Government, by surrounding your trials with mystery and shadows, causes the belief that there was some error, committed in fatal moments; and all the Philippines, by worshiping your memory and calling you martyrs, in no sense recognizes your culpability.”

José Rizal · Author’s Dedication, Ghent, 1891 (Derbyshire Trans.)
1887 – 1891 Calamba, Laguna

The Calamba Hacienda Land Dispute and Evictions

Rizal began drafting his second novel in Calamba in October 1887, just as Dominican estate managers raised land rents and threatened tenant farmers who petitioned the government for a tax audit. After courts ruled in the friars’ favor, Governor-General Valeriano Weyler dispatched artillery detachments to Calamba (1890–1891), demolishing homes and deporting Rizal’s brother Paciano and brothers-in-law—real events Rizal later invokes directly at the close of Chapter 10.

1890 – Sep 1891 Madrid · Biarritz · Ghent

Break with Madrid Assimilationism and Printing in Ghent

In late 1890 in Madrid, Rizal broke politically with Marcelo H. del Pilar over the direction of the Filipino colony and La Solidaridad, doubting that lobbying Spanish politicians in Europe could win genuine reform; that same winter, Leonor Rivera wrote that she was marrying the English engineer Charles Henry Kipping. Finishing the manuscript in Biarritz on March 29, 1891, Rizal moved to Ghent for cheaper printing, pawned his jewelry when F. Meyer-Van Loo Press halted work on August 6, and released the book on September 18, 1891 with financial aid from Valentin Ventura, to whom he sent the signed original manuscript.

Act I · Chapters I – X December · Pasig River & Tiani

Chapters 1–3 follow the steamer Tabo upstream toward Laguna de Bay; Chapters 4–10 unfold in neighboring Tiani and San Diego from Christmas Eve through the days after Christmas.

The River Steamer, the Forest Grave, and the Dispossessed Farmer

Rizal opens El Filibusterismo on a December morning aboard a slow river steamer struggling up the winding Pasig toward Laguna. Readers first encounter colonial officials, friars, university students, and a mysterious, sharp-tongued jeweler named Simoun—whose true identity and motives remain concealed until a midnight encounter in the San Diego forest in Chapter 7.

02

Aboard the Steamer Tabo: Hierarchy, Students, and River Legends

Chapters I – III · Strict Chronological Sequence
Setting & Figures (Ch. I)

Shaded upper deck under canvas awnings: Doña Victorina (hunting her runaway lame husband Don Tiburcio), journalist Ben-Zayb (anagram of Ybañez), Franciscans and Dominicans Padre Salví, Padre Sibyla, Padre Camorra, and Padre Irene, official Don Custodio, and jeweler Simoun.

How Simoun Appears Here

Known only as an influential “American mulatto” jeweler dubbed the “Brown Cardinal” and “Black Eminence” of the Captain-General, wearing tall-crowned hat and large blue-tinted goggles that completely hide his eyes.

Chapter I: Forced Labor to Straighten the Pasig

As the steamer scrapes over sandbars, Ben-Zayb, the friars, and Don Custodio argue pompously over how to improve navigation on the Pasig River. Custodio prepares to unveil his pet scheme—forcing riverside towns to raise ducks so the birds will eat snails and deepen the riverbed—when the jeweler Simoun cuts through their chatter with a brutal proposal: dig a straight new canal directly through Manila, close the old Pasig, and pay zero wages.

If convicts are insufficient, Simoun proposes forcing villagers, old men, youths, and boys to work three to five months instead of the fifteen-day obligatory labor (polo), bringing their own food and tools. When startled Don Custodio warns that such cruelty would provoke uprisings and Padre Sibyla recalls past timber revolts, Simoun coldly invokes ancient despotism before descending below deck:

“Don’t fool yourself, Don Custodio, only in this way are great enterprises carried out with small means. Thus were constructed the Pyramids, Lake Moeris, and the Colosseum in Rome... Don’t fool yourself—the dead remain dead, and might only is considered right by posterity.” Simoun · Chapter I, On the Upper Deck
Setting & Figures (Ch. II)

Suffocating lower hull beside roaring boilers, trunks, and market baskets: graduating medical student Basilio, his poet friend Isagani, rich townsman Capitan Basilio, Chinese peddlers, and retired Filipino priest Padre Florentino.

New Plot Thread Introduced

The university students’ lawful petition to establish an Academy for Instruction in Castilian—funded by wealthy student Makaraig and purportedly backed by Padre Irene with gifts of two bay horses.

Chapter II: Water, Fire, and Father Florentino

Below deck, Capitan Basilio tells young Basilio and Isagani that their proposed Castilian Academy will never pass because Padre Sibyla opposes it, though the students point out that they have already secured promises through Padre Irene and offer professors and classrooms at their own cost. Basilio and Isagani also speak lightly of Isagani’s sweetheart Paulita Gómez, whose aunt Doña Victorina has tasked Isagani with locating her missing husband Don Tiburcio—unaware that Tiburcio is secretly hiding in the home of Isagani’s uncle, Padre Florentino.

Descending to the hold, Simoun greets Basilio and remarks that he never visits Basilio’s province because its people are too poor to buy jewels—repeating Padre Camorra’s jibe that natives lack energy because they drink water instead of beer. Stung, Isagani answers that sweet water puts out fire, and when heated together, small scattered streams can turn into steam or a raging ocean—which Simoun dryly calls “Utopia!” Summoned upstairs by the captain, gentle Padre Florentino joins the friars: Rizal recounts how Florentino’s wealthy mother forced him into the priesthood despite his mutual love for a woman, and how—shortly after the 1872 execution of Gomburza—he retired quietly to his Pacific family estate to keep his dignity as a Filipino priest intact, adopting his sister’s son (or, rumor whispered, his own youthful son) Isagani.

Three Pasig Legends (Ch. III)

1. Malapad-na-Bato: sacred spirit-rock turned bandit nest.
2. Doña Jerónima’s Cave: betrayed woman walled in a riverside grotto by an Archbishop.
3. Miracle of San Nicolás: crocodile turned to stone when invoked by a Chinese infidel.

First Narrative Clue

Entering Laguna de Bay, Ben-Zayb asks where “Guevara, Navarra, or Ibarra” died thirteen years ago; Simoun suddenly turns pale and silent.

Chapter III: Legends on the River and Silence on the Lake

Returning upstairs just as Padre Florentino joins the group, Simoun remarks that no place is worth seeing unless it holds legends. The captain recounts how pre-colonial boatmen once feared spirits at the rock of Malapad-na-Bato until real human bandits replaced ghosts. Padre Florentino tells the humorous legend of Doña Jerónima, who disguised herself as a man to confront a faithless lover who had become Archbishop of Manila; when Simoun pointedly asks Padre Salví whether an Archbishop would not do better locking such a woman inside a nunnery like Santa Clara rather than a public cave, Salví shudders and shifts the topic to the crocodile miracle of San Nicolás.

As the Tabo clears the Pasig bar into broad, mountain-ringed Laguna de Bay, impressed Ben-Zayb suddenly asks the captain where on the lake a man named “Guevara, Navarra, or Ibarra” was killed thirteen years ago! Pointing toward San Diego two miles off, the captain explains how the fugitive dove under rifle fire and blood stained the water, while Padre Sibyla coldly jokes that his corpse joined his father Don Rafael’s cheap watery funeral. Noticing Simoun standing motionless, pale, and silent at the rail, Ben-Zayb asks if he is ill—and the chapter closes with rumors only that the famous traveler went “seasick” on a freshwater lake.

Epistemic Distinction

Literary Interpretation & Historical Context — separated from the explicit plot of Chapters I–III.

Structural Setup

Introduces all three competing responses to Spanish rule: authoritarian reaction (friars), gradualist student reform (Basilio & Isagani), and mysterious provocation (Simoun).

What Chapters 1–3 Reveal About Colonial Society

1. Allegory of the Colonial State: Rizal explicitly compares the circular, whitewashed Tabo—pretending to be modern while moving sluggishly against the current—to the colonial government itself. Its physical split mirrors Manila’s racial hierarchy: Peninsular friars and bureaucrats debate abstract schemes under canvas awnings while native sailors keep the vessel afloat and students sweat beside the engine.

2. Restraint in Narrative Revelation: Notice how Rizal withholds Simoun’s past in Chapters 1–3. To everyone aboard, Simoun appears merely cynical and cruel; only his sharp barb to Padre Salví about Santa Clara and his sudden pallor when “Ibarra’s” lake pursuit is mentioned alert attentive readers that the jeweler carries a personal wound beneath his blue goggles.

Cabesang Tales’ Land Loss and Basilio’s Christmas Eve Return

Chapters IV – V · Tiani and San Diego (Christmas Eve)
Chapter IV · Escalation on Tales’ Land
Clearing the Forest 2 Lives Lost

Tales clears unclaimed woodland with his father Tandang Selo; his wife and eldest daughter Lucia die of malaria during the first harvest.

Initial Friar Claim 20 – 30 Pesos

A neighboring religious order claims the cleared fields; peaceful Tales pays the annual canon to avoid quarrels, encouraged by Selo to imagine crocodiles growing fat.

Escalating Exactions 50 → 200 Pesos

Good sugar harvests double the canon to 50 pesos, while Tales is made cabeza de barangay and forced to cover unpaid tributes. When rent jumps to 200 pesos under threat of eviction, Tales demands title deeds in court and loses his savings.

Christmas Eve Capture 500-Peso Ransom

Son Tano is conscripted; disarmed of shotgun and bolo, Tales patrols with an ax until tulisanes kidnap him. Daughter Juli sells her jewels (keeping only María Clara’s locket) and enters Hermana Penchang’s service for 250 pesos.

Chapter IV: From Law-Abiding Farmer to Ransom Captive

Following Basilio toward San Diego, Chapter 4 introduces the family of Tandang Selo—the kindly old hunter from Noli Me Tángere who once nursed the wounded boy Basilio in the mountains and now weaves brooms in prosperous old age. His industrious son Telesforo (Cabesang Tales) cleared virgin forest at the town border, built a cane-mill and wooden house, and saved to send his daughter Juliana (Juli) to school in Manila alongside Basilio. Yet the moment the fields bore rich crops, a religious corporation claimed ownership and raised annual rent from twenty to fifty and finally two hundred pesos.

Refusing to yield soil watered by his wife’s and daughter Lucia’s blood unless the friars produced legal title, Tales spent every peso appealing to provincial magistrates—who feared losing their posts and ruled for the corporation. Refusing to pay for a military substitute, Tales watched his gentle son Tano marched off as a conscript. When the Captain-General banned firearms and bolos after hearing Tales patrolled his land with a shotgun, Tales carried a short ax—and fell straight into the hands of armed tulisanes demanding 500 pesos within two days! Raising 250 pesos by selling her combs and earrings—refusing to touch only a diamond-and-emerald locket given by Basilio, which Basilio had received from a grateful leper to whom María Clara had given it—Juli pledges herself on Christmas Eve to serve pious Hermana Penchang for the remaining 250 pesos.

Chapter V: Sinong’s Beating and News in San Diego

That same Christmas Eve, Basilio arrives in San Diego by hired carriage. Delayed by a religious procession of wooden saints, his coachman Sinong discovers he forgot his cédula (tax certificate) and is savagely clubbed with rifle butts by the Civil Guard—prompting Sinong to murmur about the folk king Bernardo Carpio, chained between two colliding mountains in San Mateo with only one foot left bound before he frees the Tagalogs from guards and taxes! Stopped a second time by municipal police because his carriage lamp went out, Sinong is hauled to jail while Basilio walks on foot past silent, impoverished houses. To his surprise, inside Capitan Basilio’s bright parlor Basilio glimpses Simoun bargaining jewels with the curate and the alférez. Reaching Capitan Tiago’s house, Basilio hears the overseer’s grim report: Tano has been conscripted and Cabesang Tales has been kidnapped by bandits—leaving Basilio unable to eat his supper.

What Chapters 4–5 Reveal · Literary & Historical Context

Historical Parallel vs. Novel Plot: Cabesang Tales’ rent hikes, demand for title deeds, and court defeat directly mirror the Dominican Hacienda dispute in Rizal’s hometown of Calamba (1887–1891). Meanwhile, Chapter 5’s coachman Sinong introduces peasant folk messianism (Bernardo Carpio), contrasting popular longing for deliverance with Basilio’s faith in modern education—and planting María Clara’s locket in Juli’s hands as a crucial plot link for Chapter 10.

Sisa’s Grave and the Midnight Revelation in the Forest

Chapters VI – VII · The Woods of San Diego (Christmas Midnight)
Chapter VI · Basilio’s Thirteen-Year Rise

From Orphaned Servant to Graduating Physician

Slipping out of Capitan Tiago’s house as midnight Mass bells fade, Basilio enters the hollow hill and ancient balete wood once owned by the Ibarras—now bought by Tiago—and kneels beside his mother Sisa’s unmarked grave. Rizal now fills in the thirteen years since Basilio fled San Diego wounded and starving: arriving in Manila too sick to work, he nearly threw himself beneath carriage horses until he saw Capitan Tiago and Aunt Isabel emerging from Santa Clara convent—brokenhearted on the exact day María Clara took the veil—and followed their coach as an unpaid servant in exchange for enrollment at San Juan de Letrán.

At Dominican-run Letrán, professors ignored ragged Basilio for years, never calling on him once after he recited Latin word-for-word without understanding, yet he passed four years straight and won top honors in his fourth year after fighting a cadet duel! Impressed, Capitan Tiago transferred him to the Jesuit-run Ateneo Municipal, where fair professors, museum apparatus, and natural sciences opened a new world to him. Choosing Medicine over Tiago’s preferred Law, Basilio practiced clinically by his third year, earned fine clothes and savings, and now stands two months from his medical licentiate, chosen to deliver the graduation address before returning to Tiani to marry Juli.

Chapter VII · Simoun Unmasked

The Jeweler Revealed as Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra

Heavy footsteps and a lantern interrupt Basilio’s vigil. Hiding behind the balete roots, Basilio watches Simoun set down his lantern, remove his blue goggles, and dig into the earth with a sharp spade. Stunned, Basilio recognizes the aged, white-haired features of the very stranger who helped him build Sisa’s funeral pyre and dig Elías’s grave thirteen years before! Stepping into the open to offer aid, Basilio faces Simoun’s drawn revolver—and Simoun admits he is Crisóstomo Ibarra, returned after enriching himself in Cuba’s war to destroy the corrupt regime that ruined him.

Sparing Basilio because both are victims seeking justice, Simoun unveils his true strategy: instead of fighting colonial abuses openly, he flatters and bribes the Captain-General and friars to inflame greed, multiply cruelties, and ruin commerce until universal despair forces an uprising—which the students’ campaign for Castilian now threatens to lull back to sleep! Basilio declines to join conspiracy, arguing Castilian will unite the islands and science is humanity’s peaceful future. Simoun rejects Hispanization and warns Basilio that meekness invites destruction:

“Spanish will never be the general language of the country, the people will never talk it, because the conceptions of their brains and the feelings of their hearts cannot be expressed in that language... While a people preserves its language, it preserves the marks of its liberty, as a man preserves his independence while he holds to his own way of thinking.” Simoun to Basilio · Chapter VII, Simoun

Warning Basilio that “there are no despots where there are no slaves,” Simoun lets him walk free at dawn—telling him that the day experience changes his mind, Simoun’s house on the Escolta awaits him.

Character Profile · Simoun (Ibarra)

What Chapters 6–7 Reveal · Central Ideological Axis of the Novel

Why Chapter 7 Is the Hinge of Act I: Only now does the reader understand Simoun’s brutal forced-labor speech on the Tabo (Chapter 1): he is deliberately pushing colonial officials into tyranny to provoke rebellion. Yet Rizal gives Basilio dignified moral counter-arguments—faith in medical science, lawful education, and letting Sisa and Crispin rest without bloodshed—testing throughout the rest of the novel whether colonial society will allow an honest, non-political Filipino like Basilio to live in peace.

A Mute Christmas, Village Pilates, and the Revolver of Tiani

Chapters VIII – X · Tiani (Christmas Day & Aftermath)
Chapter VIII · Merry Christmas

Tandang Selo Struck Mute

Waking before dawn on Christmas morning, Juli checks beneath the Virgin’s niche hoping for a miraculous 250 pesos, finds nothing, and bravely jokes with weeping Tandang Selo that she will return often from Hermana Penchang’s nearby house. Kissing María Clara’s locket goodbye—before wiping her lips on remembering its leper origin—she walks away carrying her trunk.

Watching from his window as scrubbed children pass in stiff Christmas finery to collect gifts they are scolded for touching, lonely Tandang Selo receives relatives later that morning—and when he tries to say “Merry Christmas,” no sound escapes his throat: grief has rendered the old man completely mute.

Transition → News of Selo’s sudden muteness spreads rapidly across Tiani, testing how local authorities respond in Chapter IX.

Chapter IX · Pilates

Washing Hands & Eviction Order

Like Pontius Pilate, every person responsible washes his hands of Selo’s calamity: the Civil Guard lieutenant shrugs that he simply obeyed orders seizing arms; the friar estate-administrator Fray Clemente blames bandits and calls it heaven’s punishment for resisting the corporation; and Hermana Penchang claims God punished Selo because Juli could not recite Latin prayers properly—locking Juli up to work, pray, and read Fray Lucio Bustamante’s anti-education tract Tandang Basio Macunat while Basilio travels to Manila to withdraw his savings to ransom her!

Ransomed Cabesang Tales finally reaches home—only to find his lands handed to a new tenant, Juli in servitude, his father mute, and a court notice ordering him out of his own house within three days. Tales sits motionless beside mute Selo without uttering a word.

Transition → Knowing Tales’ desperate plight, Simoun chooses Tales’ roadside house to display his jewels in Chapter X.

Chapter X · Wealth and Want

María Clara’s Locket & Three Deaths

Lodging overnight at Cabesang Tales’ house with two servants and steel-lined chests, Simoun dazzles Capitan Basilio, Sinang, and Hermana Penchang with priceless gems while showing off his long-range revolver firing at an areca palm two hundred paces off. Asking destitute Tales if he has anything to sell, Simoun spots María Clara’s locket and offers 500 pesos—enough to ransom Juli and save the house!

Going out to consult Juli, Tales looks across his usurped fields and sees Fray Clemente and the new tenant laughing and pointing toward his home. Turning back in blind fury, Tales leaves the locket and a brief note asking forgiveness inside Simoun’s holster, takes Simoun’s revolver, and joins the tulisanes. Next morning Fray Clemente and the new tenant lie hacked to death on the fields with dirt stuffed in their mouths, and the tenant’s wife lies slain in town with “Tales” written in blood beside her—whereupon Civil Guards arrest mute Tandang Selo, while Simoun exults that he has found his man!

Transition → Tales’ escape and Selo’s arrest go straight to the Captain-General’s vacation table at Los Baños in Chapter XI.

What Chapters 8–10 Reveal · Rizal’s Direct Address on Calamba

At the close of Chapter 10, Rizal steps directly into the narrative voice to address the “peaceful citizens of Calamba”—explicitly linking fictional Cabesang Tales (who cleared land, saw his family die, lost in biased courts, and faced eviction) to his own dispossessed townspeople, warning colonial rulers not to feel insulted if desperate peasants driven from their homes take justice into their own hands.

Act II · Chapters XI – XXIV Dec 31 – January · Los Baños & Manila

Traces the Captain-General’s holiday deliberations at Los Baños (Ch. 11), university life and student lobbying in Manila (Ch. 12–15), Simoun’s quiet preparations across Escolta, Quiapo, and Calle Iris (Ch. 16–19), and the evening of the French operetta (Ch. 20–24).

Classrooms of Scholasticism, Student Petitions, and Secret Preparations

While Cabesang Tales flees to the mountains and Tandang Selo sits in jail, the narrative moves to the centers of colonial authority at Los Baños and Manila. Across fourteen chapters, two opposing efforts advance side by side: university students campaign through legal channels to open an Academy of Castilian, while Simoun quietly gathers weapons and recruits aggrieved men for an uprising timed to a private urgency of his own.

Colonial Policy Over a Card Table at Los Baños

Chapter XI · Los Baños (December 31)

On the last day of December at Los Baños—irritable after a staged Bosoboso hunt where a brass band scared off every deer—the Captain-General settles state business casually over a card game of tresillo with Padre Irene, Padre Sibyla, and Padre Camorra (while Sibyla and Irene deliberately lose hands to keep him compliant). When hot-tempered Camorra quits the table, Simoun takes his chair and wagers his diamonds against odd stakes: demanding promises of renouncing poverty/humility from the friars, and blank orders of five-day imprisonment and summary deportation without charges from the General! Asked about the Tiani banditry after his own robbery report, Simoun advises banning all firearms except tiny six-millimeter parlor pistols—ensuring provincial towns stay helpless while the Tiani schoolmaster is summarily suspended merely for requesting roof tiles for his warehouse schoolhouse.

When the secretary raises the students’ Castilian Academy petition (held up six months), sharp fault lines emerge: the upright Spanish High Official and Dominican professor Padre Fernández defend the students’ request as fair, harmless, and politically wise, whereas Padre Sibyla calls it a disguised attack on University of Santo Tomás prestige, Camorra shouts that natives who learn Spanish turn into enemies of God and Spain, and Simoun calls it suspicious subversion! Naming the student leaders—Makaraig, Isagani, and medical student Basilio—Sibyla whispers dark hints about Basilio until the General orders his secretary to record Basilio’s name. Before adjourning for lunch without resolving the Academy, Padre Camorra steps forward to back Juli’s tearful petition to free mute Tandang Selo—which the General waves through to appear merciful.

Three Crucial Seeds Planted in Chapter XI
1. Basilio Placed on the Watchlist

Padre Sibyla flags Basilio’s name to the Captain-General—explaining why Civil Guards later arrest Basilio in Chapter 26 even though he skipped the student banquet.

2. The Deported Tiani Schoolmaster

Suspended without hearing for asking for teaching supplies, this schoolmaster reappears in Chapter 19 manufacturing bombs for Simoun.

3. Padre Camorra’s “Favor” to Juli

Camorra secures Tandang Selo’s release so Juli owes him personal gratitude—setting up the deadly trap in Chapter 30.

What Chapter 11 Reveals · Literary Interpretation

By staging school suspensions, arms decrees, and prison warrants between card deals where friars lose on purpose, Rizal exposes how arbitrary personal whim—not Spanish law—governed the archipelago, while introducing two rare voices of institutional fairness: the Spanish High Official and the Dominican Padre Fernández.

Placido Penitente and the Humiliation in Physics Class

Chapters XII – XIII · Intramuros & University of Santo Tomás
Chapter XII · Placido Penitente

Reluctant Steps to Santo Tomás

Less than a week into January classes, quiet Batangas honor student Placido Penitente ("peaceful sufferer") walks glumly along the Escolta and Intramuros, having already written twice to his mother asking to leave the University of Santo Tomás and work home in Tanauan because four years of classes have left him unrecognized and unlearned. Along the Magallanes paseo, friar pet Juanito Pelaez—hunchbacked, mischievous son of wealthy Spanish mestizo trader Don Timoteo—claps Placido violently on the shoulder, extracts four pesos for the professor’s "Saint’s Day" collection, and brags crudely of spending vacation in Tiani serenading girls alongside Padre Camorra, including “Basilio’s sweetheart Juli, who didn’t know a word of Spanish and had no money!”

Outside Santo Domingo Church, truant Tadeo (who goes to school solely to ask if there is a holiday!) points out radiant Paulita Gómez descending from her carriage with heavily powdered Doña Victorina, who scowls at Isagani while smiling sweetly on rich Juanito Pelaez. Delayed on the campus steps by a student demanding he sign an anti-Academy protest without reading it, Placido refuses to sign unread—entering Physics late just as roll call hits "P," purposely scuffing his heel so the professor will notice he is present.

Chapter XIII · The Class in Physics

Locked Instruments and the Walkout

Inside a bare, tiered hall of 250 fee-paying students—where an unused narra blackboard still bears opening-day "Viva!" and shiny brass-and-glass laboratory apparatus stays locked inside mahogany cabinets purely to show foreign guests that Filipinos lack scientific aptitude—young Dominican professor Padre Millón teaches Physics by Aristotelian syllogism. After mocking a drowsy fat student over mirrors and tangling Juanito Pelaez over whether surface or mercury amalgam constitutes a mirror (while panicked Juanito stomps hard on Placido’s foot for whispered answers!), Millón hears Placido’s cry of pain and pounces on him instead.

Mocking his name (“neither silent nor penitent”), Millón confuses him with Latin tags, counts fifteen absences in his register—explaining that since he rarely calls roll, each catch counts for five—and adds a failure mark in the daily lesson too! When Placido calmly protests that a student marked absent cannot simultaneously recite poorly, Millón sneers "philosophaster!" Stung in his self-respect, Placido flings down his textbook before 234 stunned classmates:

“Enough, Padre, enough! Your Reverence can put all the marks against me that you wish, but you haven’t the right to insult me. Your Reverence may stay with the class, I can’t stand any more.” Placido Penitente · Chapter XIII, The Class in Physics
What Chapters 12–13 Reveal · Literary & Historical Context

Education as Humiliation: Comparing Placido’s UST physics lecture with Basilio’s Ateneo science lessons (Chapter 6), Rizal demonstrates that colonial higher education deliberately crushed native curiosity and personal dignity—explaining why even a mild, non-political student like Placido walks straight out of the classroom into Simoun’s orbit six chapters later (Chapter 19). Notice also Chapter 12’s early romantic warning: Doña Victorina already favors wealthy Juanito Pelaez over Isagani for Paulita’s hand.

The Student Assembly and Señor Pasta’s Counsel of Self-Interest

Chapters XIV – XV · Makaraig’s Boarding House & Señor Pasta’s Office
Chapter XIV · In the House of the Students

Sandoval’s Optimism vs. Pecson’s Skepticism

Inside Makaraig’s bustling student boarding house—amid fencing foils, Chinese snack vendors, and boisterous study—the leaders of the Castilian Academy Association (Isagani, generous peninsular Spaniard Sandoval, pessimistic Pecson, and Juanito Pelaez) await word from Los Baños. Eloquent Sandoval insists the government cannot refuse young men asking only to learn Spain’s tongue at their own expense, declaring Spaniards feel with the same heart as Filipinos; Pecson grins skeptically and warns that if a single official fears for "national integrity," their patriotic effort will earn them charges of filibusterismo instead of praise!

Entering from his talk with Padre Irene, Makaraig announces a mixed victory: Irene saved the petition from burial at Los Baños by steering it to the Superior Commission of Primary Instruction, with "liberal" Don Custodio appointed sole arbiter! Irene suggested winning Custodio over through either the dancing-girl Pepay or influential lawyer Señor Pasta. Rejecting dishonorable back-channel manipulation through Pepay unless all honest paths fail, Isagani volunteers to visit his uncle Padre Florentino’s old classmate Señor Pasta that afternoon.

Chapter XV · Señor Pasta

The Thorn of Gray Hairs

Received after a long wait in the famous Filipino jurist’s crowded study, Isagani lays out the students’ case. Though privately impressed, Señor Pasta resolves instantly not to risk his lucrative friar clientele once he learns Vice-Rector Padre Sibyla opposes the Academy. Lecturing Isagani on colonial "prestige," Pasta claims colonial governments lose authority if they grant requests coming from citizens below!

When Isagani replies that legitimate governments rest on justice and reason rather than fear, Pasta drops legal pretexts and offers fatherly survival advice: study medicine quietly, charge rich patients well, marry a wealthy devout girl, shun public affairs, and thank him when he grows gray hairs. Rising with sad dignity, Isagani answers with one of the novel’s defining civic statements:

“When I have gray hairs like those, sir, and turn my gaze back over my past and see that I have worked only for myself, without having done what I plainly could and should have done for the country that has given me everything, for the citizens that have helped me to live—then, sir, every gray hair will be a thorn, and instead of rejoicing, they will shame me!” Isagani to Señor Pasta · Chapter XV, Señor Pasta

Chapter XVI · The Tribulations of a Chinese

Quiroga’s Debt and Smuggled Rifles

That Saturday evening, wealthy merchant Quiroga—dreaming of becoming Chinese consul—hosts friars, officials, merchants, and journalists above his Escolta bazaar. While Don Timoteo Pelaez lobbies to demolish poor nipa houses so he can sell his costly galvanized iron sheets, Simoun corners distressed Quiroga in a private room: Quiroga owes him 9,000 pesos for three diamond bracelets lent to an influential lady friend who kept all three instead of picking one!

Meanwhile, in the salon, Ben-Zayb, Padre Camorra, Padre Salví, Don Custodio, and Juanito decide to visit the Quiapo Fair to inspect an American magician’s talking head.

Transition → Simoun secures his hidden rifle depot while Salví’s party walks directly to Quiapo Square in Chapter XVII.

Chapter XVII · The Quiapo Fair

Paulita’s Suitors & Folk Carvings

Beneath paper lanterns and music in January’s Quiapo Square, lustful Padre Camorra pinches Ben-Zayb’s arm at every pretty passerby and whispers envy of Tiani’s parish—nearly bumping strangers when radiant Paulita Gómez strolls past with uneasy Isagani (angered by every male stare she attracts) and Doña Victorina, who greets Juanito Pelaez warmly as her favorite!

Stopping at native wooden figurines, the group laughs over a carving resembling Padre Camorra titled "The Philippine Press" (a one-eyed old crone ironing cloth with a heavy flatiron) and "The Country of Abaka" (two Civil Guards binding a youth before shooting him). Debating native art, stingy Padre Camorra disappears rather than pay admission for the others at Mr. Leeds’ side-show tent.

Transition → Salví, Ben-Zayb, Custodio, and Juanito step behind the black curtains of Mr. Leeds’ tent in Chapter XVIII.

Chapter XVIII · Legerdemain

Imuthis Exposes Padre Salví’s Guilt

Inside a funeral-draped hall smelling of incense, American showman Mr. Leeds (Simoun’s friend) invites cocky Ben-Zayb to search his table for hidden mirrors—finding nothing because Leeds slid the angled mirrors away with a secret foot-spring beforehand! Opening an Egyptian box from Khufu’s pyramid holding ashes and papyrus, Leeds cries "Deremof!" (anagram of freedom), and the cadaverous head Imuthis rises before the lamp!

Staring straight at Padre Salví, Imuthis recounts how in ancient Egypt he returned from foreign study loving a priest’s pure daughter, uncovered young priest Gaumata’s imposture, was framed in a sham rebellion and slain on Lake Moeris, and saw from death the lustful priest hound the virgin into the sanctuary of Philae until terror drove her mad! Recognizing every detail of Ibarra, Lake Laguna, and María Clara in Santa Clara, Padre Salví shrieks "Mercy! She still lives!" and faints dead away! Next day the Church bans the show, while Mr. Leeds sails serenely for Hong Kong.

Transition → Outside in the fairgrounds that very night, furious Placido Penitente spots Simoun bidding Mr. Leeds goodbye in Chapter XIX.

Chapter XIX · The Fuse

The Bomb-Maker & Simoun’s Crisis

Stinging from Padre Millón’s insult (Ch. 13), Placido Penitente returns to his lodging to find his mother Cabesang Andang arrived from Batangas urging patience and friar apologies. Fleeing hungry to the docks and Quiapo Fair dreaming of escaping to Hong Kong to return and fight, Placido recognizes Simoun seeing off Mr. Leeds and asks help reaching Hong Kong. Instead, Simoun takes Placido to a hidden Calle Iris hut where the deported Tiani schoolmaster prepares gunpowder and bombs!

Through a Balabac ex-convict, Simoun orders Cabesang Tales, Quiroga’s rifles, the regiment, and suburbs ready within the coming week—murmuring darkly that waiting until Lent will be too late if María Clara dies first! Left alone later in his Escolta mansion, feverish Simoun wrestles for two hours with terrifying visions of Don Rafael and Elías reproaching him for mixing patriotic revolt with personal vengeance, before hardening his resolve.

Transition → While Simoun sets his one-week countdown, Don Custodio sits down to decide the students’ fate in Chapter XX.

What Chapters 16–19 Reveal · Unifying the Conspiracy

Far from being digressions, Chapters 16–19 assemble every piece of Simoun’s machinery while revealing his hidden vulnerability: Quiroga’s warehouses supply the rifles (Ch. 16), Mr. Leeds’ "Deremof" proves Padre Salví’s guilt over María Clara (Ch. 18), and Calle Iris reveals both how institutional victims (Placido and the suspended schoolmaster) become revolutionaries and why Simoun rushes prematurely—his entire revolution is timed around rescuing María Clara alive (Ch. 19).

Don Custodio’s Ruling and the Night at the French Operetta

Chapters XX – XXII · Manila & Teatro de Variedades
Chapter XX · The Arbiter

“Eureka!” From the Red Cedar Shelf

Tasked with judging the students’ Academy, Don Custodio de Salazar y Sánchez de Monteredondo ("Buena Tinta") pores over files for fifteen days hoping to satisfy both the friars and liberal opinion. Rizal traces his hollow career: marrying a wealthy Manila mestiza, visiting Madrid where nobody noticed his provincial speeches, and returning convinced that Spaniards are born to rule while Filipinos are suited only for manual labor and wooden shoes—boasting that he "loves the indios" by keeping them humble!

Left dizzy by Señor Pasta’s evasive advice and interrupted by dancing-girl Pepay (who merely pirouettes in to extract 25 pesos to bury a fictitious aunt, saying not a word about the Academy), Custodio scans his cedar shelves of PROJECTS until he opens PROJECTS IN EXECUTION and spots the School of Arts and Trades, placed under Augustinian friars. Shouting "Eureka!", he writes out his decision before midnight.

Transition → The next evening, all Manila converges on the French operetta in Chapter XXI—where both Custodio’s ruling and Simoun’s uprising await.

Chapter XXI · Manila Types

“The Signal Is a Gunshot”

At 7:30 PM outside the packed Teatro de Variedades—where M. Jouy’s French operetta Les Cloches de Corneville is sold out strictly because Padre Salví’s pastoral ban advertised its "immorality"—strolls ragged, red-haired Spanish nobleman-turned-vagabond Camaroncocido ("boiled shrimp") with dwarf bill-poster Tío Quico.

In dark side streets around the theater, Camaroncocido notices strange provincial newcomers and overhears Simoun’s agents whispering two opposite lies: telling soldiers that the Captain-General staged an uprising to stay in power against the friars, while telling fanatics that the friars ordered it against the General! Seeing Simoun speak from a carriage to Placido’s companions, Camaroncocido clearly hears the order: "The signal is a gunshot!"—yet shrugs "What is that to me?" Meanwhile, Tadeo regales an awed newcomer with invented scandals until Makaraig, Sandoval, Pecson, and Isagani hand Tadeo Basilio’s unused ticket.

Transition → The students enter the theater hall while Basilio stays home studying beside dying Capitan Tiago.

Chapter XXII · The Performance

Students Reduced to Friar Collectors

Inside the theater—delayed until 8:45 PM so the Captain-General can enter to the Royal March—Isagani suffers torments seeing Paulita Gómez seated in a box beside Doña Victorina and Juanito Pelaez, who pretends to know French by laughing whenever Paulita laughs while Victorina daydreams of marrying Juanito once old Don Tiburcio dies! Through opera glasses, the students spot Padre Irene backstage wearing a false mustache to "investigate" French danseuse Serpolette up close!

During intermission, while Paulita slips word arranging an explanation tomorrow, Makaraig returns from Pepay’s box holding a belated note from Don Custodio: the Castilian Academy is "approved" only on condition that a religious corporation directs and supervises it completely, while the student Association serves merely to collect fees and contributions! Bitterly calling themselves friar tax-gatherers (cabezas de barangay), the students walk out mid-show to hold a sarcastic Chinese dinner at the pansitería.

Transition → At that exact hour (10:30 PM), why has Simoun’s gunshot signal never fired? Chapter XXIII reveals what happened simultaneously across town.

Ten-Thirty at Night: María Clara’s Death and Dreams on the Malecón

Chapters XXIII – XXIV · Capitan Tiago’s House & Paseo de María Cristina
Chapter XXIII · A Corpse

Why the First Uprising Never Fires

Neither Simoun nor Basilio went to the theater. Having spent his hard-earned savings to ransom Juli from Hermana Penchang’s servitude, Basilio sits in Capitan Tiago’s dim room studying borrowed, uncut medical books while nursing Capitan Tiago, whose opium poisoning—aggravated whenever Padre Irene visits—spreads through his frail body like gangrene through colonial society.

As the clock strikes half-past ten (10:30 PM), Simoun enters agitated and pale: within an hour, he announces, the revolution will break out at his signal, with Cabesang Tales downstairs and thousands armed! Laying a loaded revolver on the desk, Simoun forces Basilio to choose between death or leading a detachment to storm Santa Clara convent and bring out María Clara—confessing that rescuing María Clara is the sole reason he lived and plotted revolution! Astounded, Basilio tells him he has come too late: María Clara died at six o’clock that very afternoon, pointing to Padre Salví’s letter brought by Padre Irene while the death agony rang at dusk! Tearing his hair and crying "Dead! Without my having seen her!", Simoun stumbles out into the night like a madman—forgetting to give his waiting forces their signal and collapsing the entire uprising!

Chapter XXIV · Dreams

Isagani and Paulita by Manila Bay

The next afternoon (Thursday), word spreads that Simoun lies gravely ill in bed refusing even the Captain-General’s aides, while disappointed armed bands melt back into the hills. Walking along the seafront Malecón toward his sunset meeting with Paulita Gómez, Isagani passes Ben-Zayb and resolves to confront Paulita over Juanito Pelaez—yet melts the instant her carriage stops and she playfully blames Doña Victorina (who badgers Isagani for news of Don Tiburcio, still hidden safely at Padre Florentino’s house).

Reconciled, Isagani speaks passionately of his forest-clad coastal mountains and envisions a transformed Philippines working hand-in-hand with an enlightened Spain: ports crowded with steamships, railroads linking provinces, science and industry flourishing in freedom—or, if Spain remains deaf, dying gladly so Paulita may gaze proudly on his corpse as a patriot who fell defending his country’s rights! Pampered, city-bred Paulita shudders at mountain leeches and dismisses his vision with a smile: "Dreams, dreams! Aunt says that this country will always remain enslaved!"

What Chapters 23–24 Reveal · Private Obsession vs. Civic Idealism

Juxtaposing Chapters 23 and 24 exposes the fragility beneath both protagonists’ hopes: in Chapter 23, Simoun abandons hundreds of recruited fighters at 10:30 PM because his revolution was subordinated to a personal obsession with saving María Clara; in Chapter 24, Isagani fails to see that Paulita Gómez—who prizes carriages, comfort, and social security—will never share poverty or persecution for patriotic ideals.

Fourteen students (Basilio absent nursing Tiago, Juanito absent) dine upstairs at the Pansitería Macanista de Buen Gusto, posting a mock verse honoring Don Custodio and dedicating four Chinese courses: pansit lang-lang soup to Don Custodio, lumpiang Intsik (pork rolls) to Padre Irene, tortang alimango (crab omelet) to the friars for moving backward like crabs, and pansit gisado to the Government and Country.

Chapter XXVI · Pasquinades

Seditious Posters & Basilio’s Arrest

Rising early to visit his hospital ward and borrow licensure fees from Makaraig, Basilio finds UST panicked and guarded by sentries: seditious posters (pasquinades) threatening slaughter, convent pillage, and rebellion were torn from campus doors at dawn and sent to the Civil Government! Passing frightened students, Basilio watches Tadeo cheer over holidays before trembling at jail, and Juanito Pelaez frantically disown the Association ("You know I never took part!").

Only Isagani stands tall on the street lecturing pale classmates that courage belongs where honor lies! Chapter XXVII · The Friar and the Filipino

“That You Do Your Duty!”

Having overheard Isagani’s fearless speech from his window, Dominican professor Padre Fernández—whom Isagani always exempted from criticism—invites the youth into his study for an honest reckoning as "a friar and a Filipino student." Asked bluntly what Filipino students want of the friars, Isagani answers like a cannon-shot: "That you do your duty!"—charging that the friars, holding a monopoly over Philippine education, act like a corrupt prison food-contractor who starves inmates so they never grow strong enough to leave jail!

When Fernández blames government laws and native flaws ("bad clay"), Isagani replies that after 350 years an artist who produces only caricatures while forbidding others to sculpt proves his own incapacity! Deeply moved, Dominican Padre Fernández promises to speak to his brethren and envies the Jesuits who taught Isagani as a boy—an irony Rizal punctures immediately: when Isagani voluntarily presents himself for arrest that afternoon, the Jesuits disown him completely so as not to endanger themselves!

Citywide Panic, Capitan Tiago’s Death, and Juli’s Leap in Tiani

Chapters XXVIII – XXX · Manila & the Convent of Tiani
Chapter XXVIII · Tatakut

Panic, Shootings & Tiago’s End

Wild rumors of students allying with San Mateo bandits and foreign warships plunge Manila into tatakut (terror). Terrified Quiroga rushes to Simoun—who stays unseen in bed, sending word to "leave things in the warehouses as they are"—and then to barricaded Don Custodio and Ben-Zayb. While Placido Penitente and the pyrotechnist warn Tondo workmen not to rise with Simoun sick, provocateurs scatter coins and soldiers shoot dead a deaf passerby and shopkeeper who miss "Quien vive!", while newspapers censor Ben-Zayb’s discovery of a dead native girl below the Luneta walls!

Visiting bedridden Capitan Tiago, Padre Irene piles on horrifying tales of Basilio’s arrest, court-martials, and beheadings until trembling Tiago gasps, clamps his bony fingers onto Irene’s arm in death agony, and dies with bulging eyes—dragged onto the floor as panicked Irene bolts out the door!

Transition → Executor Padre Irene immediately takes charge of Tiago’s estate and lavish funeral in Chapter XXIX.

Chapter XXIX · Exit Capitan Tiago

Stolen Estate & Funeral Pomp

Appointed executor, Padre Irene stages magnificent obsequies with three priests, incense, and Latin dirges, yet piously dresses Tiago’s body in a frayed old Franciscan habit (saving 36 pesos against a tailor’s frock coat because "God does not check tailors’ labels"!).

Tiago’s manipulated will leaves his fortune to Santa Clara convent, the Pope, the Archbishop, and the religious orders, plus 20 pesos for poor students’ fees—while stripping imprisoned Basilio of his 25-peso legacy for alleged "ingratitude," which hypocritical Padre Irene grandly boasts he will pay out of his own pocket and conscience! Philosopher Don Primitivo solemnly debates how Tiago will challenge St. Peter to celestial cockfights without spurs, while rival bigot Doña Patrocinio longs to die tomorrow just to outshine his funeral.

Transition → Stripped of Capitan Tiago’s protection and money, Basilio has no patron left when the news reaches Tiani in Chapter XXX.

Chapter XXX · Juli

The Tiani Convent Window

In Tiani, Capitan Tiago’s death seals Basilio’s doom: while rich classmates walk free, orphaned Basilio remains jailed alone, rumored bound for exile and murder en route to the Carolines! Haunted by nightmares of her father Tales and Basilio shot in blood, Juli implores the town clerk and Justice of the Peace, who with Sister Bali tell her only Padre Camorra ("the Little Horse") can save Basilio—if Juli goes to the convento herself!

Knowing the dishonorable price Camorra demanded when freeing Selo, Juli resists in agony until news arrives that Basilio is the last prisoner left and sails soon. Dragged weeping that afternoon to the convento door and pushed inside by Sister Bali, Juli faces Padre Camorra—and at nightfall leaps from the convent window onto the stones to her death as Sister Bali runs screaming down the street! At twilight, mute Tandang Selo beats the locked convento doors in vain—and next day vanishes forever carrying his hunting-spear.

La Última Razón, the Wedding Feast, and the Pomegranate Lamp

Chapters XXXIII – XXXV · Simoun’s Laboratory & Calle Anloague (Late April)
Lampara ni Simoun · Oxidized-gold pomegranate with carnelian seeds, 2-cm steel tank & over 1 liter of nitroglycerin

Chapter XXXIII: Basilio Joins the Conspiracy

On the afternoon of the Pelaez–Gómez wedding—after more than three months in prison, freed at last through Simoun’s bribes—haggard, burning-eyed Basilio enters Simoun’s Escolta room like a living corpse risen from Sisa’s woods: "I have been a bad son and a bad brother... Now return to me my word—give me arms and let the revolution break out!" Rejoicing that his own hesitation has vanished, Simoun opens a dark shagreen red-satin case in his laboratory, unveiling an oxidized-gold lamp shaped like a split pomegranate set with carnelian seeds, housing an inner tank of steel two centimeters thick holding over a liter of yellowish Nitro-glycerin.

Calling it "concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, injustice, outrage—the last resort of the weak," Simoun details the 9:00 PM plan: placed inside the specially built dining-kiosk at Calle Anloague—whose roof and floor are packed with sacks of gunpowder—the lamp will dim after twenty minutes; raising the wick will detonate a fulminate of mercury cap, blowing the colonial elite to atoms! Hearing the blast, Cabesang Tales (Matanglawin) at Santa Mesa and deceived soldiers will attack while Basilio waits at San Sebastián, clears Calle Anloague by 8:45, leads the crowd to Quiroga’s rifle warehouses, and executes every neutral citizen who refuses to fight!

Chapter XXXIV: Capitan Tiago’s House Transformed

Checking his loaded revolver while walking until 8:00 PM, Basilio stops before his former home at Calle Anloague. With money loaned by Simoun, groom’s father Don Timoteo Pelaez has remodeled Capitan Tiago’s house in garish bad taste—replacing Filipino paintings with loud foreign chromolithographs (so the General will not suspect natives paint better than Spaniards!) and erecting on the wide azotea a sumptuous raised octagonal dining-kiosk for the seven supreme "gods" of church and state, wired invisibly to gunpowder below.

Carriages roll up bearing Manila’s highest officials, friars, and newlyweds Juanito Pelaez and radiant Paulita Gómez in gauze and orange-blossoms—provoking a fleeting pang in Basilio as he spots pale Isagani across the street staring heartbreak-stricken at his lost bride! Moments later Simoun’s carriage arrives carrying the wrapped shagreen lamp-case; Simoun climbs the carpeted stairs smiling beside Don Timoteo, stays briefly upstairs, and hurries back down deadly pale, ordering his driver: "To the Escolta—hurry!"

Chapter XXXV (Part 1): The Handwriting of Crisóstomo Ibarra

Outside on the pavement, Basilio feels momentary pity seeing carriages of uninvolved guests and even Padre Irene, before memories of Juli’s mangled body and mute Tandang Selo harden his heart; trying once to step inside, he is rudely turned back by the porter for his poor clothes and retreats down the street after seeing Simoun flee! Upstairs, the Captain-General sits moodily in the seven-plate azotea kiosk admiring Simoun’s dazzling pomegranate lamp set at the center of the banquet table.

Before the soup ends, guests find beside a plate a square parchment slip written in blood-red ink: Mene, Tekel, Phares — Juan Crisostomo Ibarra. Don Custodio gasps that the biblical words threaten slaughter tonight, and when the slip reaches Padre Salví, the Franciscan turns ashen and whispers in terror that it is unmistakably Crisóstomo Ibarra’s authentic handwriting! Though guests freeze expecting poison, nothing happens—so the General forces a laugh as the pomegranate lamp begins flickering dim and asks: "Will you turn up the wick, Padre Irene?"

Chapter XXXV (Part 2): Isagani Saves Paulita

Hurrying away up the street moments before the explosion, Basilio collides with Isagani, who lingers dazed outside hoping for one last glimpse of Paulita. Dragging him urgently by the arm ("Come away, Isagani—do not go near!"), Basilio sees Isagani refuse to leave and blurts out the truth to save his friend’s life: "The house is mined! It is going to blow up at any moment, through a rashness, through a curiosity! Simoun’s lamp—within a few minutes!"

Basilio forgets one decisive motive: Isagani still loves Paulita Gómez! Fatality: Carolino and the Mute Old Man on the Cliff

Chapter XXXVIII · Mountain Pass in Luzon

Across central and southern Luzon, Matanglawin (Cabesang Tales) ravages haciendas and towns while unable Civil Guards seize harmless peasants instead, marching them roped elbow-to-elbow beneath scorching sun and whipping them with kamagon sticks. Among the native soldiers escorting seven bound prisoners is Carolino—just returned from colonial service in the Carolines—who pleads with brutal soldier Mautang to stop clubbing fainting captives because "they are men like ourselves."

As the column enters a narrow, rock-walled defile, hidden rifles crack from the crags above: Mautang pitches dead coughing blood, and an arrow strikes the Spanish corporal’s thigh! Blind with rage, the corporal first orders his squad to shoot dead every one of the bound, kneeling prisoners. Spotting a man signaling with a rifle high on a boulder, the corporal threatens to tie Carolino up unless he proves his famed marksmanship; Carolino aims and fires, sending that man tumbling backward behind the rock. Instantly a second figure waving a spear steps onto the ledge; the soldiers fire a volley, and as Carolino scrambles up to finish the fallen rebel, he freezes in horror over a white-haired old man dying in blood upon the stone.

Carolino is Tano, the conscripted son of Cabesang Tales—and the dying man felled before him is his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo, who stares into Tano’s eyes with unspeakable grief and points one rigid, dying finger behind the rocks before breathing his last.

Remembering how Tandang Selo left Tiani carrying his hunting-spear after Juli’s death (Chapter 30), the reader grasps every link of the family’s annihilation: forced into a Spanish uniform, Tano has stood watch over bound countrymen, shot down a defender behind the crag, and taken part in killing his mute grandfather.

What Chapter 38 Reveals · Colonial Fratricide

Chapter 38 completes the tragedy begun in Chapter 4: colonial injustice first dispossessed Cabesang Tales, enslaved and killed Juli, struck Tandang Selo mute, and finally used conscripted Filipino soldiers like Tano (Carolino) to kill their own kin in defense of foreign rule.

Conclusion: Simoun’s Confession and the Pacific Abyss

Chapter XXXIX · Father Florentino’s Solitary House by the Sea

At Padre Florentino’s isolated coastal home, timid Don Tiburcio de Espadaña hastily packs his satchel and flees to a woodsman’s hut—misreading a telegram sent by Lieutenant Perez ("Spaniard hidden house Padre Florentino cojera [cogerá, 'will capture'] tonight eight alive or dead") as Doña Victorina hunting down his lameness (cojera)! In reality, the telegram refers to Simoun, who arrived two days earlier pale, bloodstained, and carrying his steel jewel-chest, refusing medical help from town. Entering Simoun’s room to warn him that Civil Guards arrive at 8:00 PM, Padre Florentino learns that Simoun has already swallowed slow poison so he will never fall alive into his enemies’ hands.

In the gathering dusk, Simoun confesses his entire secret: how thirteen years earlier he returned from Europe as Crisóstomo Ibarra hoping only to build a school and marry María Clara, escaped death through Elías’s sacrifice, dug up his buried family treasure, amassed vast wealth in Cuba’s war, bought the Captain-General’s appointment, and deliberately fomented colonial greed and cruelty to ignite revolution—only to see everything collapse twice. Demanding bitterly why God denied him victory while sparing worse tyrants, Simoun hears Padre Florentino’s quiet, unsparing verdict: even Simoun’s mortal wounds came "from the very ones you have instigated to crime," because hate breeds only monsters, and "Redemption presupposes virtue, virtue sacrifice, and sacrifice love!"

“So, while the Filipino people has not sufficient energy to proclaim, with head erect and bosom bared, its rights to social life, and to guarantee it with its sacrifices, with its own blood... why grant them liberty? With Spain or without Spain they would always be the same, and perhaps worse! Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow? And that they will be such is not to be doubted, for he who submits to tyranny loves it.” Father Florentino to Simoun · Chapter XXXIX, Conclusion

Pressing Florentino’s hand once in silent acceptance, Simoun sighs and lies still in the dark while Padre Florentino whispers his call to the future generation: "Where are the youth who will consecrate their golden hours, their illusions, and their enthusiasm to the welfare of their native land? ... Pure and spotless must the victim be that the sacrifice may be acceptable! We await you, O youth! Come, for we await you!" When a servant brings a lamp, Simoun is cold and dead. Praying "God have mercy on those who turned him from the straight path!", Padre Florentino carries the heavy steel chest of Simoun’s jewels down to Isagani’s cliff rock and hurls the treasure into the roaring Pacific waves, praying that Nature guard it in the abyss until humanity needs it "for some holy and sublime purpose."

What Chapter 39 Reveals · Ethical Resolution Without Political Complacency

Notice what Padre Florentino does not preach: he explicitly affirms that "God is justice, He cannot abandon His cause, the cause of liberty" and condemns passive submission ("he who submits to tyranny loves it"). What he rejects is Simoun’s method—corrupting society through bribery, deception, and private revenge without building civic conscience—insisting that true liberation requires self-respect, public courage, and moral readiness.

1. Historical Context (1891–1892)

Confiscation and Dapitan Exile

Rizal shipped nearly the entire Ghent edition to José María Basa in Hong Kong before returning to Asia in October 1891. Spanish customs seized smuggled crates in Manila, and colonial authorities cited Rizal’s writings and alleged anti-friar tracts when Governor-General Despujol deported him to Dapitan in July 1892.

2. Historical Context (1892–1896)

Readers in the Katipunan

Although Father Florentino rejected Simoun’s corrupt conspiracy, Andrés Bonifacio—who read both novels closely—and members of the Katipunan drew powerful lessons from Cabesang Tales’ dispossession and the crushed student Academy, seeing proof that peaceful assimilation under Spain had reached a dead end.

3. Historical Context (1925–1956)

The Manuscript and Republic Act 1425

Purchased by the Philippine government from Valentin Ventura in 1925 for ₱10,000, the 279-page manuscript survived World War II and a brief 1961 theft. On June 12, 1956, Republic Act No. 1425 (authored by Sen. Claro M. Recto and sponsored by Sen. Jose P. Laurel) mandated the study of unexpurgated editions across Philippine schools and universities.

Concluding Literary Synthesis: Why Both Reform and Conspiracy Fail

Read continuously from Chapter 1 to Chapter 39, El Filibusterismo conducts a rigorous double critique. First, Rizal demonstrates that peaceful reform inside an unaccountable colonial system is systematically blocked: Cabesang Tales loses cleared land in timid courts (Ch. 4–10), Placido Penitente’s dignity is trampled in UST classrooms (Ch. 12–13), Isagani’s lawful Castilian Academy is turned into friar tax-collection by Don Custodio (Ch. 14–22), Basilio’s quiet devotion to medicine cannot save Juli or keep him out of Bilibid Prison (Ch. 6–7, 26–32), and even the upright Spanish High Official can only resign in protest (Ch. 31).

Yet Rizal simultaneously ensures that Simoun’s conspiracy fails twice—first at 10:30 PM when he learns María Clara died at six o’clock (Ch. 23), and second at Calle Anloague when Basilio warns Isagani and Isagani hurls the pomegranate lamp into the Pasig to save Paulita (Ch. 35)—while Simoun himself receives his fatal wounds from the very outlaws he deceived (Ch. 36, 39). Through Father Florentino, Rizal distinguishes between the moral right of an oppressed people to seek liberty and a revolution poisoned by bribery, manipulation, and private revenge.

Rather than endorsing passive submission or vengeance-driven terror, Rizal leaves the responsibility with an uncorrupted future generation: to build the civic courage, self-respect, and love of justice required so that the slaves of today do not become the tyrants of tomorrow.
