01

The Title & The Accusation: What is a Filibustero?

Orientation · Historical Linguistics & Political Weaponization · 1891

Originally derived from seventeenth-century Dutch and French buccaneers (vrijbuiter / flibustier), the Spanish term filibustero was weaponized in the nineteenth-century Philippines to condemn any native who dared question Spanish supremacy.

Writing to Ferdinand Blumentritt in March 1887, José Rizal explained the political terror attached to the label:

“The word filibustero is very little known in the Philippines... The educated people fear the reach of the word... It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be on the gallows.” José Rizal to Ferdinand Blumentritt · 1887
Translation vs. Original Intent

The Subversion vs. The Reign of Greed

Charles Derbyshire’s canonical 1912 English translation titled the novel The Reign of Greed, emphasizing the insatiable avarice of colonial friars and officials.

However, Rizal’s Spanish title, El Filibusterismo, literally means The Subversion. It is not an entertaining romance, but a rigorous political anatomy examining whether an oppressed nation can achieve liberation through calculated, explosive revolution.

Core Insight: Rizal deliberately claimed the exact criminal accusation used by the colonial regime to execute native reformists and turned it into an emblem of defiant national resistance.
02

Why Rizal Wrote It: The 1872 Gomburza Martyrdom

Orientation · Formative Historical Catalyst · February 17, 1872 · Bagumbayan

On February 17, 1872, three secular Filipino Catholic priests—Mariano Gómez (72), José Burgos (35), and Jacinto Zamora (36)—were publicly garroted at Bagumbayan.

Falsely implicated by Spanish friars in the Cavite Mutiny after advocating for Filipino parishes, their deaths traumatized the colony. Rizal’s older brother Paciano was a close student of Father Burgos and was forced into hiding. Ten-year-old José vowed to dedicate his life to avenging their memory.

In an 1889 letter to Mariano Ponce, Rizal confessed: “Without 1872 there would not be today a Plaridel, nor a Jaena, nor a Sanciangco... Without 1872 Rizal would now be a Jesuit and instead of writing Noli Me Tángere, would have written the contrary.”

The Ghent Inscription

Solemn Dedication of the Novel

Rizal risked his life by dedicating the novel directly to the executed priests:

“To the memory of the priests, Don Mariano Gómez (85), Don José Burgos (30), and Don Jacinto Zamora (35). Executed on the scaffold of Bagumbayan on the 28th of February, 1872...”

Archival note: Writing from memory in Europe, Rizal noted their ages and execution date from childhood recollection. The Catholic Church refused to defrock the three priests before their execution, affirming their innocence.

Formative Catalyst: Gomburza’s execution was not incidental background; it was the spiritual trauma that proved peaceful reform was dead and compelled the writing of the sequel.
03

The Road to Ghent: Unequal Forces Behind the Novel

Orientation · Social Reality, Ideology & Material Production · 1887 – 1891
Primary Social Reality · 1887–1891

The Calamba Land Evictions

The lived reality that drives the novel’s anger. When the Dominican hacienda raised rents arbitrarily, Rizal assisted Calamba tenants in petitioning the government.

In brutal reprisal, Gov.-Gen. Valeriano Weyler dispatched artillery and troops to demolish tenant homes. Rizal’s elderly parents, brother Paciano, and sisters were dispossessed and banished. This direct injustice directly birthed the tragedy of Cabesang Tales in Chapter IV.

Political Disillusionment · 1890–1891

Madrid Schism & Heartbreak

The death of reformist illusions. In Madrid, Filipino expatriates fractured into rival camps between Rizal and Marcelo H. del Pilar. Rizal recognized that lobbying Spanish politicians was futile.

Simultaneously, his eleven-year sweetheart Leonor Rivera was coerced by her mother into marrying English engineer Henry Kipping—inspiring Paulita Gómez's abandonment of Isagani.

Material Production · Autumn 1891

The Ghent Printing Crisis

Poverty and publication. In Ghent, Belgium, living on biscuits and tea, Rizal watched printing halt at page 112 when his money vanished.

Rizal prepared to burn the manuscript until friend Valentín Ventura sent 500 francs from Paris. The first edition rolled off F. Meyer-van Loo Press on September 18, 1891.

Hierarchy of Context: The Calamba evictions provided the raw fuel of lived oppression, Madrid shattered faith in parliamentary lobbying, and Ghent was the grueling material crucible where the physical book survived.

The Bridge from Noli: From Peaceful Reform to Calculated Fire

Orientation · Literary Continuity · Thirteen Years After Noli Me Tángere
Noli Me Tángere · Berlin, 1887

Crisóstomo Ibarra: The Peaceful Reformer

A wealthy, European-educated young Filipino returns to San Diego trusting in Spanish enlightenment, modern education, and legal progress. He seeks to construct a public schoolhouse and marry his childhood love, María Clara.

Result: Framed for a sham uprising by Padre Salví, excommunicated, hunted by the Civil Guard across Laguna de Bay, and presumed dead. His ancestral fortune is confiscated and María Clara enters the cloister of Santa Clara.

El Filibusterismo · Ghent, 1891

Simoun: The Cynical Subversive

Thirteen years later, Ibarra reappears unrecognizable behind large blue-tinted goggles and a beard. Having accumulated vast wealth in Cuban mines, he returns as the Governor-General’s “Brown Cardinal,” using bribery and tyranny to push colonial abuses to intolerable extremes.

Core Question: Can violent retaliation redeem a nation, or does corrupt revolution destroy the innocent along with the guilty?

Thematic Arc: Noli was a diagnosis of Spanish colonial sickness through romance and hope; Fili is a forensic dissection of revolution, testing whether corrupt means can ever produce true liberty.

The Steamer Tabo: Anatomy of the Colonial Ship of State

Unit 1 · Chapters I – III · Late December · Pasig River
The Bapor Tabo navigating upstream toward Laguna de Bay

Rizal opens the novel aboard the river steamer Tabo, explicitly presenting it as an allegorical mirror of the colonial “Ship of State”:

  • Clumsy and Round: Shaped like a native coconut-shell dipper (tabo), lumbering forward with heavy, awkward vanity.
  • Whitewashed Rust: Freshly coated in gleaming white paint on the outside to mask filthy boilers, rust, and mechanical decay underneath.
  • Stalled on Sandbars: Continually running aground on river shallows, pushed forward only by native boatmen straining on bamboo poles.
  • Racial & Class Segregation: Two distinct decks physically segregating the colonial rulers from the subjugated population.
Allegorical Frame: Rizal’s narrator notes in paragraph 3: “...the Ship of State, so the joke runs, also has a slow and heavy gait.” A single vessel maps the entire social anatomy of the Spanish colony.

Chapter I: On the Upper Deck — Colonial Power & Forced Labor

Unit 1 · Chapter I (1) · Pasig River · Upper Deck

Beneath comfortable canvas awnings sits the ruling caste of colonial Manila:

  • The Religious Orders: Padre Salví (the calculating Franciscan), Padre Sibyla (the elegant Dominican), Padre Camorra (the sensual curate), Padre Irene (the corrupt canon).
  • The Bureaucracy & Press: Don Custodio (arrogant colonial administrator) and Ben-Zayb (pretentious Spanish journalist).
  • The Social Pretender: Doña Victorina, the Filipina desperate to pass as European, abusing native boatmen.
  • The Provocateur: The jeweler Simoun, walking among them with icy cynicism.

The Labor Debate: When the steamer stalls, Don Custodio proposes forcing riverside villagers to raise ducks to dredge river snails.

Simoun’s Chilling Solution: Dig a brand-new straight canal through Manila, close the Pasig River, and draft all villagers, old men, and boys to work without wages with their own tools and food.

“The dead remain dead, and might only is considered right by posterity... Thus were constructed the Pyramids, Lake Moeris, and the Colosseum in Rome.” Simoun · Chapter I, On the Upper Deck
Plain-English Meaning: Simoun reveals that empires celebrate magnificent monuments while history erases the millions of unpaid, disposable laborers worked to death to build them.
Simoun's Method: Simoun does not advocate reform; he proposes the most brutal, despotic measures imaginable to push the population toward armed revolt.
07

Chapter II: On the Lower Deck — The Water and Steam Warning

Unit 1 · Chapter II (2) · Pasig River · Lower Deck

Down in the stifling, airless hold beside the cargo and roaring boilers are packed native Filipino passengers, Chinese merchants, and university students Basilio and Isagani, accompanied by the secular priest Father Florentino.

Basilio and Isagani lead a student petition to establish an Academy of Castilian, offering to fund teachers and classrooms with their own contributions.

Simoun descends below deck and mocks native docility: he claims Filipinos lack spirit because they drink only water instead of beer. Isagani delivers a prophetic counter-warning:

“Water is very sweet and can be drunk, but it can put out the fire of wine and beer... Water, when heated, becomes steam; when agitated, it becomes the ocean, which can rise and destroy.” Isagani to Simoun · Chapter II, On the Lower Deck
Plain-English Meaning: Isagani warns that while the Filipino people seem calm and submissive like quiet water, if colonial oppression heats them up, they will turn into destructive steam and a tidal wave of revolution.
Philosophical Tension: The novel establishes its central debate: the students’ peaceful faith in education versus the irresistible rising pressure of revolutionary steam.

Chapter III: Legends of the Pasig & Simoun’s Haunting Silence

Unit 1 · Chapter III (3) · Entering Laguna de Bay
Three River Legends

Folklore of the Pasig

1. Malapad-na-Bato: A sacred rock once revered by spirits, later turned into a bandits’ hideout—symbolizing distorted pre-colonial faith.

2. Doña Jerónima’s Cave: An Archbishop walled his lover inside a river cave to keep his clerical vows while indulging his passion—exposing religious hypocrisy.

3. Miracle of San Nicolás: A Chinese merchant spared when a crocodile turned to stone at his prayer—showing commercial accommodation of religion.

The Hidden Plot Trigger

The Waters of San Diego

As the steamer enters Laguna de Bay, Ben-Zayb asks where a fugitive named “Guevara, Navarro, or Ibarra” was killed thirteen years ago.

The captain points to the water, recounting how the fugitive dove under gunfire and the lake turned red with blood.

Simoun stands completely motionless, deathly pale, staring silently into the dark water where he was believed to have drowned.

Transition to Chapter IV: Leaving the steamer, the narrative turns inland to the town of Tiani to witness the real-world destruction of an honest peasant family.

Chapter IV: Cabesang Tales & The Dispossession of Land

Unit 2 · Chapter IV (4) · Tiani, Laguna · The Clearing

Telesforo (Cabesang Tales), the industrious son of old broom-maker Tandang Selo, clears dense virgin woodland with his family to cultivate sugar cane and rice.

During the grueling labor of clearing the soil, fevers carry off his wife and his eldest daughter, Lucia, in the prime of her youth. Yet no sooner does the land turn fertile than the nearby religious order claims ownership, demanding an annual rent.

Tales pays twenty to thirty pesos at first, but the friar estate steadily raises the rent to fifty, then doubles it, and finally demands two hundred pesos annually.

1
Clearing the Virgin Forest

Family fells trees through sickness and toil; fevers claim his wife and eldest daughter Lucia.

2
Friar Rent Extortion

Dominican estate claims land once fertile; rent escalates from 20 to 50, then to 200 pesos a year.

3
The Rigged Legal Battle

Tales spends his life savings in court. Judges concede he is in the right, but refuse to rule against the friars without written titles.

4
Disarming the Farmer

Son Tano is conscripted; authorities seize Tales’s shotgun, then his bolo, leaving him only an axe to guard his boundary.

5
Kidnapped by Bandits

Defenseless with only an axe, Tales is ambushed and held for a 500-peso ransom.

Historical Authenticity: Chapter IV directly documents the 1887–1891 Calamba Hacienda dispute where the Dominican estate raised rents and dispossessed the Rizal family. Tales exhausts all legal channels before violence overtakes him.

Chapters V & VIII: Christmas in San Diego & Tiani — Mute Sorrow

Unit 2 · Chapters V & VIII (5 & 8) · Christmas Eve & Christmas Morning
Chapter V (5) · A Coachman’s Christmas Eve

Sinong’s Beating

Basilio arrives in San Diego by carriage to spend Christmas. His coachman Sinong is twice beaten by the Civil Guard—first for forgetting his tax card (cédula), and second because his carriage lamp blew out.

Arriving at Capitan Tiago’s home, Basilio learns that Tano has been conscripted into the military and Cabesang Tales is held captive in the mountains.

Chapter VIII (8) · Merry Christmas

Juli’s Servitude & Tandang Selo’s Silence

Christmas morning in Tiani: to raise the remaining 250 pesos for her father’s ransom, Juli sells all her jewelry except María Clara’s emerald locket, and enters domestic servitude under Hermana Penchang.

Finding himself completely alone, his son captive, his grandson conscripted, and his granddaughter enslaved, seventy-year-old Tandang Selo suffers a stroke and is struck completely mute (pipi).

Classroom Contrast: Rizal contrasts the shallow colonial celebration of Christ’s birth with the real-life suffering, terror, and mute grief inflicted upon innocent Filipino families.

Chapters VI & VII: Basilio & Simoun — The Nocturnal Confrontation

Unit 2 · Chapters VI & VII (6 & 7) · Christmas Eve Midnight · San Diego Forest
Basilio Medical Student · The Survivor

Background (Ch. 6): Orphaned son of Sisa who rose from Capitan Tiago’s barefoot servant and neglected Letrán student to top clinician at the Ateneo.

His Life Goal: Graduate as a physician, marry Juli, heal suffering individuals, and survive quietly through professional discipline.

“I only want to practice medicine, cure the sick, ransom Juli, and live in peace... Revenge cannot bring back my mother and brother.”
Simoun Crisóstomo Ibarra Unmasked · The Destroyer

The Revelation (Ch. 7): Caught digging near the balete tree by Sisa’s grave, Simoun pulls off his blue goggles. He admits he could kill Basilio, but offers an alliance.

His Doctrine: Accelerate corruption to force the colony to explode into armed revolt. He condemns Basilio’s neutrality as cowardice.

“You hold aloof from your people... Neutrality under tyranny is complicity. You resign yourself to slavery!”
Classroom Debate Prompt
Is Basilio’s desire to focus on his own career and heal individual patients a legitimate, honorable personal choice—or is Simoun right that pursuing private comfort and neutrality under an oppressive regime is an illusion? Where does moral responsibility lie?
Central Conflict: Two survivors of the same 1887 tragedy choose opposite answers: one chooses quiet healing through science; the other chooses fiery vengeance through subversion.

Chapters IX & X: The Revolver for the Locket & The Murders of Tiani

Unit 2 · Chapters IX & X (9 & 10) · Tiani · Usurped Land

Pilates (Ch. 9): The friar administrator, the Civil Guard lieutenant, and Hermana Penchang wash their hands of guilt, claiming Tales brought ruin upon himself through pride.

Wealth and Misery (Ch. 10): Ransomed by Juli’s servitude, Tales returns to find his house occupied, his daughter enslaved, and a 3-day eviction notice served.

Simoun arrives at Tales’s home to sell jewels. When Tales shows him María Clara’s gold-and-emerald locket, Simoun desperately offers 500 pesos or any diamond. Looking out the window, Tales sees the friar administrator and the new tenant walking his stolen fields, laughing together.

The Murders of Tiani

The Fatal Exchange & Matanglawin

In the dead of night, Tales slips away. He leaves María Clara’s locket inside Simoun’s empty revolver holster and takes Simoun’s loaded weapon.

At dawn, three bodies are discovered on the disputed land: the friar administrator, the new tenant, and his wife, their mouths stuffed with soil. A note written in blood reads: “Cabesang Tales”.

Tales takes to the mountains to command the peasant guerrilla uprising under the name Matanglawin.

Classroom Debate Prompt
Why did Tales stuff the victims’ mouths with soil before killing them? At what exact point did his fight for justice transform into bloody vengeance, and was any peaceful resolution possible once the courts refused to uphold the law?
Causal Ignition: The state’s failure to protect honest labor drives an ordinary citizen into becoming the colony’s most feared guerrilla leader, directly igniting the military crisis in Chapter XI.

Chapter XI: Los Baños — Arbitrary Governance

Unit 3 · Chapter XI (11) · Los Baños, Laguna

At the hot springs of Los Baños, the Captain-General, friars (Sibyla, Irene, Camorra), and bureaucrat Don Custodio play cards (tresillo) while casually deciding administrative decrees:

  • Firearms Ban: Simoun wins blank deportation warrants and supports banning hunting rifles to disarm natives.
  • Silenced Teacher: A village schoolmaster asking for a roof for his classroom is suspended for complaining.
  • The Students’ Petition: The Academy of Castilian splits the table. Sibyla fears rebellion; Irene defends it for bribes. The General puts Basilio on the police watchlist.
Padre Camorra’s Condition

Juli’s Imprisoned Grandfather

When petitions for the release of mute Tandang Selo are raised, Padre Camorra agrees to advocate for the old man—on one condition: that Selo’s granddaughter, Juli, come to his convent in Tiani to beg him in person.

Causal Link: The Captain-General refers the student petition to Don Custodio, moving the action to the universities of Manila.

Chapter XII: Placido Penitente and the Frustration of Youth

Unit 3 · Chapter XII (12) · University of Santo Tomas, Manila

Placido Penitente (“Quiet Penitent”), a diligent student from Batangas, walks toward the University of Santo Tomas filled with despair and contemplating abandoning his education entirely.

Outside the university gates, he encounters Juanito Pelaez, a wealthy, superficial mestizo student and friar pet who boasts of spending vacations carousing with Padre Camorra.

Placido is pressured into signing a petition he has had no time to read, while students are herded into class like cattle, subjected to daily racial intimidation and contempt by Spanish clerical professors.

Psychological Anatomy

The Smoldering Native Mind

Placido represents thousands of peaceful Filipino students who arrived in Manila with thirst for learning, only to find themselves insulted, treated as natural inferiors, and pushed toward quiet despair.

His surname, Penitente, reflects his patient endurance—until the systemic humiliation inside the lecture hall breaks his patience forever in Chapter XIII.

Institutional Rot: Rizal demonstrates how colonial universities crushed the natural curiosity and self-respect of Filipino youths, making education an instrument of subjugation rather than enlightenment.

Chapter XIII: The Physics Classroom — Instruments Behind Glass

Unit 3 · Chapter XIII (13) · University of Santo Tomas Physics Lecture Hall
The Setting & Apparatus

Instruments Kept Under Lock & Key

A spacious hall with rows of tiered wooden benches and a grand glass cabinet filled with expensive physics apparatus imported from Europe.

The Reality: The instruments are never touched, calibrated, or used for experiments. They are kept locked away like museum relics, polished only to impress visiting Spanish dignitaries and demonstrate European superiority.

The Pedagogical Method

Scholastic Rote & Humiliation

Dominican professor Padre Millón conducts physics entirely through scholastic memory tricks and abstract Latin metaphysics.

Rather than teaching science, Millón taunts native students, demanding they repeat word-for-word definitions of mirrors while forbidding them to understand practical mechanics.

Placido’s Breaking Point

Defiance & Rupture with Colonial Education

When Millón insults Placido, marks him absent for 15 days, and calls him an insolent “philosophaster,” Placido reaches his absolute limit:

“Enough, Padre, enough! Your Reverence may mark me with all the faults you please, but you have no right to insult me!” Placido Penitente to Padre Millón · Chapter XIII

Placido drops his books on the floor and storms out of the university forever. Disillusioned with peaceful progress, he is ripe for Simoun’s revolutionary conspiracy in Chapter XIX.

Pedagogical Indictment: Rizal delivers one of the most blistering scenes in world literature, indicting colonial schooling as a deliberate system of intellectual castration.

Chapters XIV & XV: Student Idealism vs. Selfish Careerism

Unit 3 · Chapters XIV & XV (14 & 15) · Manila · Makaraig’s House & Pasta’s Office

In the House of the Students (Ch. 14): Student leaders gather at wealthy Makaraig’s house. Spanish student Sandoval champions liberal equality; Pecson cynically predicts friar arrests; Isagani resolves to lobby Manila’s foremost native lawyer, Señor Pasta.

Señor Pasta’s Counsel (Ch. 15): Pasta urges Isagani to drop political petitions, submit to the friars, study medicine, marry, and leave governance to Spain: “Study, pass your exams, marry a rich girl, and let the government govern.”

“When I have gray hairs like yours, sir, and look back upon my past and see that I worked only for myself... every hair will be a thorn, and instead of glorying in them, I shall blush for them.” Isagani to Señor Pasta · Chapter XV
Plain-English Meaning: Isagani tells the veteran lawyer that achieving comfortable professional success while abandoning one’s country is shameful, not admirable.
Classroom Debate Prompt
Señor Pasta argues that a young professional should keep quiet, build a comfortable career, and not jeopardize his future by challenging powerful institutions. Isagani counters that personal prosperity without civic courage is cowardly. How does this ethical tension resonate in society today?
Diverging Paths: While student idealists exhaust peaceful legal advocacy, Simoun secures the clandestine weapons for his armed insurrection.

Chapters XVI – XVIII: Smuggled Rifles and the Talking Head

Unit 4 · Chapters XVI – XVIII (16–18) · Escolta & Quiapo Fair

Quiroga’s Tribulations (Ch. 16): Chinese merchant Quiroga hosts a lavish banquet for colonial elites. In debt to Simoun for 9,000 pesos, Quiroga agrees to let Simoun hide smuggled crates of German rifles in his Escolta warehouse in exchange for debt relief.

The Quiapo Fair (Ch. 17): Manila society visits the festive Quiapo fair, viewing terracotta figurines that mock colonial types and friar gluttony.

The Sphinx (Ch. 18): American illusionist Mr. Leeds presents a talking mummy head, Imuthis, at the fair. The head recounts an ancient Egyptian murder by an envious priest over a beloved maiden—an unmistakable allegorical reenactment of Padre Salví framing Ibarra over María Clara. Padre Salví faints in terror.

Double Operation

Arms and Psychological Warfare

Simoun operates on two simultaneous fronts:

Physical Force: Smuggling German rifles into the commercial heart of Manila to arm both mountain bandits and native conspirators.

Psychological Terror: Using Leeds’s sphinx to publicly break Padre Salví’s nerve, proving that colonial guilt is ripe for retribution.

Simoun’s Apparatus: Simoun exploits international commerce and colonial corruption to smuggle the weapons that will arm his first planned uprising.

Chapters XIX & XXI: The Clandestine Workshop and the Operetta

Unit 4 · Chapters XIX & XXI (19 & 21) · Trozo & Teatro de Variedades

The Fuse is Set (Ch. 19): Placido Penitente, raging after walking out of Santo Tomas, encounters Simoun. Simoun takes him to a secluded shack in Trozo where pyrotechnist maestro Castor is manufacturing bombs and cartridges for the uprising.

Manila Types (Ch. 21): The elite gather at Teatro de Variedades to see a scandalous French operetta troupe (Les Cloches de Corneville). Outside, disguised outlaws and soldiers patrol the alleys under Simoun’s orders.

The entire social order sits inside the theater, completely unaware that dynamite and guerrilla bands surround the city.

Dramatic Irony

The Decadent City on the Brink

While the friars and high officials obsess over whether the French dancers’ costumes are immoral, Simoun has positioned armed cadres across the capital.

The scene captures the blind complacency of the colonial regime on the very eve of scheduled destruction.

Plot Convergence: Placido’s academic outrage is funneled directly into Simoun’s armed revolutionary machine.

Chapters XX & XXII–XXIV: Don Custodio's Decision & Aborted Revolt

Unit 4 · Chapters XX & XXII–XXIV (20 & 22–24) · Manila

The Arbitrator (Ch. 20): Don Custodio solves the student petition with a bureaucratic sham: approve the Academy, but place it under Dominican Dominican friar supervision.

A Corpse (Ch. 23): Basilio tends dying, opium-addicted Capitan Tiago at home. Simoun enters and reveals his plan: at the signal of a cannon shot, his allies will unleash the mob, seize the city, and storm the Santa Clara convent to rescue María Clara.

Basilio delivers devastating news: “María Clara is dead. She died at six o’clock this afternoon.”

Simoun lets out a terrible cry and flees into the night, completely paralyzed. The planned revolt is instantly aborted.

Psychological Collapse

The False Engine of Revolution

Simoun’s grand political conspiracy collapses because its true driving motive was never pure national liberation—it was personal rescue and revenge for María Clara.

With her death, the entire revolutionary machinery stalls, leaving the student reformers exposed to catastrophic colonial retaliation in Act III.

Turning Point of the Novel: María Clara’s death shatters Simoun’s first conspiracy and leaves the innocent student reform movement vulnerable to mass arrest.

Chapters XXV & XXVI: The Student Banquet & The Pasquinade Panic

Unit 5 · Chapters XXV & XXVI (25 & 26) · Panciteria Macanista & Santo Tomas

Laughter and Tears (Ch. 25): Furious at Don Custodio’s mockery of their petition, fourteen students hold a satirical banquet at the Panciteria Macanista de Buen Gusto. They mock friars in toasts, unaware that Padre Salví’s spies are watching from the shadows.

Pasquinades (Ch. 26): The next morning, handwritten posters (pasquinades) advocating subversion are mysteriously discovered on university doors.

The colonial government erupts in hysterical panic. The university is closed, troops patrol the streets, and student leaders are arrested wholesale. Basilio, arriving innocently to request clinical funds, is arrested alongside Makaraig.

Provocation & Entrapment

Who Placed the Posters?

The students are universally innocent of treason. The seditious posters were planted either by friar agents to crush student reform, or by Simoun’s operatives to foment unrest.

Regardless of origin, the state seizes the pretext to unleash total police terror against educated Filipino youth.

State Paranoia: The regime responds to harmless youthful satire with mass incarcerations, driving the moderates toward radical despair.
21

Chapters XXVII & XXVIII: Intellectual Defiance & Citywide Panic

Unit 5 · Chapters XXVII & XXVIII (27 & 28) · Santo Tomas & Manila

The Friar and the Filipino (Ch. 27): Summoned by Dominican rector Padre Fernández, Isagani conducts an extraordinary, dignified intellectual debate. When Fernández claims Spain gave the Philippines religion and civilization, Isagani answers:

“When a student pays his fees and receives only insults, he has a right to complain... You say we are ungrateful; but what you teach us is to be hypocrites and slaves.”

Tatakut (Ch. 28): Wild rumors consume Manila—threats of poison, native riots, and nighttime executions. Capitan Tiago dies of terrified shock; Padre Irene forges his will, disinheriting Basilio.

A Rare Honest Dialogue

The Dignity of the Colonized Mind

Padre Fernández is one of the few friars portrayed with intellectual seriousness. Yet even he admits the Dominican Order cannot reform without collapsing its authority.

Isagani demonstrates that the new generation of educated Filipinos can no longer be silenced by religious dogma or racial paternalism.

Intellectual Rupture: Even the most enlightened Spanish churchmen cannot defend the colonial status quo against principled Filipino reason.
22

Chapters XXIX–XXXI: Juli’s Martyrdom & The Resigned Official

Unit 5 · Chapters XXIX–XXXI (29–31) · Tiani Convent & Governor’s Palace

Juli’s Agony (Ch. 30): While wealthy students buy their freedom, Basilio remains imprisoned without influential patrons. Hermana Bali pressures Juli to seek Padre Camorra’s help.

Terrified of Camorra’s known lechery, Juli resists, but finally enters the Tiani convent to save Basilio.

That afternoon, screams echo from the convent. A woman’s body crashes through a second-story window onto the stones below: Juli has leaped to her death to protect her honor. Mute Tandang Selo beats on the convent doors in howling grief, then vanishes into the hills.

The High Official (Ch. 31): A decent Spanish official pleads with the Governor-General to free innocent Basilio. The Governor refuses; the official tenders his resignation: “When a country is denied light, justice, and liberty, the citizen treats the government as a robber.”

The Moral Abyss

Sacrifice of the Pure

Juli’s death represents the complete moral bankruptcy of colonial friar rule. The pure, devoted native woman is driven to suicide by the very representative of the Church.

Her death completely shatters Basilio’s belief in peaceful survival, turning him into Simoun’s willing instrument of vengeance.

Tragic Crucible: Juli’s suicide and the high official’s resignation prove that the colonial apparatus cannot be redeemed by good intentions.
23

Chapter XXXII: Broken Students & Paulita’s Choice

Unit 5 · Chapter XXXII (32) · Manila · Calle Anloague

The aftermath of the student terror crushes the youth movement:

  • The Dispersal: Makaraig flees to Europe; Pecson abandons his law degree to become a merchant clerk; Tadeo burns his textbooks with joy.
  • Paulita’s Desertion: Beautiful Paulita Gómez breaks her engagement with the disgraced, radical Isagani. She chooses security and social prestige, agreeing to marry the wealthy, obedient mestizo Juanito Pelaez.
  • Basilio Forgotten: Basilio remains rotting in a dark cell in Fort Santiago, penniless and abandoned.
Sociological Reflection

Leonor Rivera’s Parallel

Paulita’s choice directly mirrors Leonor Rivera marrying Henry Kipping in 1891 under parental pressure.

Rizal portrays how colonial society systematically rewards cowardice, wealth, and servility, while punishing patriotism, integrity, and intellectual ambition.

All Ties Broken: Every character is stripped of love, family, and hope—setting the stage for Simoun’s apocalyptic second conspiracy.

Chapter XXXIII: A Transformed Basilio Demands Arms

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXIII (33) · Simoun’s House on the Pasig

Released after months in prison through Simoun’s influence, a gaunt, hollow-eyed Basilio arrives at Simoun’s house.

He has lost everything: his medical career ruined, Capitan Tiago dead, his home gone, and Juli dead at the convent of Tiani. All his patient moderation has been scorched away:

“I was bad, I was blind... Now let there be war! You were right, Señor Simoun. Give me a weapon, let the destruction begin!” Basilio to Simoun · Chapter XXXIII
Simoun’s Masterpiece

The Pomegranate of Death

Simoun leads Basilio to his laboratory table. Upon it rests an exquisite, ornate kerosene table lamp fashioned in the shape of a golden pomegranate.

Inside its hidden hollow walls is not harmless lamp oil, but a lethal reservoir of liquid nitroglycerin.

The Final Conversion: The most moderate, peaceful character in the novel is driven by relentless colonial injustice into becoming the accomplice of an apocalyptic bombing.

The Centerpiece: The Nitroglycerin Pomegranate Lamp

Unit 6 · Technical Schematic & Operational Plan · Calle Anloague
Component 1

The Nitroglycerin Core

A double-walled reservoir containing a large charge of liquid nitroglycerin, concealed inside an elegant golden pomegranate centerpiece.

Component 2

The Delayed Trigger

The lamp burns ordinary alcohol for twenty minutes. As the flame dims, someone will turn up the wick, triggering a concealed friction capsule that detonates the nitro.

Component 3

The Underfloor Mines

Sacks of gunpowder hidden beneath the floors and roof beams of Calle Anloague, designed to bring down the entire mansion in a catastrophic collapse.

Simoun’s Battle Plan

The explosion will kill the Governor-General, the Archbishop, the high friars, and wealthy merchants in one strike. Hearing the blast, Simoun and Cabesang Tales’s guerrillas will storm the city, distribute rifles, and butcher any who refuse to fight.

Technical Anatomy: Rizal, with his scientific medical background, crafts a precise, devastating explosive mechanism that embodies technological terror.

Chapter XXXIV: The Mined Wedding on Calle Anloague

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXIV (34) · Calle Anloague (Capitan Tiago’s Former House)

The wedding feast of Juanito Pelaez and Paulita Gómez is held at Capitan Tiago’s former mansion on Calle Anloague—the very house where Crisóstomo Ibarra was welcomed in Chapter I of Noli Me Tángere.

Don Timoteo Pelaez has lavishly remodeled it with carpets, chandeliers, and mirrors. The Governor-General acts as the bridegroom’s godfather; high friars, judges, and military brass arrive in splendor.

Outside in the dark street, Basilio wanders back and forth in agonizing moral torment, watching the corrupt guests arrive, knowing they are walking into a tomb.

Spatial Irony

The Ground Zero of Two Novels

Rizal deliberately stages the apocalypse at Calle Anloague:

Where the saga began in 1887 with joyous hospitality and romantic hope, it now prepares to conclude in fire, gunpowder, and wholesale slaughter.

Atmospheric Tension: The claustrophobic suspense peaks as the colonial elite dines directly above sacks of black powder.

Chapter XXXV: Mene, Tekel, Phares & The Thrown Lamp

Unit 6 · Chapter XXXV (35) · The Dining Room on Calle Anloague

At eight o’clock, Simoun delivers the golden pomegranate lamp to the dining table, bids farewell to the Governor-General, and leaves the house.

Outside, Basilio encounters Isagani staring heartbroken at the lighted windows. Basilio warns him: “Flee! The house is going to explode!”

Inside, a parchment note circulates among the guests:

“Mene, Tekel, Phares.”
— Crisóstomo Ibarra

Padre Salví recognizes Ibarra’s handwriting and trembles in terror. The lamp’s flame begins to flicker. As Padre Irene reaches to turn up the wick, a figure bursts through the door, seizes the lamp, rushes to the veranda, and hurls it into the dark waters of the Pasig River.

Isagani’s Fatal Choice

Love Defeats the Bomb

Isagani knew that the explosion would kill his beloved Paulita, even though she had betrayed him to marry another.

By saving the woman he loved, Isagani unintentionally disarms the revolution and saves the lives of the colonial tyrants who oppressed his people.

Climactic Pivot: The revolution fails not because of military power, but because human love and instinct overcome cold, calculated terrorist ideology.

Teaching Analysis: The Supreme Irony of the Climax

Unit 6 · Literary & Moral Analysis of Chapter XXXV
The Two Flaws of Simoun’s Terror

Why the Conspiracy Collapsed

1. The Vanity of the Calling Card: Simoun could not resist signing his own name to the biblical death sentence (Mene, Tekel, Phares). The delay caused by the circulating note created the exact window needed for Isagani to act.

2. The Disregard for Human Love: Simoun treated human beings as abstract pieces on a chessboard. He underestimated the irrational, self-sacrificing power of genuine love.

The Tragic Irony

Saved by the Very Victim

The Spanish Governor-General, Padre Salví, and Padre Irene were saved from fiery death by an impoverished native youth whom they had insulted and oppressed.

Rizal constructs the climax so that virtue preserves life, but simultaneously leaves the tyrannical regime alive to inflict further suffering.

Moral Center: Rizal proves that vengeance can destroy, but it cannot redeem. A movement born of hatred inevitably produces self-defeating contradictions.

Chapters XXXVI & XXXVII: Silenced Presses & Santa Cruz Secrets

Unit 7 · Chapters XXXVI & XXXVII (36 & 37) · Manila & Santa Cruz District

Ben-Zayb’s Afflictions (Ch. 36): Journalist Ben-Zayb writes a sensational, heroizing article praising the Governor-General’s courage during the lamp incident. The Governor strictly censors and suppresses it, terrified that news of the bomb will expose the regime’s vulnerability.

Meanwhile, the betrayed mountain bandits—unsupported by Simoun’s aborted uprising—attack a wealthy friar villa on the Pasig. Captured outlaws confess that their leader was the jeweler Simoun.

Civil guards raid Simoun’s mansion, finding huge caches of gunpowder and weapons. Simoun has fled.

The Mystery (Ch. 37): In the silversmith district of Santa Cruz, Chichoy and Momoy whisper the terrifying truth: sacks of gunpowder were found beneath the dining room floor. Isagani listens quietly, knowing what he prevented, and leaves Manila forever.

Censorship & Disclosure

The Collapse of the Masquerade

The colonial state attempts to silence the press and bury the story, but oral gossip in the streets spreads faster than printed newspapers.

Simoun’s dual identity as the Governor-General’s confidant and the chief conspirator is revealed, making him the most hunted fugitive in the archipelago.

The State Unravels: The regime cannot acknowledge how close it came to extinction without destroying its own authority.

Chapter XXXVIII: Fatality — Fratricide in the Hills

Unit 7 · Chapter XXXVIII (38) · The Mountain Pass of Luzon

A military column of Spanish Civil Guards and native conscript soldiers marches through rough mountain crags, escorting barefoot peasant prisoners under brutal beatings.

Guerrillas ambush the column from high clifftops. The conscript soldier Carolino is ordered to return fire at a lone figure standing atop the highest rock, brandishing a spear.

Carolino aims his rifle, fires, and drops the man. Climbing the cliff, Carolino discovers that the dying guerrilla he has just shot is his own grandfather, the mute Tandang Selo.

Selo points a dying finger at the boy, gazing at him with eyes full of sorrow, recognizing his grandson Tano.

The Ultimate Tragedy

Colonial Fratricide

Rizal’s most heartbreaking indictment of colonial subjugation:

Spain conscripts native sons into uniforms, hands them rifles, and forces them to slaughter their own fathers and grandfathers who are fighting for their ancestral land.

The family of Cabesang Tales is completely extinguished: wife and daughter dead to fevers, Juli dead to suicide, Tales an outlaw, and Selo killed by his own grandson.

The Cost of Empire: The colonial machine forces colonized brothers to kill one another to preserve imperial mastery.

Chapter XXXIX: The Fugitive’s Poison & Final Confession

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX (39) · Father Florentino’s House by the Pacific

Wounded and pursued by military telegrams, Simoun arrives at the lonely coastal retreat of the retired Filipino secular priest, Father Florentino.

Learning that the Civil Guard will surround the house by nightfall to arrest him alive, Simoun drinks a lethal dose of slow-acting poison.

He calls Father Florentino to his bedside, reveals his true identity as Crisóstomo Ibarra, and pours out the full confession of his thirteen years of bitter wanderings, his accumulated diamond fortune, and his failed plots to incinerate the colony.

The Dying Cry

Simoun’s Anguished Question

Dying in agony, Simoun asks why God abandoned his righteous cause:

“Why has God refused me His aid? Is not God justice? Can He desire that a people be held in eternal slavery?” Simoun to Father Florentino · Chapter XXXIX
The Novel's Climax of Ideas: Simoun’s tragic deathbed sets up the crowning philosophical dialogue of Rizal’s entire literary work.
32

Father Florentino’s Dialogue: Liberty, Virtue & The Youth

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX (39) · The Moral Philosophy of Rizal

Father Florentino takes the dying man’s hand and explains why God thwarted the conspiracy:

“No, God is justice and cannot abandon His cause... But the glory of saving a country cannot be won by those who have helped to corrupt it. Pure and spotless must the victim be that the sacrifice may be acceptable!” Father Florentino · Chapter XXXIX

Florentino explains that hatred breeds only monsters, and crime produces only slaves. Freedom must be won through moral elevation, education, work, and love:

“Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?” Father Florentino · Chapter XXXIX
The Invocation to the Youth

Where Are the Youth?

Florentino turns toward the window, calling out to future generations of Filipinos:

“Where are the youth who will consecrate their golden hours, their illusions, and their enthusiasm to the welfare of their native land?... Where are you, youth, who will embody in yourselves the vigor of life... We await you, O youth! Come, for we await you!”

Classroom Debate Prompt
Father Florentino insists that a free nation cannot be built on crime, bribery, and hatred: “Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?” Do you agree that the moral methods of a liberation movement determine whether real freedom is achieved, or was Simoun’s radical ruthlessness necessary against an entrenched colonial power?
The Moral Testament: True emancipation cannot be gifted by foreign powers or stolen by violent terrorism; it must be cultivated through moral civic virtue and education.

The Jewel Chest in the Depths of the Sea

Unit 8 · Chapter XXXIX (39) · The Pacific Ocean Coastline
Father Florentino hurling Simoun’s cursed treasures into the Pacific abyss

Simoun breathes his last. Father Florentino kneels, offers a solemn prayer for the tormented soul, and picks up Simoun’s heavy steel chest filled with diamonds and gems.

He climbs the lonely ocean cliff, stares down into the boundless waves of the Pacific, and hurls the cursed treasure into the dark abyss:

“May nature guard you in the deep abysses among the corals and pearls... When men need you for a holy and sublime purpose, God will draw you from the breast of the waves. Meanwhile, there you will do no evil, you will not distort justice, nor incite greed!” Father Florentino · Final Lines of the Novel
Finis: The novel concludes with the rejection of corrupt material wealth in favor of moral, spiritual purification.

Comparative Analysis: Noli Me Tángere vs. El Filibusterismo

Synthesis · The Two Masterpieces Compared · 1887 vs. 1891
Dimension Noli Me Tángere (Berlin, 1887) El Filibusterismo (Ghent, 1891)
Literary Tone Romantic, satirical, lyrical, hopeful reformism. Dark, political, clinical, tragic, urgent dissection of revolution.
Central Protagonist Crisóstomo Ibarra: European-educated liberal reformer. Simoun: Disguised, cynical financier and nihilistic subversive.
Core Mechanism Building a modern schoolhouse to awaken minds. Using a nitroglycerin bomb and weapons to blow up the state.
Dedication To the Fatherland (A Mi Patria). To the martyred priests Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora (Gomburza).
Primary Question Can enlightened Spanish reform cure the cancer? Can violent revolution born of corruption create true liberty?
Comparative Arc: Noli is a diagnosis of colonial sickness; Fili is a severe moral warning that revenge cannot build a free nation.

The 1896 Revolution & Republic Act No. 1425

Legacy · Historical Impact & National Legislation · 1896 & 1956
Historical Impact · 1896

The Katipunan & Bagumbayan

Although Rizal warned against premature, unorganized revolt in Fili, Andrés Bonifacio’s Katipunan adopted “Rizal” as their secret password.

On December 30, 1896, Spanish authorities executed Rizal by firing squad at Bagumbayan—on the very ground where Gomburza was martyred in 1872.

Legislative Mandate · June 12, 1956

Republic Act No. 1425 (The Rizal Law)

Championed by Senator Claro M. Recto and sponsored by José P. Laurel, RA 1425 mandates that all public and private universities include unexpurgated editions of Noli and Fili in their curricula.

Despite fierce opposition from the Catholic hierarchy, the law ensures that every Filipino student confronts Rizal’s moral questions on freedom, tyranny, and civic responsibility.

Living Heritage: Rizal’s masterpiece remains required study across the Philippines to foster critical civic consciousness.
