## PART I: Historical Context, Authorial Motivation & Publication (`#context`)

### Beat `01` — The Word *Filibustero* (`Outline I.A.1`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Left: Main explanation & Blumentritt quote → Right: 3-stage Etymological Evolution (`1. 17th C. Antilles` → `2. 1887 Colonial Manila` → `3. 1912 Derbyshire Edition`).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "Pressing forward to **Beat 01**, let's examine the title. In the 17th century, a *filibustero* was a Caribbean pirate. By the late 19th century in the Philippines, Spanish colonial authorities weaponized the word to brand anyone suspected of subverting Spanish rule. Writing to Ferdinand Blumentritt in 1887, Rizal explained that educated Filipinos feared the word like a death sentence: *'It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged.'* While Charles Derbyshire's 1912 English translation popularized the title *The Reign of Greed*, *El Filibusterismo* literally means *The Subversion*."

### Beat `02` — 17 February 1872 · The Gomburza Dedication (`Outline I.A.2–3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Monumental `17 February 1872` banner → Left: Rizal's 1891 Dedication excerpt → Right: Archival Corrections Ledger (NHCP / Ambeth Ocampo).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 02**, Rizal dedicates the novel to **Gomburza**—Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora—the three Filipino secular priests executed by garrote at Bagumbayan after the 1872 Cavite Mutiny. As shown in the archival ledger on the right, because Rizal wrote from memory in Europe without access to colonial archives, he misremembered the date as February 28 instead of **February 17, 1872**, and listed their ages as 85, 30, and 35 instead of **72–73, 35, and 36–37**. Yet dedicating his book to executed 'traitors' was a direct challenge to colonial authority."

### Beat `03` — Three Crises That Darkened the Sequel (`Outline I.B.1–3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** 3 numbered columns (`I. Calamba`, `II. Madrid`, `III. Dagupan`).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 03**, why did Rizal's tone darken so sharply between 1887 and 1891? Follow the three columns from left to right:
  > 1. **First (`I`), The Calamba Agrarian Dispute:** Dominican friars hiked land rentals and Governor-General Valeriano Weyler sent troops to evict Rizal's own family and demolish their homes.
  > 2. **Second (`II`), Fractures in Madrid:** Leadership disputes with Marcelo H. del Pilar over *La Solidaridad* convinced Rizal that peaceful lobbying in Spain was failing and the struggle had to return to the Philippines.
  > 3. **Third (`III`), The Loss of Leonor Rivera:** Late in 1890, Rizal learned that Leonor Rivera was set to marry British engineer Henry Kipping—a personal loss echoed in Paulita Gómez abandoning Isagani for Juanito Pelaez."

### Beat `04` — Halted at Page 112: The Ghent Printing Struggle (`Outline I.C.1–3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** 4-step horizontal timeline (`Step 1: Calamba/London` → `Step 2: Biarritz` → `Step 3: Ghent Page 112` → `Step 4: Valentin Ventura's Rescue`).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 04**, we trace the four-step composition and printing struggle:
  > - **Step 1:** Begun in Calamba in October 1887 and revised across London, Paris, and Madrid.
  > - **Step 2:** Completed in Biarritz on March 29, 1891, before Rizal moved to Ghent, Belgium, for cheaper printing at the **F. Meyer-Van Loo Press**.
  > - **Step 3:** Pawning his jewels and rationing food with roommate José Alejandrino, Rizal ran out of money on **August 6, 1891**, halting the press at page 112.
  > - **Step 4:** **Valentin Ventura** sent emergency funds from Paris, allowing the book to come off the press on **September 18, 1891**."

## PART II.A: Act I — The Pasig, Tiani, and the Forest (`#act-1`, Chapters 1–10)

### Beat `05` — Aboard the *Bapor Tabo* & The Return of Simoun (`Outline II.A.1–2`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Top: Plate I (`bapor-tabo.jpg`) → Below: `1. Upper Deck` (Left) vs. `2. Lower Deck` (Right).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "Moving to **Beat 05**, Act I opens aboard the steamer **Bapor Tabo** on the Pasig River—a circular, dirty vessel painted white to pretend it is modern, symbolizing the stagnant colonial state.
  > - Look at **Column 1 on the left (The Upper Deck):** Shaded under awnings sit the friars, Don Custodio, Ben Zayb, and Doña Victorina. Here we meet **Simoun**—Crisóstomo Ibarra disguised as a wealthy jeweler—who coldly proposes digging a canal using unpaid forced labor, citing the pyramids of Egypt.
  > - Look at **Column 2 on the right (The Lower Deck):** Down beside the furnace sit the *indios*, Chinese traders, and students **Basilio** and **Isagani**. When Simoun mocks Filipinos as water-drinkers, Isagani warns that water heated by fire becomes steam—and ruffled, becomes an ocean that can swallow humanity."

### Beat `06` — The Clearing at Tiani: Cabesang Tales & Juli (`Outline II.A.3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Left: 6-step Agrarian Escalation Ladder (`1. 2 Graves` → `2. 20–30 Pesos` → `3. 50 Pesos` → `4. 200 Pesos` → `5. 500-Peso Ransom` → `6. 1 Revolver`) → Right: Narrative & Character Plinths.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 06**, Rizal dramatizes the Calamba evictions through **Cabesang Tales**. Follow the six-step escalation ladder on the left:
  > 1. Tales clears virgin forest, burying his wife and daughter Lucia in the soil.
  > 2. Dominican friars claim the land at **20 to 30 pesos** rent.
  > 3. The rent rises to **50 pesos**, and Tales is made tax-collector.
  > 4. When rent hits **200 pesos**, Tales demands a title deed, loses in court, and is disarmed.
  > 5. Kidnapped by bandits for a **500-peso ransom** after his son Tano is conscripted, Tales is freed only because his daughter **Juli** enters domestic servitude under Hermana Penchang on Christmas Day.
  > 6. Finally, in Chapter 10, evicted from his home, Tales trades María Clara's locket for Simoun's revolver, kills the friar administrator and the new tenant, and flees to the hills as the outlaw **Matanglawin**."

### Beat `07` — Two Men by a Grave: Assimilation vs. Revolution (`Outline II.A.4 & IV.A.1`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Two-page open folio: `1. Simoun's Critique of Hispanization` (Left) vs. `2. Basilio's Choice of Clinical Science` (Right).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 07** (Chapters 6–7), Basilio visits his mother Sisa's grave on Christmas Eve and discovers Simoun's true identity. This sets up the novel's first great ideological clash:
  > - **On the left (`1`), Simoun** attacks the students' petition for a Spanish Academy, warning that Castilian will never be the national language and that Hispanization will only subordinate Filipino minds and make them slaves.
  > - **On the right (`2`), Basilio** refuses violent revolution, placing his faith in clinical medicine and peaceful progress—until colonial injustice destroys his life in Act III."

## PART II.B: Act II — Reformist Idealism vs. Colonial Decay (`#act-2`, Chapters 11–24)

### Beat `08` — The Class in Physics: Instruments Behind Glass (`Outline II.B.1–2 & IV.B`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Left: UST Physics Amphitheater & Placido Penitente → Right: `Father Millon's Roll Book` (`15 Absences`, `"Absent in Spirit"`, `Placido's Walkout`).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 08**, Act II moves to Manila. In Chapter 13, **'The Class in Physics'** at the University of Santo Tomas, laboratory instruments are locked behind glass cabinets strictly to impress foreign visitors. As shown in the roll book on the right, **Father Millon** marks Batangas student **Placido Penitente** with **15 absences** for missing 3 days and mocks him until Placido throws down his book, walks out, and joins Simoun's plot—illustrating how colonial education crushes independent thought."

### Beat `09` — The Student Petition: Señor Pasta & Don Custodio (`Outline II.B.3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Two-page open folio: `1. Señor Pasta (Ch. 15)` on the Left vs. `2. Don Custodio (Ch. 20)` on the Right.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 09**, Isagani and Macaraig try to win approval for their Castilian Academy through legal channels, hitting two bureaucratic walls:
  > - **On the left (`1`), Señor Pasta**, the wealthy Filipino lawyer who tells Isagani to protect his own career and ignore the country—prompting Isagani's famous reply that *'every gray hair will be a thorn'* if he works only for himself.
  > - **On the right (`2`), Don Custodio**, the pretentious Spanish arbitrator whose 'compromise' hands the academy over to the religious orders, turning the students into mere fee-collectors."

### Beat `10` — Gun-Running, The Sphinx *Imuthis* & María Clara's Death (`Outline II.B.4–5`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Top: 3 numbered columns (`I. Quiroga's Debt`, `II. The Sphinx Imuthis`, `III. 6:00 PM at Santa Clara`) → Bottom: Dramatic 6:00 PM / 7:00 PM / 8:00 PM Timeline Banner.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 10**, three nocturnal events bring Act II to a climax:
  > 1. **In Chapter 16 (`I`):** Simoun reduces Chinese merchant **Quiroga's** 9,000-peso diamond debt by 2,000 pesos to hide crates of rifles in his warehouse.
  > 2. **In Chapters 17–18 (`II`):** At the Quiapo Fair, Mr. Leeds' talking sphinx head **Imuthis** recounts an ancient Egyptian tale of priestly betrayal that mirrors **Father Salví's** crime against Ibarra and María Clara so closely that Salví faints in terror.
  > 3. **In Chapter 23 (`III`):** At 7:00 PM, one hour before Simoun's regiments are set to attack Manila at 8:00 PM, Simoun tells Basilio his mission is to rescue **María Clara** from the Santa Clara convent—only for Basilio to reveal she died at **6:00 PM**. Heartbroken, Simoun abandons the uprising, proving his first revolt was driven by personal obsession."

## PART II.C: Act III — Betrayal, Repression & Radicalization (`#act-3`, Chapters 25–34)

### Beat `11` — The *Pasquinades* & Exit Captain Tiago (`Outline II.C.1–3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Two-page open folio: `1. The Banquet & Subversive Posters (Ch. 25–26)` (Left) vs. `2. Father Fernández & Captain Tiago's Will (Ch. 27–29)` (Right).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 11**, after fourteen students hold a satirical banquet at a Chinese noodle shop (Chapter 25), seditious placards (**pasquinades**) appear on the university doors (Chapter 26). Authorities arrest the student leaders, including Basilio, who wasn't even at the dinner.
  > - On the right (`2`), after Isagani debates the Jesuit **Father Fernández** on academic freedom (Chapter 27), the wealthy students buy or negotiate their release. Only poor Basilio remains in prison—and when **Captain Tiago dies**, Father Irene revokes Basilio's 25-peso bequest, leaving him penniless."

### Beat `12` — Chapter 30 · Juli at the Tiani Convent (`Outline II.C.3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Solitary centered stage in dark negative space.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 12** (Chapter 30), word reaches Tiani that Basilio is to be deported to the Carolines. Desperate to save him and pressured by Sister Bali and the town judge, **Juli** reluctantly enters the Tiani parish convent at dusk to plead with the predatory **Father Camorra**. That night, to escape Camorra's assault, Juli leaps from the convent window to her death—and at dawn, her mute grandfather Tandang Selo takes his hunting spear into the forest."

### Beat `13` — *La última razón*: Basilio's Radicalization & The Lamp (`Outline II.C.4 & IV.D.3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Left: Plate II (`crystal-lamp.jpg`) + Technical Specification Ledger → Right: Simoun's Escolta Laboratory Scene.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 13** (Chapters 33–34), bailed out of prison two months later and learning of Juli's death, Basilio abandons peaceful science and asks Simoun for a weapon. Look at **Plate II on the left**: Simoun unveils a pomegranate-shaped lamp filled with **two liters of liquid nitroglycerin**, calling it *'concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, and injustices.'* The lamp will be placed at the wedding banquet of Paulita Gómez and Juanito Pelaez at Captain Tiago's old mansion on Calle Anloague; when the flame dims after 20 minutes and someone raises the wick, a mercury fulminate cap will detonate the house and the entire colonial elite."

## PART II.D: Act IV — The Abortive Revolution & Theological Resolution (`#act-4`, Chapters 35–39)

### Beat `14` — *Mane, Thecel, Phares* & Isagani's Leap (`Outline II.D.1–2`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Top: Blood-red `Mane · Thecel · Phares` banner → Below: `1. Outside on Calle Anloague` (Left) vs. `2. Upstairs in the Dining Kiosk` (Right).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 14** (Chapter 35), at the wedding feast, Simoun leaves the nitroglycerin lamp along with a red-ink parchment bearing the biblical warning **'Mane, Thecel, Phares · Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra'** (*Measured, Weighed, Found Wanting*).
  > - **On the left (`1`), outside in the street:** Basilio spots his friend **Isagani** gazing up at Paulita's window and warns him to run because the house is mined.
  > - **On the right (`2`), upstairs in the dining kiosk:** Just as Father Irene reaches to turn up the dimming wick, Isagani—acting out of love to save Paulita rather than political calculation—rushes inside, seizes the lamp, and hurls it into the Pasig River, foiling the revolution."

### Beat `15` — Chapters 36–38 · Press Censorship & Carolino's Rifle (`Outline II.D.3`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Solitary centered stage.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 15**, while the Captain-General censors Ben Zayb's newspaper coverage of the wedding (Chapter 36), Chapter 38 takes us to a mountain pass where Filipino *Guardia Civil* soldiers are marching bound prisoners. A compassionate young soldier named **Carolino**—actually **Tano**, Cabesang Tales' conscripted son—is ordered at gunpoint by his corporal to shoot an old rebel on the rocks above. Carolino fires, only to discover as the dying man points silently at him that he has killed his own mute grandfather, **Tandang Selo**."

### Beat `16` — Chapter 39 · Simoun's Confession & Father Florentino's Verdict (`Outline II.D.4 & IV.A.2`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Top: Plate III (`final-ocean.jpg`) → Below: `1. Simoun's Confession` (Left) vs. `2. Father Florentino's Ethical Thesis` (Right).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 16** (Chapter 39), fatally wounded and having swallowed poison before the 8:00 PM arrest deadline, Simoun confesses his identity to **Father Florentino** by the Pacific Ocean.
  > - **On the left (`1`), Simoun** asks why God denied him aid while allowing the wicked to rule.
  > - **On the right (`2`), Father Florentino** delivers the ethical thesis of the novel: Simoun failed because he tried to cure corruption with further corruption and personal vengeance. Florentino warns: *'Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?'* After Simoun dies, Florentino casts Simoun's chest of jewels into the Pacific abyss until humanity needs it for a pure and holy purpose."

### Beat `17` — *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) vs. *El Filibusterismo* (1891) (`Outline V`)
* **Layout on Screen:** Full-width 4-row comparison table.
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "In **Beat 17**, we compare Rizal's two novels across four dimensions:
  > 1. **Tone & Genre:** *Noli* (1887) is a romantic, diagnostic 'Work of the Heart' dedicated to the Fatherland; *Fili* (1891) is a dark, dialectical 'Work of the Head' dedicated to Gomburza.
  > 2. **Protagonist's Arc:** Trusting reformist **Crisóstomo Ibarra** becomes the accelerationist conspirator **Simoun**.
  > 3. **Target of Critique:** *Noli* targets local friar abuses while hoping Madrid will reform the colony; *Fili* indicts the entire colonial structure—Madrid officials, friars, and complicit Filipinos alike.
  > 4. **Structure:** *Noli* spans 64 expansive chapters of town life; *Fili* is a tight 39-chapter political drama."

### Beat `18` — Historical Impact, Reception & Legacy (1891–1956) (`Outline VI.A–C`)
* **Layout on Screen:** 3 numbered columns (`I. Smuggling & Exile`, `II. The 1896 Revolution`, `III. Preservation & RA 1425`) + Closing Question & Reverse Book Outro (`#closing-book`).
* **Presenter Script:**
  > "Finally, in **Beat 18**, we trace the historical legacy of the book across three milestones:
  > 1. **Smuggling & Exile (`I`):** Shipped in crates from Ghent to **José María Basa** in Hong Kong in late 1891, copies smuggled into Manila were confiscated by Spanish customs, and subversive writings were cited when Rizal was deported to **Dapitan in July 1892**.
  > 2. **The Katipunan & 1896 Revolution (`II`):** Despite Father Florentino's call for patience, **Andrés Bonifacio** and the **Katipunan** read the collapse of the student academy and the tragedy of Cabesang Tales as proof that peaceful reform under Spain was dead.
  > 3. **Preservation & The Rizal Law (`III`):** The original 279-page manuscript was purchased from Valentin Ventura in **1925 for ₱10,000**, survived WWII and a brief 1961 theft at the National Library, and on **June 12, 1956**, **Republic Act No. 1425**—authored by **Sen. Claro M. Recto** and sponsored by **Sen. Jose P. Laurel**—mandated the study of unexpurgated editions across all Philippine schools and universities.
  >
