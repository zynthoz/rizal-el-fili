## Part I: Historical Context & Timeline (`#context`)

### Beat `01` — Meaning of the Title: *El Filibusterismo*
* **On Screen:** Left explanation + Right 3-stage etymological summary.
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "In the seventeenth century, *filibustero* meant a Caribbean pirate. By the late nineteenth century in the Philippines, colonial officials used it to brand anyone suspected of subverting Spanish rule. Writing to Ferdinand Blumentritt in 1887, Rizal explained that educated Filipinos feared the word like a death sentence: *'It means a dangerous patriot who will soon be hanged.'* Charles Derbyshire's 1912 English translation titled it *The Reign of Greed*, whereas the Spanish title literally means *The Subversion*."

### Beat `02` — Historical Background and Writing Timeline (1872–1891)
* **On Screen:** Vertical timeline from `17 Feb 1872` to `Aug – Sept 1891` with archival excerpts.
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "This timeline traces the four historical events that shaped the book:
  > 1. **February 17, 1872:** The execution of Filipino secular priests Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora at Bagumbayan. Working from memory in Europe, Rizal misremembered the date in his dedication as February 28 and misstated their ages (actual ages: 72–73, 35, and 36–37 per NHCP records).
  > 2. **1887–1891 (Calamba):** Rizal began the manuscript in Calamba while his family and fellow tenants faced rent hikes and military evictions on the Dominican hacienda under Governor-General Valeriano Weyler.
  > 3. **1890–1891 (Madrid & Dagupan):** Leadership divisions with Marcelo H. del Pilar over *La Solidaridad* convinced Rizal that lobbying in Spain had reached its limit, while Leonor Rivera's marriage to Henry Kipping informed the subplot of Paulita Gómez and Isagani.
  > 4. **August–September 1891 (Ghent):** After finishing the manuscript in Biarritz on March 29, 1891, Rizal moved to Ghent for lower printing costs at F. Meyer-Van Loo Press. On August 6, 1891, printing halted at page 112 when his money ran out, until Valentin Ventura sent funds from Paris to finish publication on September 18, 1891."

## Part II.A: Opening Chapters & The *Bapor Tabo* (`#act-1`, Ch. 1–10)

### Beat `03` — Chapters 1–2: Social Hierarchy Aboard the *Bapor Tabo* *(Interactive)*
* **Action:** Show the river steamer engraving centerpiece, then click through the **3 Deck Tabs** (`1. The Upper Deck` → `2. The Lower Deck` → `3. The Ship of State`).
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "The novel opens aboard the Pasig River steamer *Bapor Tabo*:
  > - *(Tab 1 — Upper Deck):* Shaded beneath awnings sit the Spanish friars, Don Custodio, Ben Zayb, Doña Victorina, and Simoun, who provokes the officials by proposing unpaid forced labor to dig a straight canal.
  > - *(Tab 2 — Lower Deck):* Crowded beside the hot boiler sit native *indios*, Chinese traders, and university students Basilio and Isagani. When Simoun remarks that Filipinos lack energy because they drink water, Isagani warns that water heated by fire turns into steam and a surging ocean.
  > - *(Tab 3 — The Ship of State):* Round like a dipper, painted white over dirt and rust, and pushed off sandbars with bamboo poles, the vessel mirrors the stagnant colonial administration."

### Beat `04` — Chapters 4 & 10: Cabesang Tales and the Hacienda Dispute
* **On Screen:** Left 5-step progression of rent/loss (`20–30` → `50` → `200 pesos` → `500-peso ransom`) + Right narrative.
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Cabesang Tales clears forest land at the cost of his wife and eldest daughter Lucia's lives, only for a friar corporation to claim the fields and raise rents from thirty to two hundred pesos. After colonial courts rule against him without a title deed and bandits kidnap him for 500 pesos, his daughter Juli enters domestic service under Hermana Penchang on Christmas Day to pay his ransom. Facing eviction in Chapter 10, Tales leaves María Clara's locket in Simoun's holster, takes Simoun's revolver, kills the friar administrator and new tenant, and joins the mountain guerrillas as *Matanglawin*."

### Beat `05` — Chapters 6–7: Basilio and Simoun in the San Diego Forest
* **On Screen:** Side-by-side comparison (`1. Simoun's Argument Against Assimilation` vs. `2. Basilio's Position`).
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "At Sisa's grave on Christmas Eve, Basilio recognizes Simoun as Ibarra. Simoun criticizes the students' campaign for a Spanish-language academy, arguing that Hispanization without political rights will only erase Filipino identity. Basilio declines to join the revolt, choosing instead to graduate in medicine and marry Juli."

## Part II.B: Colonial Education & Manila (`#act-2`, Ch. 11–24)

### Beat `06` — Chapter 13: The Physics Class at Santo Tomas
* **On Screen:** Left narrative + Right `Father Millon's Roll Book` (`15 Absences`).
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "In Chapter 13, laboratory equipment at the University of Santo Tomas is kept locked behind glass strictly for show. Father Millon marks Batangas student Placido Penitente with 15 absences for missing three classes and insults him until Placido walks out—showing how rote instruction and humiliation push students away from peaceful reform."

### Beat `07` — Chapters 14–20: Campaign for the Castilian Academy
* **On Screen:** `1. Señor Pasta (Ch. 15)` vs. `2. Don Custodio (Ch. 20)`.
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "When Isagani appeals to the prominent Filipino lawyer Señor Pasta, Pasta tells him to look after his own career—prompting Isagani's reply that *'every gray hair will be a thorn'* if he works only for himself. Referred to Don Custodio, the petition is approved only under friar direction, turning the students into tuition collectors."

### Beat `08` — Chapters 16–23: Smuggled Weapons, the Quiapo Fair, and María Clara's Death
* **On Screen:** 3-column sequence (`1. Quiroga's Warehouse` → `2. Mr. Leeds and Imuthis` → `3. The Aborted 8:00 PM Signal`).
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Simoun reduces Chinese merchant Quiroga's 9,000-peso debt to store smuggled rifles (Ch. 16), while at the Quiapo Fair Mr. Leeds' talking Egyptian head *Imuthis* causes Father Salví to faint by recounting a story mirroring Ibarra and María Clara (Ch. 18). Yet in Chapter 23, one hour before his 8:00 PM uprising, Simoun learns from Basilio that María Clara died at 6:00 PM inside the Santa Clara convent. Overcome with grief, Simoun abandons his waiting forces."

## Part II.C: Repression & Simoun's Lamp Centerpiece (`#act-3`, Ch. 25–34)

### Beat `09` — Chapters 25–29: The Subversive Posters and Captain Tiago's Will
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "After fourteen students mock Don Custodio's ruling at a noodle shop, anonymous seditious posters (*pasquinades*) appear on university doors. Authorities arrest the student leaders—including Basilio, who was not at the dinner. Wealthy students quickly regain their freedom, while Captain Tiago dies and Father Irene revokes Basilio's twenty-five-peso bequest, leaving him alone in jail."

### Beat `10` — Chapter 30: Juli and Father Camorra in Tiani
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Learning that Basilio faces deportation to the Carolines, Juli is urged to seek help from Tiani's parish priest, Father Camorra. Entering the convent in dread at dusk, Juli leaps from the window to her death that night rather than submit to Camorra's assault, driving her mute grandfather Tandang Selo into the mountains."

### Beat `11` — Chapters 33–35: Simoun's Conspiracy and the Pomegranate Lamp *(Interactive Centerpiece)*
* **Action:** Click through **`Stage 01` → `Stage 02` → `Stage 03` → `Stage 04`** beside the lamp illustration.
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Bailed out of prison two months later and told of Juli's death, Basilio joins Simoun's conspiracy:
  > - *(Stage 01 — The Disguise):* A wedding gift shaped like a gold-and-steel pomegranate for Juanito Pelaez and Paulita Gómez.
  > - *(Stage 02 — The Mechanism):* Holds two liters of nitroglycerin—which Simoun calls *'concentrated tears, repressed hatred, wrongs, and injustices'*—set to dim after 20 minutes and detonate via mercury fulminate when the wick is raised.
  > - *(Stage 03 — Intended Target):* Captain Tiago's former mansion on Calle Anloague, packed with gunpowder to wipe out the Captain-General and senior friars and signal a citywide attack.
  > - *(Stage 04 — Why It Fails):* Basilio warns his friend Isagani outside in the street, and Isagani rushes in to save Paulita."

## Part II.D: Climax & Coastal Ending (`#act-4`, Ch. 35–39)

### Beat `12` — Chapter 35: *Mane, Thecel, Phares* and Isagani's Choice
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Inside the banquet hall, Simoun's red-ink note—*Mane, Thecel, Phares · Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra*—terrifies Father Salví. As the lamp dims and Father Irene reaches for the wick, Isagani seizes the lamp and throws it into the Pasig River, creating a central irony: the students' boldest critic of colonial rule saves the regime out of love for Paulita."

### Beat `13` — Chapters 36–38: Censorship and the Death of Tandang Selo
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "While the Captain-General censors press reports of the wedding (Ch. 36), a young Filipino *Guardia Civil* conscript named Carolino—actually Cabesang Tales' son Tano—is forced at gunpoint during a mountain ambush to shoot an elderly rebel on the cliffs, realizing afterward that he has killed his own mute grandfather, Tandang Selo (Ch. 38)."

### Beat `14` — Chapter 39: Simoun's Confession and Father Florentino's Response
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Wounded while escaping Manila, Simoun drinks poison at Father Florentino's home above the Pacific Ocean and asks why God denied him aid. Florentino answers that liberty cannot be won by corrupting society further: *'Why independence, if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow?'* After Simoun dies quietly, Florentino casts the chest of jewels into the Pacific."

## Parts V & VI: Legacy & Concluding Interpretation (`#verdict`)

### Beat `15` — Comparing *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) and *El Filibusterismo* (1891)
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "This table summarizes the shift from *Noli* to *Fili*: from a 64-chapter diagnosis of friar abuses appealing to Madrid, to a 39-chapter critique of the entire colonial structure and Filipino complicity."

### Beat `16` — Historical Reception and the Unresolved Question of 1891
* **Spoken Cue:**
  > "Shipped to Hong Kong in 1891 and confiscated by Manila customs, the novel contributed to Rizal's exile to Dapitan in July 1892, inspired readers in Andrés Bonifacio's Katipunan, and was mandated in Philippine schools under Republic Act No. 1425 in 1956.
  >
  > Ultimately, Rizal leaves the conflict unresolved for a reason: the first thirty-eight chapters show that peaceful reform under colonial institutions had been blocked, while Simoun's failure warns that an uprising driven by personal hatred and moral unpreparedness risks producing new tyrants—placing the responsibility squarely on an educated, civic-minded generation."
