## Stage 2: Historical Context, Authorial Motivation & Printing Struggle (`#context`)

### [Visual & Scroll Cue]
*Scrolling advances across three analytical panels on the open book spread: (1) The Gomburza Dedication, (2) Rizal's Three Catalysts, and (3) The Writing & Ghent Printing Struggle.*

### 2A. On-Screen Website Text

#### Panel 2.1 — The Gomburza Dedication (1872)
- **Section Heading:** I. Dedicated to the Martyrs of 1872
- **On-Screen Epigraph:**
  > *"To the memory of the priests, Don Mariano Gómez (85 years old), Don José Burgos (30 years old), and Don Jacinto Zamora (35 years old), executed in Bagumbayan on the 28th of February, 1872..."* — José Rizal's Dedication
- **Analytical Fact Box (The Historical Corrections):**
  - **Actual Execution Date:** February 17, 1872 (following the Cavite Mutiny)—not February 28.
  - **Actual Ages:** Mariano Gómez (**72**, not 85), José Burgos (**35**, not 30), Jacinto Zamora (**36**, not 35).
  - **Why the Inaccuracy?** Rizal wrote the dedication from memory in Europe without access to colonial archives. Yet the thematic weight is foundational: Gomburza's martyrdom awakened Rizal's nationalism at age 11 and anchors the novel's tribute to the native Filipino secular clergy.

#### Panel 2.2 — Three Catalysts Behind the Sequel (1887–1891)
- **Section Heading:** Why Shift from Reform to Revolution?
- **Card 1: The Calamba Agrarian Dispute (1887–1891)**
  - Dominican friars drastically raised land rentals and arbitrarily evicted the Rizal family and tenant farmers from the *Hacienda de Calamba* under Governor-General Valeriano Weyler.
  - *Literary Impact:* Hardened Rizal's political outlook and directly inspired the tragedy of **Cabesang Tales**.
- **Card 2: Personal Heartbreak (1890)**
  - Rizal's longtime love, **Leonor Rivera**, married British railway engineer Henry Kipping after her mother intercepted their letters.
  - *Literary Impact:* Directly inspired the subplot of **Paulita Gómez** abandoning the idealistic poet **Isagani** for the wealthy, opportunistic **Juanito Pelaez**.
- **Card 3: Disillusionment with the Propaganda Movement**
  - Internal rivalries among Filipino expatriates in Madrid—especially between Rizal and **Marcelo H. del Pilar** over the editorial direction of *La Solidaridad*.
  - *Literary Impact:* Convinced Rizal that peaceful assimilationist lobbying in Spain was a dead end; the struggle had to return to Philippine soil.

#### Panel 2.3 — The Writing & Printing Struggle (1887–1891)
- **Section Heading:** An Itinerant Manuscript Saved from the Brink
- **Timeline Strip:**
  - `Oct 1887 (Calamba)` → Begun while practicing medicine amid friar harassment.
  - `1888–1890 (London, Paris, Madrid, Brussels)` → Revised and expanded as news from Calamba worsened.
  - `March 29, 1891 (Biarritz, France)` → Manuscript completed.
  - `Summer 1891 (Ghent, Belgium)` → Moved to Ghent for cheaper printing at **F. Meyer-Van Loo Press**. Rizal pawned his jewelry and skipped meals; printing halted in **August 1891** when funds ran out completely.
  - `September 18, 1891 (Paris / Ghent)` → **Valentin Ventura** sent emergency funds from Paris to finish printing. In gratitude, Rizal gifted Ventura the original handwritten manuscript.

### 2B. Spoken Presentation Script
> **[PRESENTER SCRIPT — STAGE 2]**
> *(Scroll to Panel 2.1 — Gomburza)*
> "Before the story even begins, Rizal dedicates *El Filibusterismo* to **Gomburza**—the three Filipino secular priests executed by garrote at Bagumbayan in 1872 after being framed for the Cavite Mutiny. An important historical detail for our class is that Rizal's famous dedication actually contains slight factual errors: he lists the date as February 28 instead of **February 17, 1872**, and misstates their ages—writing that Gómez was 85 instead of 72, Burgos 30 instead of 35, and Zamora 35 instead of 36. Historians note this is because Rizal was writing from memory in Europe without archival records. Still, dedicating a book to executed 'traitors' was itself an act of political defiance.
>
> *(Scroll to Panel 2.2 — Three Catalysts)*
> Why is *El Fili* so much darker and more political than *Noli Me Tángere*? Three real-life crises between 1887 and 1891 transformed Rizal's outlook:
> 1. First, the **Calamba Agrarian Dispute**. While Rizal was writing this book, Dominican friars hiked land rents in Calamba, and Governor-General Valeriano Weyler sent troops to evict Rizal's own family and burn their homes.
> 2. Second, **personal heartbreak**. In 1890, Rizal's fiancée of eleven years, Leonor Rivera, married a British engineer named Henry Kipping—a betrayal Rizal channeled directly into the character of Paulita Gómez abandonar Isagani for Juanito Pelaez.
> 3. Third, **disillusionment with the Propaganda Movement**. Bitter leadership splits in Madrid between Rizal and Marcelo H. del Pilar over *La Solidaridad* convinced Rizal that begging Spanish politicians for reforms in Madrid was futile.
>
> *(Scroll to Panel 2.3 — Printing Struggle)*
> Even publishing the book was a battle. Rizal started writing in Calamba in October 1887, worked across London, Paris, Madrid, and Brussels, and finished the manuscript in Biarritz on March 29, 1891. He moved to Ghent, Belgium, for cheaper printing at the F. Meyer-Van Loo Press, where he lived in extreme poverty, pawning his watch and jewels. By August 1891, printing stopped completely because he was broke. The book only survived because **Valentin Ventura** sent funds from Paris, allowing *El Filibusterismo* to come off the press on **September 18, 1891**."

## Stage 3: Act I — Return of the Subversive & The Colonial Microcosm (Chapters 1–10) (`#act-1`)

### [Visual & Scroll Cue]
*A page-turn transition opens Act I. `bapor-tabo.jpg` expands across the viewport with pinned scroll labels pointing to the hull, the upper deck, and the lower deck. Scrolling further reveals character portraits (`simoun.png`, `cabesang-tales.png`, `juli.png`, `basilio.png`) and analytical breakdown cards.*

### 3A. On-Screen Website Text

#### Scene 3.1 — Allegory of the *Bapor Tabo* (Chapters 1–2)
- **Heading:** Act I (Ch. 1–10) · The Colonial Microcosm
- **Visual Asset:** `bapor-tabo.jpg`
- **Pinned Annotation 1 — The Vessel Itself:**
  - **The Ship of State:** A slow, heavy, circular steamship struggling up the winding Pasig River. Freshly whitewashed on the outside to appear modern, yet filthy and archaic underneath—a direct allegory for the stagnant, hypocritical Spanish colonial government.
- **Pinned Annotation 2 — The Upper Deck (Privilege & *Frailocracia*):**
  - Shaded under awnings sit European officials, Spanish friars (Salví, Camorra, Sibyla, Irene), Ben Zayb, and the Hispanized Doña Victorina—debating colonial policy in comfort while detached from the masses below.
- **Pinned Annotation 3 — The Lower Deck (The Colonized Majority):**
  - Crushed between baggage and the boiling engine room sit the *indios* (native Filipinos), Chinese merchants, and university students (Basilio and Isagani)—bearing the heat and weight of the ship.

#### Scene 3.2 — The Subversive, The Peasant, and The Student (Chapters 3–10)
- **Card A: The Return of Simoun (`simoun.png`)**
  - **Identity:** Crisóstomo Ibarra returns after 13 years disguised as a wealthy, bearded jeweler wearing blue-tinted spectacles.
  - **Strategy (Accelerationism):** Known as the "Brown Cardinal," Simoun whispers into the ear of the corrupt Captain-General, deliberately encouraging greed, cruelty, and higher taxes to push the abused populace into violent revolution.
- **Card B: Cabesang Tales & The Lockean Tragedy (`cabesang-tales.png` & `juli.png`)**
  - **Agrarian Dispossession (Ch. 4 & 10):** Tales clears virgin forest through his family's blood and sweat, only for Dominican friars to claim ownership and impose extortionate rent.
  - **The Breaking Point:** When bandits kidnap Tales, his daughter **Juli** enters indentured servitude to pay his ransom. Pushed to ruin, Tales steals Simoun's revolver, slaughters the friar administrator and the new land usurpers, and flees to the mountains as the feared outlaw ***Matanglawin***.
- **Card C: Confrontation in the Ibarra Forest (Ch. 6–7) (`basilio.png` & `simoun.png`)**
  - **The Discovery:** On Christmas Eve at his mother Sisa's grave, medical student **Basilio** discovers Simoun digging up Ibarra's treasure.
  - **The Dialectical Debate (Assimilation vs. Revolution):**
    - *Basilio's Position:* Advocates peaceful reform, clinical science, and nationwide Spanish language education to unite Filipinos with Spain.
    - *Simoun's Critique:* Condemns Hispanization as cultural suicide—arguing that adding another language without political rights only destroys native identity and turns the nation into *"a republic of the rapacious and the discontented."*

### 3B. Spoken Presentation Script
> **[PRESENTER SCRIPT — STAGE 3]**
> *(Scroll to Bapor Tabo scene)*
> "In Act I, covering Chapters 1 to 10, Rizal does not ease us into the plot—he immediately gives us a political diagram of the Philippines through the **Bapor Tabo**.
>
> Look at how Rizal constructs this symbol: the steamship is almost perfectly circular, so you can't tell its front from its back, and it moves agonizingly slowly up the Pasig River while blowing its whistle constantly. Its hull is freshly whitewashed to pretend it is progressive and clean, covering up the rust and dirt underneath. That is the Spanish colonial state.
>
> Even more important is its vertical hierarchy:
> - On the **Upper Deck**, shaded and comfortable, sit the Spanish friars, colonial bureaucrats, and elite pretenders like Doña Victorina.
> - Down on the **Lower Deck**, packed next to the suffocating heat of the engine room, are the *indios*, Chinese traders, and Filipino students. In two chapters, Rizal maps out the entire racial and economic stratification of the colony.
>
> *(Scroll to Simoun, Tales, and Basilio cards)*
> Moving among both decks is **Simoun**—Crisóstomo Ibarra returning thirteen years after *Noli Me Tángere*. Notice how radically his character has changed. Ibarra was a trusting idealist; Simoun is a calculating conspirator who uses his jewels to bribe the Captain-General and the friars. His political strategy is what political scientists today call **accelerationism**: he intentionally pushes the government to commit worse abuses so the Filipino people will finally snap and revolt.
>
> We see why people snap in the subplot of **Cabesang Tales** in Chapters 4 and 10. Tales represents Rizal's own tenants in Calamba and embodies John Locke's labor theory of property: land belongs to the farmer who clears it and buries his wife and child in its soil. Yet the friar corporation claims the land, hikes the rent every year, and uses colonial courts to disarm and rob him. When his daughter **Juli** is forced into indentured servitude to ransom him from bandits, Tales steals Simoun's revolver, executes the friar administrator and the new tenants, and becomes the guerrilla outlaw **Matanglawin**. Rizal is demonstrating sociologically that institutional land-grabbing leaves peaceful citizens no legal recourse but armed insurgency.
>
> Finally, in Chapter 7, Basilio discovers Simoun's true identity in the forest on Christmas Eve. Rather than a simple action scene, Rizal writes a profound political debate. Basilio represents the assimilationist youth who believe learning Spanish and practicing science will modernize the Philippines. Simoun dismantles this illusion, warning Basilio that begging to become a Spanish province will never bring freedom—and that learning Spanish while remaining slaves will only erase their own native identity."

## Stage 4: Act II — Reformist Idealism vs. Colonial Decay (Chapters 11–24) (`#act-2`)

### [Visual & Scroll Cue]
*A page-turn transition leads into Act II. Four analytical cards unfold sequentially on scroll, accompanied by portraits of `isagani.png`, `maria-clara.png`, and `simoun.png`.*

### 4A. On-Screen Website Text

#### Card 4.1 — Arbitrary Rule & Academic Oppression (Chapters 11–13)
- **Los Baños Governance (Ch. 11):** While playing cards (`tresillo`) at Los Baños, the Captain-General and friars casually trade favors over state policy—banning firearms while manipulation and bribery dictate law.
- **The Class in Physics (Ch. 12–13):**
  - At the University of Santo Tomas, **Father Millon** humiliates Batangas student **Placido Penitente**.
  - **Key Symbol — Instruments Behind Glass:** Expensive physics laboratory equipment is locked in glass cabinets strictly to impress foreign visitors, never to be touched by Filipino students.
  - **Analytical Takeaway:** Colonial education relies on medieval scholasticism, rote memorization, and verbal abuse to crush critical thinking and keep Filipinos compliant, pushing Placido into Simoun's conspiracy.

#### Card 4.2 — The Campaign for the Castilian Academy (Chapters 14–15, 20–21)
- **Portrait Featured:** `isagani.png` (The Idealistic Poet-Patriot)
- **The Student Petition:** Led by **Isagani**, **Macaraig**, and **Sandoval**, university students petition to open an academy to teach the Spanish language.
- **Two Pillars of Bureaucratic Failure:**
  - **Señor Pasta (The Complicit Technocrat):** A brilliant Filipino lawyer who refuses to help the students out of fear of offending the friars—representing educated elites who abandon national solidarity for personal comfort.
  - **Don Custodio / *"Buena Tinta"* (Colonial Incompetence):** A pompous Spanish official who approves the academy only on the condition that **religious orders strictly supervise it**, reducing the students to mere fee-collectors stripped of academic freedom.

#### Card 4.3 — Subterranean Subversion & Psychological Mirroring (Chapters 16–18)
- **Quiroga's Consulate & Gun-Running (Ch. 16):** Simoun exploits the financial debts of Chinese merchant **Quiroga** (who desires a Chinese consulate) to smuggle rifles into Manila hidden inside crates of fireworks and merchandise.
- **The Talking Head *Imuthis* (Ch. 17–18):** At the Quiapo Fair, American illusionist **Mr. Leeds** exhibits an ancient Egyptian sphinx head named **Imuthis**. Imuthis recounts how a lustful young Egyptian priest forged letters to frame him and assault a pure maiden in a temple—an exact psychological mirror of **Father Salví's** betrayal of Ibarra and María Clara, causing Salví to faint in terror.

#### Card 4.4 — The Death of María Clara & The Aborted First Uprising (Chapters 22–23)
- **Portraits Featured:** `maria-clara.png` & `simoun.png`
- **Double Disillusionment:**
  - At the Teatro de Variedades performance of *Les Cloches de Corneville*, the students realize Don Custodio's ruling is a humiliating sham.
  - Across town, Basilio informs Simoun that **María Clara has just died** inside the Santa Clara convent.
- **Analytical Significance:** Simoun's entire revolutionary timeline was driven by a personal desire to rescue María Clara. Her death sends him into an emotional collapse, aborting his first coordinated uprising that very night and exposing the flaw of a revolution built on personal obsession.

### 4B. Spoken Presentation Script
> **[PRESENTER SCRIPT — STAGE 4]**
> *(Scroll to Act II — Card 4.1)*
> "In Act II, covering Chapters 11 through 24, Rizal systematically tests whether peaceful reform is actually possible inside colonial institutions.
>
> First, he examines the government and the university. At Los Baños in Chapter 11, the Captain-General decides the fate of teachers, prisoners, and student petitions while playing cards with the friars—showing that colonial law is completely arbitrary. Then, in Chapter 13, **'The Class in Physics,'** we see how education is used as an instrument of control. At the University of Santo Tomas, laboratory instruments are locked behind glass cabinets just to show off to guests, while Father Millon forces students to memorize texts word-for-word and insults **Placido Penitente** until Placido walks out in disgust and eventually joins Simoun's plot.
>
> *(Scroll to Card 4.2 — Castilian Academy)*
> Meanwhile, idealistic students led by **Isagani** and **Macaraig** try to work within the system by petitioning for a Castilian Academy to teach Spanish. Notice who blocks them: first is **Señor Pasta**, a wealthy Filipino lawyer who tells Isagani to just focus on making money and stay out of politics—an archetype of the educated Filipino technocrat who prioritizes his own comfort over his country. Second is **Don Custodio**, the supposed liberal arbitrator, whose ridiculous 'compromise' hands control of the academy right back to the friars and turns the students into mere tax collectors.
>
> *(Scroll to Card 4.3 & 4.4 — Quiroga, Imuthis, and María Clara)*
> While reform fails in the daylight, Simoun prepares for war in the shadows. In Chapter 16, he uses the debts of the Chinese businessman **Quiroga** to smuggle weapons hidden inside fireworks crates. In Chapter 18, at the Quiapo fair, Mr. Leeds' talking sphinx head **Imuthis** tells an ancient Egyptian story of priestly lust and forged letters that mirrors Father Salví's crime against Ibarra and María Clara so closely that Salví faints on the spot.
>
> Act II reaches its turning point in Chapter 23. On the very night Simoun's armed forces are waiting for his signal to attack Manila, Basilio tells him that **María Clara has died** in the Santa Clara convent. Simoun breaks down completely and fails to give the signal. This is a crucial analytical point: Simoun's first revolution collapses because his motive isn't pure national liberation—it is still tied to his private obsession with saving María Clara."

## Stage 5: Act III — Betrayal, Repression & Radicalization (Chapters 25–34) (`#act-3`)

### [Visual & Scroll Cue]
*A dark page-turn transition shifts the background tone deeper. Portraits of `isagani.png`, `juli.png`, `basilio.png`, and `simoun.png` frame the narrative progression, culminating in a pinned dramatic reveal of `crystal-lamp.jpg`.*

### 5A. On-Screen Website Text

#### Panel 5.1 — Satire, Pretext & The Educational Debate (Chapters 25–27)
- **The *Pansiteria* Banquet (Ch. 25):** Mocking the sham approval of their academy, fourteen students hold a satirical banquet at a Chinese noodle shop (*Pansiteria Macanista de Buen Gusto*), dedicating each dish to the friars and Don Custodio's incompetence.
- **The *Pasquinades* / Subversive Posters (Ch. 26):** The next morning, seditious posters appear on university doors. Colonial authorities seize the pretext to arrest all student leaders—including **Basilio**, who was not even at the banquet.
- **The Friar and the Filipino (Ch. 27) (`isagani.png`):** In a landmark philosophical dialogue with the progressive Jesuit **Father Fernández**, Isagani argues that the friars act like corrupt prison contractors who deliberately starve prisoners' minds so they can claim Filipinos are unfit for liberty.

#### Panel 5.2 — The Death of Juli & The Death of Innocence (Chapters 29–30)
- **Portrait Featured:** `juli.png`
- **Institutional Betrayal:** Terrified by Father Irene's tales of execution, **Captain Tiago** dies; Irene alters his will to strip imprisoned Basilio of his inheritance.
- **Juli's Martyrdom (Ch. 30):** With everyone else released through family bribes, only poor Basilio remains in jail. Desperate to save him, **Juli** reluctantly approaches the lustful **Father Camorra** at the Tiani convent. To escape Camorra's sexual assault, she leaps from the convent window to her death.

#### Panel 5.3 — Basilio's Radicalization & The Nitroglycerin Lamp (Chapters 31, 33–34)
- **Visual Asset:** `crystal-lamp.jpg` alongside `basilio.png` & `simoun.png`
- **From Healer to Bomb-Maker:** Released after two months in prison through Simoun's intervention—and learning of Juli's suicide and Tandang Selo's disappearance—a hardened Basilio abandons peaceful science and demands to join Simoun's terror plot.
- **Anatomy of the Weapon (`La Lámpara`):**
  - **Disguise:** An ornate crystal lamp shaped like a gold-and-jewel pomegranate.
  - **Chemical Payload:** Filled with **nitroglycerin**—which Simoun chillingly calls *"the concentrated tears of the oppressed."*
  - **Target:** The wedding reception of **Paulita Gómez and Juanito Pelaez** at Captain Tiago's former mansion, where the Captain-General, senior friars, and colonial elite will all be gathered in one room.

### 5B. Spoken Presentation Script
> **[PRESENTER SCRIPT — STAGE 5]**
> *(Scroll to Act III — Panel 5.1)*
> "In Act III, Chapters 25 to 34, the novel turns from political satire to state repression and radicalization.
>
> After their Spanish Academy is gutted, the students vent their frustration at a Chinese noodle shop in Chapter 25, satirically dedicating dishes like *lumpia* and crab to the friars and Don Custodio. The very next morning, mysterious seditious posters—called **pasquinades**—are found on the university doors. Just like the 1872 Cavite Mutiny, the regime uses this incident as a convenient pretext to sweep up every student leader, including **Basilio**, who wasn't even involved.
>
> Before the crackdown completes, Rizal gives us Chapter 27, **'The Friar and the Filipino,'** a remarkable one-on-one debate between **Isagani** and the sympathetic Jesuit **Father Fernández**. Here, Isagani delivers Rizal's thesis on colonial education: he compares the friars to prison wardens who auction off prisoners' food, starve them, and then blame the prisoners for being too weak to walk.
>
> *(Scroll to Panel 5.2 — Juli's Martyrdom)*
> Now look at how the system treats the rich versus the poor. Wealthy students like Macaraig buy their way out of prison immediately. Basilio has no one—because **Father Irene** manipulated the dying Captain Tiago's will to steal Basilio's inheritance. Desperate to free Basilio, Cabesang Tales' daughter **Juli** goes to the one man she dreads most: the predatory **Father Camorra**. When Camorra attempts to sexually assault her inside the convent, Juli chooses to leap out the window to her death rather than surrender her honor.
>
> *(Scroll to Panel 5.3 — The Crystal Lamp)*
> Juli's death destroys the last shred of reformist faith in **Basilio**. Throughout the first thirty chapters, Basilio is the model assimilated Filipino: he keeps his head down, endures humiliation, and studies medicine to heal people. When Simoun finally bails him out of prison and Basilio learns what happened to Juli, he walks straight into Simoun's laboratory and asks for a weapon.
>
> This brings us to the central visual symbol of Act III: **Simoun's Crystal Lamp**. Shaped like a pomegranate and filled with liquid nitroglycerin, Simoun tells Basilio that this fuel is more than dynamite—it is *'the concentrated tears of the oppressed, crystallized into hatred.'* Their plan is total decapitation of the state: place the lamp at the center of the dining table during the wedding feast of Paulita Gómez and Juanito Pelaez at Captain Tiago's old house. When the light dims and someone raises the wick, the entire colonial ruling class—along with the house—will be blown to pieces, signaling Cabesang Tales and armed rebels outside to storm Manila."

## Stage 6: Act IV — The Abortive Revolution & Theological Resolution (Chapters 35–39) (`#act-4`)

### [Visual & Scroll Cue]
*`crystal-lamp.jpg` pins at the center of the screen with glowing red typography for `Mane, Thecel, Phares`. As you scroll, `isagani.png` appears, the lamp's warm glow plunges into dark river tones, and the scene transitions to `final-ocean.jpg` with `padre-florentino.png` and `simoun.png`.*

### 6A. On-Screen Website Text

#### Scene 6.1 — The Wedding Feast & *Mane, Thecel, Phares* (Chapter 35)
- **Visual Asset:** `crystal-lamp.jpg` (Pinned Climax)
- **The Gathering of the Elite:** Inside Captain Tiago's heavily redecorated mansion, the Captain-General, senior friars, and opportunists toast the marriage of Paulita Gómez and Juanito Pelaez.
- **The Biblical Death Warrant:** Simoun presents the glowing pomegranate lamp as his wedding gift and slips away, leaving behind a parchment note written in blood-red ink:
  > ### `MANE · THECEL · PHARES`
  > **Juan Crisóstomo Ibarra**
  - *Biblical Meaning (Book of Daniel):* **"Measured, Weighed, Found Wanting"**—the writing on the wall foretelling the immediate fall of King Belshazzar's corrupt empire.
  - *Psychological Reaction:* Recognizing the handwriting of a man thought dead for 13 years, Father Salví turns pale with terror.

#### Scene 6.2 — Isagani's Intervention: Romantic Impulse vs. Political Calculation (Chapter 35)
- **Portraits Featured:** `isagani.png` & `basilio.png`
- **The Turning Point:** Outside in the street, Basilio prepares to flee but spots his friend **Isagani** gazing heartbreakingly at his lost love, Paulita. Basilio warns him to run because the house is rigged to explode.
- **The Foiled Coup:** Calculating nothing of politics or colonial tyranny—driven purely by chivalrous love to save Paulita's life—Isagani sprints into the dining hall just as Father Irene reaches to raise the dimming wick, seizes the nitroglycerin lamp, and hurls it into the **Pasig River**.

#### Scene 6.3 — State Terror & The Carolino Tragedy (Chapters 36–38)
- **Censorship of the Press (Ch. 36):** Sensationalist journalist **Ben Zayb** attempts to write an epic heroic article about the friars at the wedding, only for the Captain-General to censor all mention of the attack to hide the regime's vulnerability.
- **The Tragedy of Carolino / Tano (Ch. 38):**
  - In the countryside, Filipino *Guardia Civil* soldiers brutally march and torture innocent prisoners.
  - One young soldier—**Carolino** (actually **Tano**, Cabesang Tales' conscripted son)—protests his corporal's cruelty.
  - Ambushed from a cliff by Matanglawin's outlaws, Carolino is forced at gunpoint by his corporal to bayonet-shoot an elderly rebel standing on the rocks—realizing too late in horror that he has just killed his own mute grandfather, **Tandang Selo**.

#### Scene 6.4 — Simoun's Confession & Father Florentino's Verdict (Chapter 39)
- **Visual Asset:** `final-ocean.jpg` with `padre-florentino.png` & `simoun.png`
- **The Fugitive's Question:** Wounded by pursuing soldiers and having swallowed slow-acting poison to avoid capture alive, Simoun takes refuge at the Pacific coastal home of the dignified native secular priest **Father Florentino**. Confessing his true identity as Ibarra, Simoun asks bitterly: *"Why did God deny me His aid and allow the wicked to triumph?"*
- **Father Florentino's Verdict (Rizal's Ethical Thesis):**
  1. **Means Corrupt the Ends:** *"Mr. Simoun, He has frustrated your plans one by one... because you chose a method of which He could not approve. Hate creates nothing but monsters; crime, criminals; only love creates wonderful things."*
  2. **The Danger of Premature Independence:** *"What is the use of independence if the slaves of today will be the tyrants of tomorrow? And no doubt they will be, because whoever submits to tyranny loves it!"*
  3. **Moral Readiness & Civic Virtue:** True liberty must be earned by elevating the intelligence, dignity, and civic virtue of the people to the point of dying for what is just.
- **The Jewel Chest Cast Into the Sea:** After Simoun dies in peace, Father Florentino takes Simoun's iron chest of diamonds and rubies to the cliff edge and hurls it into the abyss of the Pacific Ocean—praying that nature guard it until humanity needs it for a holy, sublime purpose.

#### Sub-Section 7.2 — Structural Comparison: *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) vs. *El Filibusterismo* (1891)

| Analytical Dimension | *Noli Me Tángere* (Berlin, 1887) | *El Filibusterismo* (Ghent, 1891) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Tone & Genre** | **Romantic & Diagnostic ("A Social Cancer" / Work of the Heart):** Satirical, emotional, idealistic, and descriptive. | **Political & Dialectical ("The Reign of Greed" / Work of the Head):** Dark, cynical, brooding, and philosophical. |
| **Protagonist's Evolution** | **Crisóstomo Ibarra:** Trusting, idealistic reformist who believes in building schools and cooperating with Spain. | **Simoun:** Disillusioned, calculating conspirator who uses bribery, extortion, and terror to implode the regime. |
| **Target of Critique** | Primarily exposes the abuses of the **Spanish friars (*frailocracia*)** while holding out hope that Madrid will intervene if informed. | Condemns the **entire colonial apparatus**—both Madrid officials (Captain-General) and friars—alongside the **complacency of Filipinos**. |
| **Length & Narrative Pacing** | **64 Chapters** (plus deleted *"Elias and Salome"*); expansive, rich in folkloric scenes and domestic life. | **39 Chapters;** tighter, claustrophobic, heavily driven by ideological debates and political tension. |

#### Sub-Section 7.3 — Historical Impact, Reception & Legacy (1891–Present)
- **1. Immediate Colonial Censorship & Smuggling (1891–1892):**
  - Most of the Ghent first edition was shipped to **Hong Kong**, where Spanish customs and consular agents intercepted and burned smuggled crates in Manila.
  - Surviving copies circulated clandestinely among *ilustrados* and urban workers. Possession was treated as treason by the *Comisión Permanente de Censura* and *Guardia Civil*—directly fueling the charges that led to Rizal's exile to **Dapitan (July 1892)** and execution at Bagumbayan on **December 30, 1896**.
- **2. The Katipunan Paradox & The 1896 Revolution:**
  - **Authorial Intent vs. Revolutionary Reading:** While Rizal wrote the failure of Simoun's plot as a cautionary warning against premature, vengeance-driven revolt, **Andrés Bonifacio** and **Emilio Jacinto** drew revolutionary fire from the novel's proof that peaceful reform under Spain was dead (mirroring the collapse of *La Solidaridad* and *La Liga Filipina*) and from the righteous resistance of **Cabesang Tales**.
- **3. Institutionalization in the Modern Republic:**
  - **Preservation of the Manuscript (1925–1961):** Purchased by the Philippine government from Valentin Ventura in **1925 for ₱10,000**, survived the devastation of the Battle of Manila in 1945, was briefly stolen and recovered in 1961, and rests today in the National Library as a National Cultural Treasure.
  - **Republic Act No. 1425 (The Rizal Law of 1956):** Authored by **Senator Claro M. Recto** and sponsored by **Senator Jose P. Laurel**, mandating the study of unexpurgated editions of *Noli* and *Fili* across all Philippine schools and universities despite intense opposition from the 1950s Catholic hierarchy.

#### Sub-Section 7.4 — Interactive Class Reflection Prompts ("The Final Verdict")
*(Clickable discussion cards for your concluding Q&A with the class)*

1. **Question 1: Reform vs. Revolution**
   - *Prompt:* Did Rizal write *El Filibusterismo* to condemn revolution altogether, or to define the moral conditions required for a revolution to succeed?
   - *Reveal Answer:* Rizal exposes peaceful assimilation (the Castilian Academy) as a dead end, yet warns through Father Florentino that an uprising led by personal hatred and corruption (Simoun) will only birth new tyrants.
2. **Question 2: The Responsibility of the Educated Youth**
   - *Prompt:* Between Señor Pasta (self-preservation), Basilio (quiet compliance turned vengeance), and Isagani (principled idealism), what does Rizal demand from the Filipino student?
   - *Reveal Answer:* Through Father Florentino's closing prayer—*"Where are the youth who will consecrate their golden hours, their illusions, and their enthusiasm to the welfare of their native land?"*—Rizal calls for an educated generation that refuses to become another Señor Pasta.
